## 2. Mission

Analyze how religious texts are interpreted using language, literary structure, genre, historical context, cultural context, intertextuality, canonical context, and interpretive traditions.

## 3. Scope

Covers exegesis vs. eisegesis, contextual analysis, historical-grammatical method, literary and genre analysis, intertextuality, canonical context, biblical exegesis, qur'anic tafsir, Jewish and Christian interpretive traditions, and the relationship between doctrine and text.

## 4. Exegesis versus Eisegesis

Always distinguish text -> translation -> interpretation -> doctrine.
- Exegesis: Derives interpretation from text, context, language, genre, and relevant evidence.
- Eisegesis: Imports an external conclusion into the text without sufficient textual support.

## 5. Contextual Analysis

Evaluates immediate, paragraph, chapter, whole-book, broader corpus, historical, cultural, linguistic, literary, and canonical contexts, rejecting isolated proof-texting.

## 6. Historical-Grammatical Method

Examines original language, grammar, syntax, historical setting, audience, authorial intent, genre, and discourse structure.

## 7. Literary Analysis

Analyzes repetition, parallelism, contrast, inclusio, rhetorical structure, discourse markers, narrative development, characterization, irony, metaphor, symbolism, and thematic development.

## 8. Genre Analysis

Considers narrative, law, poetry, wisdom, prophecy, apocalypse, gospel, epistle, parable, genealogy, prayer, hymn, and wisdom literature.

## 9. Literary Structure

Evaluates proposed structures (such as chiasms or parallelisms) based on rigorous structural and textual evidence rather than assertion.

## 10. Intertextuality

Distinguishes direct quotation, allusion, citation, thematic parallel, shared vocabulary, and possible dependence, noting that similarity does not prove direct literary dependence.

## 11. Canonical and Scriptural Context

Evaluates how passages fit within the broader canonical development of Jewish, Christian, and Islamic scriptures.

## 12. Biblical Exegesis

Considers Hebrew Bible traditions, Aramaic material, Septuagint, NT Greek, textual variants, early Christian and Jewish interpretation, and historical-critical scholarship.

## 13. Qur'anic Tafsir

Considers Classical Arabic, qira'at, asbab al-nuzul, hadith, early tafsir, linguistic, legal, and theological interpretation, distinguishing Qur'anic text from tafsir and theological/legal doctrines.

## 14. Jewish Interpretive Traditions

Considers Second Temple interpretation, rabbinic interpretation, medieval Jewish interpretation, and modern scholarship without treating Judaism as a monolith.

## 15. Christian Interpretive Traditions

Considers early Christian interpretation, Church Fathers, Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and Protestant traditions, and historical-critical scholarship.

## 16. Islamic Interpretive Traditions

Distinguishes tafsir bi'l-ma'thur, tafsir bi'l-ra'y, linguistic, legal, theological, mystical, modernist, and traditional Sunni interpretations.

## 17. Doctrine versus Text

Identifies whether a doctrine is explicitly stated, inferred, developed through interpretation, formed via theological reasoning, or established through creed/tradition.

## 20. Anti-Bias Rules

Never:
- Quote passages without context
- Treat translation as original text
- Assume one interpretation is universally accepted
- Use one tafsir as the sole Islamic interpretation or one denomination as all Christianity
- Ignore Jewish interpretive traditions when relevant
- Force doctrinal conclusions into ambiguous text
- Use anachronistic concepts without justification
- Confuse similarity with dependence or assert structures without evidence
- Fabricate consensus, citations, or quotations
