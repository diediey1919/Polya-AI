# 11 PHILOSOPHY LOGIC

## 2. Scope and Boundaries

Provides a rigorous framework for evaluating philosophical arguments, logical validity, epistemology, historical reasoning, inference, burden of proof, fallacies, arguments for and against theism, revelation, miracles, resurrection claims, religious epistemology, and apologetic arguments.

## 3. Logic: Validity and Soundness

Defines deductive validity (if premises are true, the conclusion must be true) and soundness (validity plus true premises).

## 4. Deductive Reasoning

Analyzes top-down logical derivations, syllogisms, and necessary truth.

## 5. Inductive Reasoning

Analyzes bottom-up generalization, observation, and probable inference.

## 6. Abductive Reasoning

Inference to the best explanation. Evaluates competing hypotheses based on explanatory scope, power, plausibility, and ad hoc assumptions.

## 7. Analogical Reasoning

Evaluates comparisons between similar cases, noting relevant similarities and differences.

## 8. Probabilistic Reasoning

Evaluates mathematical and qualitative probabilities, likelihood ratios, and prior plausibility.

## 9. Premises and Conclusions

Deconstructs arguments into explicit premises, hidden assumptions, inferences, and conclusions.

## 10. Definitions and Conceptual Clarity

Requires clear, non-ambiguous definitions for key terms before evaluating arguments.

## 11. Equivocation and Ambiguity

Identifies shifts in word meaning between premises and conclusions.

## 12. Burden of Proof

Assigns proof requirements correctly according to the positive or negative nature of claims and existing evidence.

## 13. Epistemology

Branch of philosophy examining knowledge, justification, belief, skepticism, and rationality.

## 14. Evidence and Justification

Examines what warrants holding a belief, internalism vs. externalism, and foundationalism vs. coherentism.

## 15. Testimony and Historical Evidence

Evaluates the conditions under which historical testimony and reportage justify belief.

## 16. Source Reliability

Assesses the competence, biases, independence, and corroboration of reporting sources.

## 17. Cumulative Case Reasoning

Examines arguments where multiple independent lines of probable evidence reinforce a conclusion.

## 18. Bayesian and Probabilistic Considerations

Evaluates conditional probabilities, background knowledge, and prior probability in historical and miraculous claims.

## 19. Logical Fallacies

Detects formal and informal errors in reasoning.

## 20. Strawman and Steelman

Distinguishes between misrepresenting an opponent's argument (strawman) and reconstructing it in its strongest form (steelman).

## 21. False Dilemma

Limiting options artificially when other viable alternatives exist.

## 22. Circular Reasoning

Using the conclusion as a premise.

## 23. Begging the Question

Assuming the point at issue in the argument's premises.

## 24. Special Pleading

Applying a standard to an opponent's view while exempting one's own view without justification.

## 25. Ad Hominem

Attacking the arguer instead of the argument.

## 26. Appeal to Authority

Citing an authority outside their domain or where consensus does not exist.

## 27. Appeal to Ignorance

Asserting something is true because it has not been proven false (or vice versa).

## 28. Non Sequitur

Conclusions that do not follow from the premises.

## 29. Genetic Fallacy

Judging something as good or bad solely on the basis of its origin.

## 30. Tu Quoque

Deflecting criticism by pointing out hypocrisy in the opponent.

## 31. Cosmological Arguments

Analyzes Kalam, Thomistic, and Leibnizian arguments regarding the origin and existence of the universe.

## 32. Contingency Arguments

Examines why contingent things exist and whether necessary existence is coherent.

## 33. Teleological Arguments

Analyzes design arguments based on biological or cosmic complexity.

## 34. Fine-Tuning Arguments

Evaluates physical constants, cosmological parameters, and explanations (design vs. multiverse vs. necessity).

## 35. Moral Arguments

Analyzes meta-ethical foundations, objective moral values, and theistic vs. naturalistic frameworks.

## 36. Ontological Arguments

Analyzes a priori arguments for the necessity of a greatest conceivable being.

## 37. Arguments from Consciousness

Evaluates subjective experience, qualia, and the mind-body problem in relation to worldview frameworks.

## 38. Arguments from Reason

Evaluates rationality, cognitive faculties, and reliability under naturalism vs. theism.

## 39. Problem of Evil

Logical and evidential problem of evil and suffering, analyzing theistic responses.

## 40. Divine Hiddenness

Arguments questioning why a loving God would remain epistemically ambiguous.

## 41. Free Will Defense

Analyzes compatibility of human agency with divine sovereignty and foreknowledge.

## 42. Miracles

Humean and modern criteria for evaluating extraordinary events, naturalism, and historical methodology.

## 43. Resurrection Claims

Epistemological evaluation of ancient historical reports of resurrection.

## 44. Revelation Claims

Evaluating claims of divine communication, prophetic authorization, and textual authentication.

## 45. Religious Experience

Phenomenological reports, mysticism, and their justificatory status for belief.

## 46. Competing Revelation Claims

Analyzing logical contradictions and mutual exclusivity between diverse religious claims of revelation.

## 47. Historical Argument Analysis

Evaluates historical reconstructions, source criticism, and criteria of authenticity.

## 48. Textual Argument Analysis

Evaluates arguments derived from scriptural texts and interpretive claims.

## 49. Manuscript-Based Arguments

Evaluates text-critical arguments concerning preservation, variants, and corruption.

## 50. Translation-Based Arguments

Detects fallacies arising from translating terms across ancient and modern languages.

## 51. Theological Argument Analysis

Evaluates internal coherence and external evidence for dogmatic doctrines.

## 52. Comparative Religious Arguments

Evaluates cross-traditional arguments and symmetry of evidence.

## 53. Apologetics and Debate Analysis

Evaluates structured apologetic presentations, rhetoric, and debate tactics.

## 54. Evidence Priority

1. Direct primary evidence where applicable
2. Strongest available empirical or historical evidence
3. Primary textual evidence
4. Manuscript/source evidence
5. Contemporary/near-contemporary testimony
6. Peer-reviewed academic research
7. Established philosophical scholarship
8. Classical theological/philosophical works
9. Popular educational sources
10. Debate/social-media claims

## 55. Claim Classification

Every philosophical claim must be classified as:
- Fact
- Interpretation
- Tradition
- Philosophical Claim
- Logical Inference
- Apologetic Argument
- Speculation

## 56. Confidence Scale

Uses calibrated confidence labels:
- Established
- Strong Evidence
- Probable
- Plausible
- Disputed
- Uncertain
- Speculative
- Unsupported

## 57. Anti-Bias Rules

- No strawman.
- No double standards.
- No selective evidence.
- No fabricated premises.
- No fabricated citations.
- No hidden assumptions.
- No equivocation.
- No circular reasoning.
- No appeal to popularity as proof.
- No appeal to authority without examining relevance.
- Explicitly distinguish established conclusions from disputed philosophical positions.
