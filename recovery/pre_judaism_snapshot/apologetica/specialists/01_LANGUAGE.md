## 2. Scope

Analyzes original-language evidence and linguistic claims relevant to Biblical Hebrew, Aramaic, Syriac, Koine Greek, Classical and Qur'anic Arabic, Semitic linguistics, morphology, syntax, semantics, lexicography, transliteration, etymology, and translation-related claims.

## 3. Biblical Hebrew

Covers phonology, morphology (stems/binyanim, noun patterns), syntax, clause structure, verbal system (perfect/imperfect, waw-consecutive, wayyiqtol), and classical Hebrew lexicography.

## 4. Aramaic and Syriac

Covers Imperial/Biblical Aramaic, Targumic Aramaic, and Classical Syriac (Estrangela, Serto, Nestorian scripts), grammar, verb stems, and vocabulary relevant to ancient Near Eastern texts and textual witnesses.

## 5. Koine Greek

Covers Hellenistic Greek (Koine) grammar, case system, verbal aspect (present, aorist, perfect), moods, voice, syntax, discourse analysis, and lexical semantics in early Christian and Hellenistic Jewish literature.

## 6. Classical and Qur'anic Arabic

Covers Classical Arabic grammar (nahw), morphology (sarf), root-and-pattern system (roots, binyanim/awwazin), triliteral/quadriliteral roots, syntactic particles, case endings (i'rab), orthography (scriptio defectiva/plena), and Qur'anic linguistic features.

## 7. Semitic Linguistics

Covers comparative Semitic philology, phonological correspondences, root evolution, morphology across Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic, Ethiopic, and Ugaritic, and semantic reconstruction.

## 8. Lexeme, Root, Stem and Morphology

Distinguishes between roots (abstract consonantal roots), lexemes (dictionary headwords), stems (derived verb/noun patterns), and surface morphological forms in inflected languages.

## 9. Syntax and Grammar

Analyzes clause structure, word order, subject-verb-object dynamics, subordination, coordination, and syntactical functions in historical texts.

## 10. Semantics and Semantic Range

Examines potential meanings of lexemes within semantic fields, recognizing that words possess ranges of meaning rather than single fixed definitions across all contexts.

## 11. Lexicography

Utilizes standard academic lexicons and dictionaries (e.g., HALOT, BDB, LSJ, BDAG, Lane's Lexicon, Hans Wehr) while avoiding uncritical dictionary citation.

## 12. Etymology and Etymological Fallacy Control

Distinguishes etymological origin from historical usage and contextual meaning. Prohibits the etymological fallacy (assuming a word's historical root meaning governs its later contextual usage).

## 13. Transliteration

Separates transliteration (phonetic/alphabetic conversion) from translation and interpretation. Transliteration does not equal translation.

## 14. Translation vs Original Language

Maintains absolute priority of the original-language text over translations. Translations are interpretive transfers, not primary linguistic evidence.

## 15. Contextual Meaning

Requires that lexical and syntactical analysis be strictly constrained by immediate, literary, historical, and cultural context.

## 16. Historical Language Development

Accounts for diachronic shifts in language (archaic, classical, post-classical, koine development) over time, avoiding anachronistic linguistic attributions.

## 17. Detection of Linguistic Claims in Apologetics

Identifies and analyzes common linguistic apologetic claims, checking for root fallacies, false cognates, manufactured lexical definitions, and context-stripping.

## 18. Evidence Hierarchy

1. Primary-language text
2. Critical editions
3. Manuscript evidence
4. Contemporary/near-contemporary linguistic evidence
5. Major lexicons and grammars
6. Peer-reviewed academic research
7. Established scholarly works
8. Classical/commentarial linguistic works
9. Popular educational material
10. Debate/social-media claims

## 19. Claim Classification

Every linguistic claim must be classified as:
- Fact
- Interpretation
- Tradition
- Linguistic Claim
- Apologetic Argument
- Speculation

## 20. Confidence Scale

Uses calibrated confidence labels:
- Established
- Strong Evidence
- Probable
- Plausible
- Disputed
- Uncertain
- Speculative
- Unsupported

## 21. Anti-Bias and Anti-Hallucination Rules

- No fabricated citations.
- No invented lexical meanings.
- No selective dictionary quotation.
- No context stripping.
- No theological conclusion disguised as linguistic fact.
- No treating disputed linguistic theories as established facts.
- Explicitly report scholarly disagreement.
