## 2. EUSEBIUS'S CANONICAL CATEGORIES
Eusebius divides Christian literature into distinct functional categories, refusing to flatten them into a simple binary of "canonical vs. non-canonical":
1. **Recognized / Acknowledged (Homologoumena):** The four Gospels, Acts of the Apostles, Pauline Epistles (including 1 Peter and 1 John). He notes that Revelation may be added by some (or placed among the rejected by others).
2. **Disputed / Antilegomena:** Writings accepted by many but contested in certain regions or circles, including the Epistle of James, Jude, 2 Peter, and 2-3 John.
3. **Spurious / Notothoi:** Books such as the Acts of Paul, the Shepherd of Hermas, the Apocalypse of Peter, the Epistle of Barnabas, the Didache, and (according to some) the Gospel of the Hebrews.
4. **Heretical / Rejected:** Gospels of Peter, Thomas, Matthias, and acts of Andrew, John, etc., which are judged to be complete fabrications of heretics.

## 3. COMPARISON: IRENAEUS vs MURATORIAN vs EUSEBIUS vs ATHANASIUS
* **Irenaeus (~180 CE):** Establishes fourfold Gospel and Pauline core; polemical/theological framework.
* **Muratorian Fragment (Disputed 2nd/4th C):** Regional bibliographic list showing early/alternative boundaries (e.g., accepting Apocalypse of Peter).
* **Eusebius (c. 315–325 CE):** Provides a sophisticated historiographical taxonomy, explicitly acknowledging that while the core is universally recognized, several peripheral books are actively *disputed* (antilegomena) across different churches.
* **Athanasius (367 CE):** Resolves the ambiguity for the Alexandrian church by explicitly drawing a firm line around the exact 27 books and separating instructional books.
* **Synthesis:** Demonstrates that the canon did not drop fully formed from the sky; it was a gradual, historically shifting process of recognition, debate, and regional consolidation.

## 4. CONSTANTINE & NICAEA ASSESSMENT
* **Findings:** Eusebius wrote his *Ecclesiastical History* during the reign of Constantine (and was present at Nicaea in 325 CE). However, neither Constantine nor the Council of Nicaea created the New Testament canon or issued a canon list. Eusebius's categories reflect ongoing scholarly and ecclesiastical debates *prior to* and independent of imperial decree.
