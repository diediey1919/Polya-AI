## A. PRIMARY OBJECTIVE

This audit forensically reconstructs the concept of naskh (abrogation) in Qur'anic
studies. It establishes:

1. **Naskh has multiple definitions** — lexical, technical, exegetical, and legal
   meanings must be distinguished.
2. **Classical scholars disagreed** about the scope and mechanism of naskh.
3. **Naskh ≠ textual corruption** — abrogation of a ruling does not mean editing
   a physical manuscript.
4. **Chronology is essential** — valid naskh requires establishing which revelation
   came first.
5. **Naskh theories developed over time** — later systematizations should not be
   projected backward onto Qur'anic language.
6. **Multiple evidence layers** must be consulted: language, tafsir, ḥadīth, asbāb,
   uṣūl al-fiqh, textual evidence.
7. **Specification ≠ abrogation** — later scholars sometimes classified takhṣīṣ as
   naskh, inflating numbers.
8. **Naskh theory and textual preservation** are analytically distinct categories.

**Final Principle:** "Naskh must be analyzed as a graded exegetical, legal, and
theological concept whose lexical meaning, chronology, textual basis, transmission,
and juristic application must be separated before drawing conclusions about revelation,
law, or textual preservation."

## B. CORE TERMINOLOGY

### B.1 Lexical Root

**ن-س-خ (n-s-kh):**

| Form | Arabic | Transliteration | Primary Meaning |
|------|--------|----------------|-----------------|
| Verb I | نَسَخَ | nasakha | To copy, transcribe |
| Verb I | نَسَخَ | nasakha | To efface, obliterate, remove |
| Verb I | نَسَخَ | nasakha | To supersede, replace |
| Noun | نَسْخ | naskh | Copying, transcription |
| Noun | نَسْخ | naskh | Abrogation, supersession |
| Active Participle | نَاسِخ | nāsikh | Abrogator |
| Passive Participle | مَنْسُوخ | mansūkh | Abrogated |

**Key point:** The root ن-س-خ carries BOTH "copy/transcribe" AND "efface/remove"
meanings. This semantic range is the source of much interpretive disagreement.

**In Hebrew/Aramaic:** The cognate root נסך (nsk) in Hebrew means "to pour out"
(libation), not related. The Arabic root is distinct.

### B.2 Other Key Terms

| Arabic | Transliteration | Meaning | Naskh Relevance |
|--------|----------------|---------|-----------------|
| نُنسِئُها / نَنْسَأْها | nunsiʾuhā / nansaʾuhā | "We postpone/defer it" | Q. 2:106 variant reading |
| آيَة | āyah | Sign, verse, miracle | What is abrogated |
| بَدَل | badal | Replacement, substitute | Q. 16:101 |
| تَبْدِيل | tabdīl | Changing, replacing | Q. 16:101 |
| تَخْصِيص | takhṣīṣ | Specification | Often confused with naskh |
| تَقْيِيد | taqyīd | Qualification, restriction | Often confused with naskh |
| بَيَان | bayān | Clarification, explanation | Distinct from naskh |
| تَدَرُّج | tadarruj | Gradualism | Explains staged legislation |
| مَحْو | maḥw | Effacement | Q. 13:39 |

### B.3 Technical Definitions

**Definition 1 (al-Shāfiʿī, d. 204/820):**
> "Naskh is the removal (rafʿ) of a legal ruling (ḥukm) established by a prior
> text, through a later text that is incompatible with it."

**Definition 2 (al-Āmidī, d. 631/1233):**
> "Naskh is the lifting (rafʿ) of a Sharīʿah ruling by means of a later Sharīʿah
> evidence, such that without the later evidence the earlier ruling would remain."

**Definition 3 (Ibn Ḥazm, d. 456/1064, Ẓāhirī):**
> "Naskh is the removal of a ruling or text by explicit divine statement."
> (Restrictive definition; rejects naskh of Qurʾān by Sunnah)

**Definition 4 (Shāh Walī Allāh, d. 1176/1762):**
> Narrows naskh to genuine contradiction; reclassifies most cases as specification
> (takhṣīṣ), qualification (taqyīd), or contextual differentiation. Reduces
> count to approximately 5 cases.

**Key distinction:** Lexical meaning vs. technical juristic meaning must be
maintained. When a later scholar says "naskh," they may mean specification or
qualification that earlier scholars did not classify as naskh.

## C. QUR'ANIC PASSAGES — MANDATORY PRIMARY ANALYSIS

### C.1 Q. 2:106

**Arabic:**
> مَا نَنْسَخْ مِنْ آيَةٍ أَوْ نُنْسِهَا نَأْتِ بِخَيْرٍ مِنْهَا أَوْ مِثْلِهَا

**Transliteration:** *mā nansakh min āyatin aw nunsihā naʾti bi-khayrin minhā aw mithlihā*

**Literal translation:** "We do not abrogate (nansakh) a verse (āyah) or cause it
to be forgotten (nunsihā), except that We bring [the like of it] or something similar."

**Variant reading:** نُنْسِئُها (nunsiʾuhā, "We defer/postpone it") — attested in
some qirāʾāt and tafsir traditions (al-Ṭabarī, al-Zamakhsharī).

**Immediate context:** Q. 2:105-108 — addressing those who wish a verse were not
revealed, or wish it were replaced. Context is polemical against those who reject
revelation.

**Classical interpretations:**

| Scholar | Interpretation | Key Point |
|---------|---------------|-----------|
| Al-Ṭabarī (d. 310/923) | Abrogation of rulings or texts; replacement with better or similar | Records multiple opinions |
| Al-Qurṭubī (d. 671/1273) | Primarily legal abrogation; discusses types of naskh | Legal framework |
| Al-Zamakhsharī (d. 538/1144) | Emphasizes divine wisdom in gradual revelation; Muʿtazilī framework | Rationalist approach |
| Ibn Kathīr (d. 774/1373) | Naskh as divine mercy; replacement with better rulings | Follows ḥadīth |
| Al-Rāzī (d. 606/1210) | Explores philosophical dimensions; divine wisdom | Theological analysis |

**Modern academic interpretations:**
- **John Burton** (*The Collection of the Qurʾān*, 1977): Argues Q. 2:106 was
  revealed to explain why certain verses were removed from the compiled codex
  without admitting textual loss. Controversial thesis.
- **Andrew Rippin**: Emphasizes the semantic range of naskh; notes that early
  exegetes may not have used the term in its later technical sense.
- **Nicolai Sinai**: Analyzes Q. 2:106 in its literary context; argues for
  intra-Qur'anic supersession without necessarily implying textual deletion.

**Critical finding:** The word نَنْسَخْ (nansakh) in Q. 2:106 does NOT automatically
mean "edit the physical manuscript." The āyah (verse/sign) can mean:
1. A legal ruling (ḥukm)
2. A Qur'anic verse (text)
3. A sign or miracle
4. A general divine communication

**Confidence:** MODERATE — verse is clear in structure; precise referent of "āyah"
is debated.

**Limitations:** Multiple classical interpretations exist; no single reading is
universally agreed.

### C.2 Q. 16:101

**Arabic:**
> وَإِذَا بَدَّلْنَا آيَةً مَّكَانَ آيَةٍ ۙ وَاللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا يُنَزِّلُ
> قَالُوا إِنَّمَا أَنتَ مُفْتَرٍ ۚ بَلْ أَكْثَرُهُمْ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

**Transliteration:** *wa-idhā baddalnā āyatan makāna āyatin wa-llāhu aʿlamu bimā
yunazzilu qālū innamā anta muftarin bal aktharuhum lā yaʿlamūn*

**Literal translation:** "And when We replace (baddalnā) a verse in place of
[another] verse — and God is most knowing of what He reveals — they say, 'You
are but an inventor [of lies].' But most of them do not know."

**Immediate context:** Q. 16:98-105 — addressing accusations that the Prophet
fabricates revelation. Polemical context.

**Key term:** بَدَّلْنَا (baddalnā, "We replace/substitute") — from root ب-د-ل
(b-d-l, "to change, replace, substitute"). Different root from ن-س-خ (n-s-kh).

**Classical interpretations:**
- **Al-Ṭabarī:** "We replace a ruling with another ruling" — primarily legal
  interpretation
- **Al-Zamakhsharī:** Emphasizes progressive revelation; Muʿtazilī framework
- **Ibn Kathīr:** Divine wisdom in gradual revelation; response to pagan objections
- **Al-Qurṭubī:** Naskh as replacement of rulings; discusses ḥadīth evidence

**Critical finding:** Q. 16:101 uses بَدَّلْنَا (baddalnā), not نَسَخْنَا (nasakhnā).
The verse describes a REPLACEMENT of one āyah with another. This does NOT
automatically mean "physical rewriting." It can mean:
1. Replacement of a ruling with another ruling
2. Replacement of one verse with another in the revelatory sequence
3. Progressive revelation addressing changing circumstances

**Do NOT infer manuscript rewriting without evidence.**

**Confidence:** MODERATE — verse is clear about replacement; mechanism unspecified.

### C.3 Q. 13:39

**Arabic:**
> يَمْحُو اللَّهُ مَا يَشَاءُ وَيُثْبِتُ ۖ وَعِندَهُ أُمُّ الْكِتَابِ

**Transliteration:** *yamḥu llāhu mā yashāʾu wa-yuthbitu wa-ʿindahu ummu al-kitāb*

**Literal translation:** "God effaces (yamḥū) what He wills and confirms [what
He wills], and with Him is the Mother of the Book."

**Immediate context:** Q. 13:38-43 — discussing divine sovereignty, signs, and
the Preserved Tablet.

**Key term:** يَمْحُو (yamḥū, "He effaces/erases") — from root م-ح-و (m-ḥ-w, "to
efface, obliterate, erase").

**Classical interpretations:**
- **Al-Ṭabarī:** Multiple opinions: (1) naskh of rulings, (2) divine decree changes,
  (3) general divine sovereignty
- **Al-Qurṭubī:** Primarily naskh interpretation; some cite it for abrogation theory
- **Ibn Kathīr:** Divine decree and predestination; less emphasis on legal naskh
- **Al-Rāzī:** Philosophical analysis of divine will; Preserved Tablet theology

**Critical finding:** يَمْحُو (yamḥū) does NOT automatically mean "physical textual
deletion." It can mean:
1. Divine effacement of rulings (naskh)
2. Changes in divine decree (qaḍāʾ)
3. General divine sovereignty over creation
4. Theological statement about God's power

**Do NOT automatically equate maḥw with physical textual deletion.**

**Confidence:** MODERATE — verse describes divine action; specific referent debated.

### C.4 Q. 17:86

**Arabic:**
> وَلَئِن شِئْنَا لَنَذْهَبَنَّ بِالَّذِي أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْكَ ثُمَّ لَا تَجِدُ
> لَكَ بِهِ عَلَيْنَا وَكِيلًا

**Transliteration:** *wa-laʾin shiʾnā lanadhhabanna bi-lladhī awḥaynā ilayka
thumma lā tajidu laka bihi ʿalaynā wakīlan*

**Literal translation:** "And if We willed, We could surely take away that which
We have revealed to you. Then you would not find for yourself a protector
against Us."

**Immediate context:** Q. 17:85-88 — addressing the nature of revelation and
divine power. Theological context.

**Classical interpretations:**
- **Al-Ṭabarī:** Statement of divine sovereignty; NOT typically used in naskh theory
- **Ibn Kathīr:** Divine power over revelation; reassurance of Prophet
- **Al-Qurṭubī:** Not primarily a naskh verse; emphasizes divine authority

**Critical finding:** Q. 17:86 is a THEOLOGICAL statement about divine sovereignty,
NOT a technical naskh passage. It states God COULD remove revelation if He willed.
This is a conditional/hypothetical statement about divine power, not about actual
abrogation.

**Confidence:** HIGH — verse is clearly theological, not about naskh mechanism.

### C.5 Q. 87:6-7

**Arabic:**
> سَنُقْرِئُكَ فَلَا تَنسَىٰ * إِلَّا مَا شَاءَ اللَّهُ

**Transliteration:** *sanuqriʾuka fa-lā tansā * illā mā shāʾa llāh*

**Literal translation:** "We will make you recite, so you will not forget — except
what God wills."

**Immediate context:** Q. 87:1-8 — addressing the Prophet directly about revelation
and memory.

**Classical interpretations:**
- **Al-Ṭabarī:** (1) Divine guarantee of preservation, (2) exception for abrogated

- **Al-Qurṭubī:** Exception clause refers to abrogated rulings/texts
- **Ibn Kathīr:** Divine preservation of revelation; exception for what God removes
- **Al-Suyūṭī:** Used as evidence for naskh al-tilāwah

**Critical finding:** The exception clause (illā mā shāʾa llāh) does NOT automatically
mean prophetic forgetting equals textual abrogation. Possible interpretations:
1. Divine exception for abrogated material (naskh al-tilāwah)
2. General divine sovereignty over memory
3. Rhetorical reassurance with theological qualification
4. Reference to gradual legislation (tadarruj)

**Do NOT automatically equate prophetic forgetting with textual abrogation.**

**Confidence:** LOW — multiple interpretations; exception clause ambiguous.

### C.6 Q. 22:52

**Arabic:**
> وَمَا أَرْسَلْنَا مِن قَبْلِكَ مِن رَّسُولٍ وَلَا نَبِيٍّ إِلَّا إِذَا تَمَنَّىٰ
> أَلْقَى الشَّيْطَانُ فِي أُمْنِيَّتِهِ ۖ فَيَنسَخُ اللَّهُ مَا يُلْقِي الشَّيْطَانُ
> ثُمَّ يُحْكِمُ اللَّهُ آيَاتِهِ

**Transliteration:** *wa-mā arsalnā min qablika min rasūlin wa-lā nabiyyin illā
idhā tamannā alqā al-shayṭānu fī umniyyatihi fa-yansakhu llāhu mā yulqī
al-shayṭānu thumma yuḥkimu llāhu āyātihi*

**Literal translation:** "And We did not send before you any messenger or prophet
except that when he spoke [or recited], Satan threw into it [some interference].
But God effaces (yan-sakhu) what Satan throws in; then God makes precise His
verses."

**Immediate context:** Q. 22:49-55 — addressing prophetic experience and Satan's
interference. Theological context.

**Key term:** يَنسَخُ (yansakh, "He effaces/removes") — from ن-س-خ (n-s-kh).

**Classical interpretations:**
- **Al-Ṭabarī:** Satan's interference is removed by God; divine protection of
  revelation
- **Al-Qurṭubī:** Naskh here means removal/effacement of Satan's insertion
- **Ibn Kathīr:** Discusses Gharānīq (Satanic Verses) incident; divine protection
- **Al-Zamakhsharī:** Muʿtazilī interpretation; emphasizes divine justice and
  prophetic protection

**Critical finding:** The naskh in Q. 22:52 means EFFACEMENT/REMOVAL of Satan's
interference — NOT abrogation of a Qur'anic ruling. This is a different semantic
use from legal naskh.

**Gharānīq connection:** Some classical scholars link Q. 22:52 to the disputed
"Satanic Verses" incident. However, the historicity of this incident is debated
among both classical and modern scholars.

**Do NOT turn disputed exegetical narratives into historical facts.**

**Confidence:** HIGH — verse clearly describes removal of Satanic interference;
NOT legal naskh.

## D. Q. 2:106 DETAILED FORENSIC AUDIT

### D.1 Lexical Possibilities

**نَنْسَخْ (nansakh):**
1. "We abrogate" (technical legal sense)
2. "We efface/remove" (literal effacement)
3. "We supersede/replace" (replacement sense)
4. "We copy/transcribe" (original lexical sense)

**آيَة (āyah):**
1. Legal ruling (ḥukm sharʿī)
2. Qur'anic verse (text)
3. Sign or miracle
4. General divine communication

**نُنْسِهَا (nunsihā):**
1. "We cause it to be forgotten" (from ن-س-ي, n-s-y, forgetfulness)
2. "We leave it/abandon it" (from n-s-y, to leave)
3. Variant reading: نُنْسِئُها (nunsiʾuhā, "We defer/postpone it")

**بِخَيْرٍ مِّنْهَا (bi-khayrin minhā):**
1. "Better than it" (in reward/benefit)
2. "Better ruling" (legal superiority)

**أَوْ مِثْلِهَا (aw mithlihā):**
1. "Or similar to it" (equivalent replacement)

### D.2 Syntactic Possibilities

**Reading 1 (Standard legal interpretation):**
"We do not abrogate a ruling/verse, or cause it to be forgotten, except that
We bring something better or similar."

**Reading 2 (Postponement interpretation, nunsiʾuhā):**
"We do not abrogate a verse, or defer it, except that We bring something better
or similar."

**Reading 3 (Effacement interpretation):**
"We do not efface a sign/verse, or cause it to be forgotten, except that We
bring something better or similar."

### D.3 Classical Tafsir Summary

| Tafsir Work | Author (d.) | Primary Interpretation | Naskh Theory |
|-------------|-------------|----------------------|--------------|
| Jāmiʿ al-Bayān | Al-Ṭabarī (310/923) | Abrogation of rulings; replacement with better | Foundational |
| Al-Kashshāf | Al-Zamakhsharī (538/1144) | Divine wisdom; progressive revelation | Muʿtazilī framework |
| Aḥkām al-Qurʾān | Al-Jaṣṣāṣ (370/981) | Legal abrogation; Ḥanafī framework | Legal methodology |
| Al-Jāmiʿ li-Aḥkām al-Qurʾān | Al-Qurṭubī (671/1273) | Legal naskh; detailed classification | Mālikī framework |
| Tafsīr al-Qurʾān al-ʿAẓīm | Ibn Kathīr (774/1373) | Divine mercy; replacement with better | Ḥadīth-based |
| Mafātīḥ al-Ghayb | Al-Rāzī (606/1210) | Philosophical analysis; divine wisdom | Theological |

### D.4 Legal Interpretation

**Primary legal reading:** Q. 2:106 establishes the principle that when God abrogates
a legal ruling, He replaces it with something better or equivalent. This justifies
legal change within the revelatory process.

**Key legal implications:**
1. Naskh is a legitimate divine mechanism
2. Abrogated rulings are replaced, not simply cancelled
3. The replacement is "better or similar" — divine wisdom ensures improvement

**Limitations:** The verse does not specify the MECHANISM of abrogation (textual
removal vs. ruling change vs. supersession).

### D.5 Theological Interpretation

**Theological reading:** Q. 2:106 is a statement about divine sovereignty over
revelation. God controls what is revealed, preserved, abrogated, or forgotten.

**Key theological implications:**
1. Divine control over revelation
2. Progressive revelation as divine wisdom
3. Nothing is "lost" — replacement ensures continuity

### D.6 Modern Academic Interpretation

**John Burton (1977):** Q. 2:106 was revealed to explain why certain verses are
missing from the compiled codex without admitting textual loss. The verse serves
an apologetic function.

**Criticism of Burton:**
- Circular reasoning (assumes verses were lost, then explains their absence)
- Lacks manuscript evidence for "missing verses"
- Most scholars reject his thesis as speculative

**Andrew Rippin:** Emphasizes semantic range; early exegetes may have used "naskh"
in a broader sense than later technical definitions.

**Nicolai Sinai:** Analyzes intra-Qur'anic context; argues for supersession without
necessarily implying textual deletion.

### D.7 Critical Distinction

**"Replacement/abrogation of a sign or ruling" is NOT automatically "editing the
physical Qur'anic manuscript."**

The verse can describe:
1. Legal abrogation (ruling replaced, text remains)
2. Textual abrogation (text removed from recitation)
3. Progressive revelation (new revelation supersedes old)
4. Divine sovereignty (general theological statement)

**Manuscript alteration is a separate claim requiring separate evidence.**

## E. Q. 16:101 DETAILED AUDIT

### E.1 Key Term: بَدَّلْنَا (baddalnā)

**Root:** ب-د-ل (b-d-l, "to change, replace, substitute")

**Forms:**
- بَدَّلَ (baddala, Form II): To change, alter, substitute
- تَبْدِيل (tabdīl, verbal noun): Changing, replacement

**Semantic range:**
1. Replace one thing with another
2. Change or alter something
3. Substitute

### E.2 "Place of a Verse" Interpretation

**مَكَانَ آيَةٍ (makāna āyatin):**
1. "In place of a verse" — spatial/location metaphor
2. "Instead of a verse" — replacement/substitution
3. "In the position of a verse" — sequential replacement in revelation

### E.3 Relation to Revelation Sequence

**Interpretation:** The verse describes the process of progressive revelation where
later revelations address new circumstances, effectively "replacing" earlier guidance.

**This does NOT require physical manuscript alteration.** It describes the revelatory
process, not codex editing.

### E.4 Relation to Legal Rulings

**Primary legal interpretation:** God replaces one legal ruling with another. The
earlier ruling is superseded by the later ruling.

**Example (classical):** Q. 2:180 (bequest for parents) superseded by Q. 4:11
(inheritance shares).

### E.5 Relation to Textual Replacement

**Critical finding:** Q. 16:101 does NOT explicitly describe physical textual
replacement. The verse uses بَدَّلْنَا (We replaced) in the context of divine
revelation, not manuscript editing.

**Do NOT infer manuscript rewriting without evidence.**

**Confidence:** MODERATE — verse describes replacement; mechanism unspecified.

## F. Q. 13:39 DETAILED AUDIT

### F.1 Key Term: مَحْو (maḥw)

**Root:** م-ح-و (m-ḥ-w, "to efface, obliterate, erase")

**Semantic range:**
1. Physical erasure (wiping away writing)
2. Metaphorical effacement (removal of ruling, decree)
3. Divine action (God's sovereign removal)

### F.2 Divine Decree Interpretation

**Primary reading:** Q. 13:39 describes divine sovereignty over decree (qaḍāʾ).
God effaces some decrees and confirms others. This is about divine providence,
not necessarily legal abrogation.

**Example:** God may decree a lifespan, then change it due to prayer or charity
(classical theological interpretation).

### F.3 Naskh Interpretation

**Secondary reading:** Some classical scholars interpret Q. 13:39 as evidence for
naskh theory. God "effaces" earlier rulings and "confirms" later rulings.

**Limitation:** This is a SECONDARY interpretation; the primary context is theological.

### F.4 Theological Interpretation

**Primary context:** Q. 13:39 is a theological statement about divine sovereignty
and the Preserved Tablet (umm al-kitāb). God controls what is written, effaced,
and confirmed.

**Do NOT automatically equate maḥw with physical textual deletion.**

**Confidence:** HIGH — verse is primarily theological; naskh is secondary reading.

## H. Q. 87:6-7 DETAILED AUDIT

### H.1 Prophetic Memory

**Primary reading:** God guarantees the Prophet will remember revelation, with the
exception "except what God wills."

### H.2 Divine Exception

**The exception clause (illā mā shāʾa llāh):**
1. What God wills to abrogate (naskh al-tilāwah)
2. What God wills to supersede (gradual legislation)
3. General divine sovereignty over memory
4. Rhetorical qualification

### H.3 Classical Tafsir

**Al-Suyūṭī (al-Itqān):** Uses Q. 87:6-7 as evidence for naskh al-tilāwah — verses
that were revealed but later removed from recitation.

**Al-Ṭabarī:** Records multiple opinions: (1) abrogated verses, (2) general divine
control, (3) preservation guarantee with theological exception.

### H.4 Modern Interpretation

**Critical finding:** The exception clause does NOT automatically mean prophetic
forgetting equals textual abrogation. It can be:
1. Theological statement about divine sovereignty
2. Reference to abrogated material (but not necessarily physically removed)
3. Rhetorical reassurance with divine qualification

**Do NOT automatically equate prophetic forgetting with textual abrogation.**

**Confidence:** LOW — multiple interpretations; exception clause ambiguous.

## I. Q. 22:52 DETAILED AUDIT

### I.1 Linguistic Meaning

**يَنسَخُ (yansakh):** "He effaces/removes" — from ن-س-خ (n-s-kh).

**Context:** God effaces what Satan throws in (interference), then makes His verses
precise.

### I.2 Satanic Interference Interpretation

**Primary reading:** God protects revelation by removing Satan's attempted
interference. This is about divine protection, not legal abrogation.

**Gharānīq connection:** Some classical scholars link Q. 22:52 to the disputed
"Satanic Verses" incident where Satan allegedly inserted verses praising pagan
goddesses (al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, Manāt). God then removed these insertions.

**Historicity:** The Gharānīq incident is DEBATED among classical and modern scholars.
Many reject it as historically unreliable.

### I.3 Textual Preservation Interpretation

**Secondary reading:** Q. 22:52 demonstrates divine protection of revelation. God
ensures His verses are precise and free from Satanic corruption.

### I.4 Naskh Here Means Removal, Not Legal Abrogation

**Critical finding:** The naskh in Q. 22:52 means REMOVAL/EFFACEMENT of Satan's
interference. This is a DIFFERENT semantic use from legal naskh (abrogation of
rulings).

**Do NOT conflate the two uses of naskh.**

**Confidence:** HIGH — verse clearly describes removal of Satanic interference.

## J. TYPES OF NASKH — TAXONOMY

### J.1 Classification System

| Type | Arabic | Definition | Example | Status |
|------|--------|-----------|---------|--------|
| 1 | نَسْخ الحُكْم دُونَ التِّلاوَة | Ruling abrogated, text remains | Q. 2:180 (bequest) abrogated by Q. 4:11 (inheritance) | Widely accepted |
| 2 | نَسْخ التِّلاوَة دُونَ الحُكْم | Text abrogated, ruling remains | Stoning verse (āyat al-rajm) — ruling applies, text not in codex | Controversial |
| 3 | نَسْخ الحُكْم والتِّلاوَة | Both ruling and text abrogated | Theoretical; no clear examples | Debated |
| 4 | تَخْصِيص (takhṣīṣ) | Specification of general ruling | Q. 2:228 (divorce waiting) specified by Q. 65:4 (pregnant women) | Not true naskh |
| 5 | تَقْيِيد (taqyīd) | Qualification of unrestricted ruling | General ruling qualified by exception | Not true naskh |
| 6 | بَيَان (bayān) | Clarification | Ambiguous ruling clarified | Not true naskh |
| 7 | تَدَرُّج (tadarruj) | Gradual legislation | Fasting stages (Q. 2:183-185) | Not true naskh |
| 8 | نَسْخ مُطْلَق (supersession) | Later revelation supersedes earlier | Qiblah change (Jerusalem → Mecca) | Accepted |

### J.2 Classical Proponents

**Type 1 (Ruling abrogated, text remains):**
- **Proponents:** Majority of classical scholars (al-Shāfiʿī, al-Jaṣṣāṣ, al-Qurṭubī)
- **Evidence:** Q. 2:106, Q. 16:101, ḥadīth reports
- **Status:** Widely accepted

**Type 2 (Text abrogated, ruling remains):**
- **Proponents:** Some classical scholars (al-Suyūṭī, Ibn Ḥajar)
- **Evidence:** Stoning verse reports, Q. 87:6-7
- **Counterarguments:** No manuscript evidence for "missing" verses; circular reasoning
- **Status:** Controversial; rejected by many modern scholars

**Type 3 (Both abrogated):**
- **Proponents:** Theoretical category
- **Evidence:** None clear
- **Status:** Debated; most scholars reject or find no examples

**Types 4-7 (Not true naskh):**
- **Proponents:** Shāh Walī Allāh, modern reformists
- **Argument:** These are specification, qualification, clarification, or gradual
  legislation — NOT abrogation
- **Status:** Accepted by many modern scholars

### J.3 Critical Distinction

**Many cases classified as "naskh" by earlier scholars are actually:**
- Specification (takhṣīṣ) — narrowing a general ruling
- Qualification (taqyīd) — adding conditions to unrestricted ruling
- Clarification (bayān) — explaining ambiguous ruling
- Gradual legislation (tadarruj) — staged implementation

**This explains why counts of "abrogated verses" vary dramatically (from 5 to 500+).**

## K. NASKH AND TEXTUAL TRANSMISSION (INTEGRATION: STEP 101-106)

### K.1 Strict Distinction

**NASKH THEORY ≠ MANUSCRIPT VARIANT ≠ QIRĀʾĀT ≠ RASM ≠ COMPANION CODEX ≠ TEXTUAL CORRUPTION**

**Naskh theory** is a THEOLOGICAL/LEGAL concept about how divine revelation addresses
changing circumstances. It describes the MECHANISM of legal change within the
revelatory process.

**Manuscript variants** are TEXTUAL evidence — physical differences in surviving
manuscripts (STEP 102, 103).

**Qirāʾāt** are READING TRADITIONS — different oral/textual traditions for reciting
the Qurʾān (STEP 104).

**Rasm** is the CONSONANTAL SKELETON — the base text without diacritics (STEP 104).

**Companion codices** are PRE-ʿUTHMĀNIC manuscripts — variant codices from early
companions before ʿUthmānic standardization (STEP 106).

**Textual corruption** is a POLEMICAL CLAIM that the Qurʾān has been altered.

**These are SEPARATE evidence categories. A report that a ruling was abrogated
does NOT automatically establish that a physical manuscript was altered.**

### K.2 Naskh and Manuscript Evidence

**Critical question:** Does naskh theory require manuscript alteration?

**Answer:** NO. Naskh can occur through:
1. Legal supersession (new ruling replaces old, both texts remain in codex)
2. Textual removal (verse removed from recitation but ruling remains)
3. Complete abrogation (both text and ruling removed)

**Only Type 2 and Type 3 would require physical removal from a manuscript.**

**Manuscript evidence (STEP 102, 103):**
- Early manuscripts (Ṣanʿāʾ, Birmingham, etc.) show the standard ʿUthmānic text
- No clear evidence of "missing verses" in surviving manuscripts
- Variants are primarily orthographic, dialectal, or qirāʾāt differences

**Conclusion:** There is NO strong manuscript evidence for large-scale textual
removal due to naskh.

### K.3 Naskh al-Tilāwah and Manuscript Evidence

**The controversial claim:** Some verses were revealed but later removed from the
codex (naskh al-tilāwah).

**Examples cited:**
- Stoning verse (āyat al-rajm) — see Section Y.8
- "Valley of gold" verse — reported in some ḥadīth
- Other scattered reports

**Manuscript evidence:**
- NO clear evidence in early manuscripts (Ṣanʿāʾ, Birmingham, etc.)
- NO evidence in companion codices (Ibn Masʿūd, Ubayy ibn Kaʿb)
- Reports are primarily ḥadīth-based, not manuscript-based

**Critical finding:** The claim of naskh al-tilāwah is primarily based on ḥadīth
reports, NOT manuscript evidence. This is a THEOLOGICAL/LEGAL claim, not a
textual-historical claim.

**Do NOT conflate naskh theory with manuscript alteration.**

## L. NASKH AND QIRĀʾĀT (INTEGRATION: STEP 104)

### L.1 Qirāʾāt Differences Are NOT Naskh

**Critical distinction:** Qirāʾāt differences (variant readings) are NOT examples
of naskh. They are different READING TRADITIONS for the SAME text.

**Example:** Q. 1:4 — مَالِكِ يَوْمِ الدِّينِ (māliki, "Master") vs. مَلِكِ يَوْمِ
الدِّينِ (maliki, "King"). Both are canonical qirāʾāt. This is NOT naskh.

**Do NOT classify a qirāʾāt difference as naskh automatically.**

### L.2 Q. 2:106 Variant Reading

**نُنْسِهَا (nunsihā) vs. نُنْسِئُها (nunsiʾuhā):**
- This is a QIRĀʾĀT VARIANT, not an example of naskh
- Both readings are attested in classical tafsir
- The variant affects interpretation but does not demonstrate textual abrogation

### L.3 Separation Principle

**Maintain strict separation:**
- QIRĀʾĀT = reading traditions (STEP 104)
- NASKH = abrogation theory (this audit)

**Do NOT conflate the two.**

## M. NASKH AND ḤADĪTH (INTEGRATION: STEP 105)

### M.1 Ḥadīth Reports Used for Naskh

**Examples:**
- "The Prophet said: 'I used to forbid you from visiting graves, but now visit
  them.'" (Muslim) — used for naskh of prohibition
- "The Prophet said: 'There is no bequest for an heir.'" (Abū Dāwūd, Tirmidhī)
  — used for naskh of Q. 2:180
- Stoning reports — used for naskh al-tilāwah

### M.2 Chronology and Ḥadīth

**Critical requirement:** Naskh requires establishing WHICH revelation came first.
Ḥadīth can provide chronology, but:

1. Ḥadīth chronology is not always reliable
2. Competing reports may give different chronologies
3. Ḥadīth grading (ṣaḥīḥ/ḥasan/ḍaʿīf) does not automatically establish
   chronology

### M.3 Ḥadīth Evidence ≠ Proof of Textual Deletion

**Critical distinction:** Ḥadīth evidence for naskh does NOT automatically prove
textual deletion.

**Example:** Ḥadīth reporting that a ruling was abrogated does NOT mean the verse
was physically removed from the codex. It may mean the RULING was superseded while
the TEXT remains.

**Mandatory distinction:**
```
ḤADĪTH EVIDENCE FOR NASKH
    ≠
PROOF OF TEXTUAL DELETION
```

## N. NASKH AND ASBĀB AL-NUZŪL (INTEGRATION: STEP 109)

### N.1 Chronology is Essential

**Naskh requires chronology:**
1. Earlier revelation (mansūkh)
2. Historical occasion (sabab)
3. Later revelation (nāsikh)
4. Reported abrogation

**Without reliable chronology, naskh cannot be established.**

### N.2 Asbāb Reports and Chronology

**Asbāb al-nuzūl reports can provide chronology, but:**
1. Asbāb reports have their own transmission issues (STEP 109)
2. Multiple conflicting asbāb reports may exist
3. Asbāb chronology is not always reliable
4. Source criticism is required (STEP 109)

### N.3 Valid Naskh Argument Requires

**A valid naskh claim requires establishing:**
1. Earlier rule (with reliable evidence)
2. Later rule (with reliable evidence)
3. Substantive incompatibility (cannot be reconciled)
4. Authority of later revelation (divine supersession)
5. Reliable chronology (which came first)

**Do NOT assume chronology merely because one report says so.**

## O. NASKH AND TAFSIR (INTEGRATION: STEP 108)

### O.1 Tafsir and Naskh Interpretation

**For every naskh interpretation, distinguish:**
```
VERSE (Qur'anic text)
    ↓
LANGUAGE (lexical analysis)
    ↓
TAFSIR (exegetical interpretation)
    ↓
CHRONOLOGY (which revelation came first)
    ↓
LEGAL CONCLUSION (fiqh ruling)
```

### O.2 Do NOT Use Later Terminology as Proof

**Critical principle:** Later tafsir terminology (e.g., "nāsikh/mansūkh") should
NOT be used as proof of the original lexical meaning.

**Example:** When al-Qurṭubī (d. 671/1273) classifies a verse as "mansūkh," this
is a LATER legal classification, not necessarily the original Qur'anic meaning.

**Maintain distinction:**
- Qur'anic language (7th century)
- Tafsir interpretation (8th-15th centuries)
- Modern scholarship (19th-21st centuries)

## P. NASKH AND FIQH (INTEGRATION: SPECIALIST 09)

### P.1 Naskh in Uṣūl al-Fiqh

**Naskh is a central concept in Islamic legal theory (uṣūl al-fiqh).** It provides
the mechanism for legal change within the revelatory framework.

### P.2 Madhhab Approaches

**Al-Shāfiʿī (d. 204/820):**
- **Work:** Al-Risālah
- **Definition:** Naskh is removal of a ruling by a later incompatible text
- **Methodology:** Strict; requires clear evidence
- **Sunnah abrogating Qurʾān:** Accepted (with conditions)

**Ḥanafī school:**
- **Approach:** More flexible; accepts specification (takhṣīṣ) more readily
- **Sunnah abrogating Qurʾān:** Accepted (broader than Shāfiʿī)
- **Key scholars:** Al-Jaṣṣāṣ (d. 370/981), al-Ṭaḥāwī (d. 321/933)

**Mālikī school:**
- **Approach:** Balances text and practice (ʿamal ahl al-Madīnah)
- **Sunnah abrogating Qurʾān:** Accepted
- **Key scholars:** Al-Qurṭubī (d. 671/1273)

**Ḥanbalī school:**
- **Approach:** Text-focused; follows ḥadīth closely
- **Sunnah abrogating Qurʾān:** Accepted
- **Key scholars:** Ibn Qudāmah (d. 620/1223)

**Ẓāhirī school (Ibn Ḥazm, d. 456/1064):**
- **Approach:** Restrictive; rejects most naskh claims
- **Sunnah abrogating Qurʾān:** REJECTED — Qurʾān can only be abrogated by Qurʾān
- **Definition:** Naskh requires explicit divine statement

**Critical finding:** Madhhabs do NOT have identical naskh methodology. Ibn Ḥazm
(Ẓāhirī) is significantly more restrictive than other schools.

### P.3 Do NOT Assume Uniform Methodology

**Each madhhab has its own:**
- Definition of naskh
- Requirements for establishing naskh
- Acceptance of Sunnah abrogating Qurʾān
- Number of recognized naskh cases

**Do NOT present one school as universal Islamic doctrine.**

## Q. NASKH AND THE SUNNAH

### Q.1 Possible Categories

| Category | Description | Classical Position |
|----------|-------------|-------------------|
| Qurʾān abrogates Qurʾān | Later verse supersedes earlier verse | Universally accepted |
| Qurʾān abrogates Sunnah | Qur'anic verse supersedes Prophetic practice | Universally accepted |
| Sunnah abrogates Sunnah | Later ḥadīth supersedes earlier ḥadīth | Universally accepted |
| Sunnah abrogates Qurʾān | Prophetic practice supersedes Qur'anic ruling | CONTROVERSIAL |

### Q.2 Classical Disagreements

**Sunnah abrogating Qurʾān — Classical positions:**

**Al-Shāfiʿī:** Accepted with conditions — the Sunnah must be mutawātir (mass-transmitted)
or have strong evidence.

**Ḥanafī:** Accepted more broadly — Sunnah can abrogate Qurʾān if there is clear
evidence.

**Mālikī:** Accepted — follows Prophetic practice.

**Ḥanbalī:** Accepted — follows ḥadīth closely.

**Ẓāhirī (Ibn Ḥazm):** REJECTED — Qurʾān can only be abrogated by Qurʾān. Sunnah
cannot abrogate Qurʾān.

**Modern reformists:** Generally REJECTED — Qurʾān has supremacy; Sunnah clarifies
but does not abrogate.

### Q.3 Example: Stoning for Adultery

**Qurʾān:** Q. 24:2 prescribes 100 lashes for adultery (zinā).

**Sunnah:** Ḥadīth reports stoning (rajm) for married adulterers.

**Classical majority:** Sunnah specifies Qurʾān — 100 lashes for unmarried, stoning
for married.

**Ibn Ḥazm (Ẓāhirī):** REJECTED — Qurʾān says 100 lashes; Sunnah cannot abrogate
this. Stoning is not Qur'anic punishment.

**Modern reformists:** Generally follow Ibn Ḥazm — reject stoning as non-Qur'anic.

**Critical finding:** This is a MAJOR classical disagreement. Do NOT present one
position as universal Islamic doctrine.

- Different definitions of naskh

## T. SHĀH WALĪ ALLĀH DETAILED AUDIT

### T.1 Narrower Approach

**Shāh Walī Allāh's classification:**

1. **True naskh:** Genuine contradiction where no reconciliation is possible
   (~5 cases)

2. **Specification (takhṣīṣ):** General ruling narrowed to specific cases
   (NOT naskh)

3. **Qualification (taqyīd):** Unrestricted ruling qualified by conditions
   (NOT naskh)

4. **Contextual differentiation:** Different rulings for different contexts
   (NOT naskh)

5. **Gradual legislation (tadarruj):** Staged implementation
   (NOT naskh)

### T.2 Example Re-classifications

**Traditional naskh claim:** Q. 2:180 (bequest for parents) abrogated by Q. 4:11
(inheritance shares)

**Shāh Walī Allāh:** This is SPECIFICATION, not naskh. Q. 2:180 is a general
principle; Q. 4:11 specifies the shares. Both remain valid.

**Traditional naskh claim:** Q. 2:240 (widow maintenance for one year) abrogated
by Q. 2:234 (four months and ten days)

**Shāh Walī Allāh:** This is CONTEXTUAL DIFFERENTIATION. Q. 2:240 applies to
maintenance; Q. 2:234 applies to waiting period (ʿiddah). Both remain valid.

### T.3 Critical Finding

**Shāh Walī Allāh demonstrates that most "naskh" cases are actually:**
- Specification (takhṣīṣ)
- Qualification (taqyīd)
- Contextual differentiation
- Gradual legislation (tadarruj)

**Only ~5 cases are genuine naskh (substantive incompatibility with no
reconciliation).**

**Do NOT present his system as identical to earlier classical systems.** He is
significantly more restrictive.

## U. TRADITIONAL SUNNI STEELMAN

### U.1 Strongest Traditional Argument

**Divine wisdom in gradual legislation:**

1. **Premise:** God reveals guidance progressively to address changing circumstances.

2. **Evidence:**
   - Q. 2:106: "We do not abrogate a verse... except that We bring [the like
     of it] or something similar."
   - Q. 16:101: "When We replace a verse in place of [another] verse..."
   - Prophetic chronology: 23 years of revelation addressing evolving community

3. **Reasoning:**
   - Progressive revelation is divine wisdom (ḥikmah)
   - Early rulings addressed early circumstances
   - Later rulings address later circumstances
   - Naskh is the mechanism for legal change within revelation

4. **Ḥadīth evidence:**
   - "I used to forbid you from visiting graves, but now visit them." (Muslim)
   - Stoning reports (Bukhārī, Muslim)
   - Qiblah change reports (multiple sources)

5. **Legal methodology:**
   - Naskh provides mechanism for legal development
   - Ensures Sharīʿah remains relevant to changing circumstances
   - Preserves divine authority while allowing adaptation

### U.2 Strongest Version Before Criticism

**The strongest traditional argument:**
- Naskh is a legitimate divine mechanism for legal change
- Supported by Qurʾān (Q. 2:106, 16:101) and ḥadīth
- Essential for understanding legal development in early Islam
- Preserves both divine authority and practical relevance

**This argument is internally coherent and has strong classical support.**

## V. CRITICAL ACADEMIC STEELMAN

### V.1 Strongest Critical Model

**Legal development and exegetical retrojection:**

1. **Premise:** Naskh theory developed as a retrospective mechanism to harmonize
   legal contradictions and explain textual difficulties.

2. **Evidence:**
   - **John Burton** (*The Collection of the Qurʾān*, 1977): Naskh al-tilāwah
     was invented to explain missing verses without admitting textual loss
   - **Joseph Schacht:** Isnād backward growth; later legal positions projected
     back to Prophet
   - **Andrew Rippin:** Naskh terminology developed over time; early exegetes
     used broader meanings

3. **Reasoning:**
   - Legal schools developed different positions
   - Contradictory ḥadīth needed harmonization
   - Naskh provided mechanism to explain contradictions
   - Some naskh claims may be retrojected to justify later legal positions

4. **Source-critical analysis:**
   - Naskh theory developed in 8th-10th centuries (not 7th century)
   - Later scholars (al-Naḥḥās, al-Makkī) inflated counts
   - Narrower scholars (al-Zarkashī, Shāh Walī Allāh) criticized overuse

5. **Textual criticism:**
   - No clear manuscript evidence for "missing verses"
   - Naskh al-tilāwah is primarily ḥadīth-based, not manuscript-based
   - Circular reasoning: assumes verses were lost, then explains their absence

### V.2 Distinguishing Positions

**Mainstream academic:**
- Naskh theory developed over time
- Some naskh claims may be retrojected
- Requires source-critical analysis

**Minority academic:**
- Naskh is entirely a later invention
- No historical basis in 7th-century revelation

**Speculative:**
- Burton's thesis that naskh al-tilāwah was invented to hide textual loss
- Lacks manuscript evidence; circular reasoning

**Controversial:**
- Claims that Qurʾān was edited/altered based on naskh theory
- Polemical; not supported by mainstream scholarship

### V.3 Strongest Version Before Criticism

**The strongest critical argument:**
- Naskh theory developed as a retrospective harmonization mechanism
- Some naskh claims may be retrojected to justify later legal positions
- Requires source-critical analysis of transmission and chronology
- Naskh al-tilāwah lacks manuscript evidence

**This argument has merit for source-critical analysis but should not be
caricatured as "Qurʾān is corrupted."**

## W. TEXTUAL CRITICISM CONTROL

### W.1 The Claim: "Abrogation Proves the Qurʾān Was Changed"

**Classification:** POLEMICAL CLAIM requiring careful analysis

### W.2 Naskh Within Revelation

**Theological/interpretive concept:** Naskh describes how divine revelation addresses
changing circumstances. It is a THEOLOGICAL concept about the revelatory process.

**This does NOT automatically prove manuscript alteration.**

### W.3 Manuscript Alteration

**Textual-historical claim:** Manuscript alteration is a claim about PHYSICAL TEXTS
being changed, edited, or corrupted.

**This requires MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE, not theological argument.**

### W.4 Critical Distinction

**NASKH THEORY (theological/legal concept)**
    ≠
**MANUSCRIPT ALTERATION (textual-historical claim)**

**They require different evidence:**
- Naskh theory: Qurʾānic verses, ḥadīth, tafsir, uṣūl al-fiqh
- Manuscript alteration: Physical manuscripts, paleography, codicology, textual
  criticism

### W.5 Evaluating the Claim

**Does naskh prove the Qurʾān was changed?**

**Answer:** NO. Naskh is a theological/legal concept about how revelation addresses
changing circumstances. It does NOT prove manuscript alteration.

**To prove manuscript alteration, one would need:**
1. Physical manuscript evidence showing editing/changes
2. Paleographic evidence of textual layers
3. Codicological evidence of revision
4. Textual-critical evidence of corruption

**Current manuscript evidence (STEP 102, 103, 106):**
- Early manuscripts (Ṣanʿāʾ, Birmingham, etc.) show standard ʿUthmānic text
- No clear evidence of large-scale editing or removal
- Variants are primarily orthographic, dialectal, or qirāʾāt differences

**Conclusion:** Naskh theory does NOT prove manuscript alteration. This is a
polemical claim that conflates theological concept with textual evidence.

## Z. ERROR REGISTER (E1–E12)

### E1: Naskh = Corruption

**Error:** Equating naskh (abrogation) with textual corruption.

**Risk:** HIGH — conflates theological concept with textual evidence.

**Why wrong:** Naskh is a theological/legal concept about how revelation addresses
changing circumstances. Textual corruption is a claim about manuscript alteration.
They are separate categories.

**Safe formulation:** Naskh is a theological/legal concept that must be distinguished
from textual corruption. They require different evidence.

**Status:** REGISTERED

---

### E2: Naskh = Manuscript Alteration

**Error:** Assuming naskh automatically means physical manuscript was altered.

**Risk:** HIGH — conflates theological concept with physical evidence.

**Why wrong:** Naskh can occur through: (1) ruling abrogated, text remains; (2)
text abrogated, ruling remains; (3) both abrogated. Only types 2-3 would involve
textual removal, and even then, this is a theological claim requiring manuscript
evidence.

**Safe formulation:** Naskh theory does not automatically prove manuscript alteration.
Manuscript evidence is required separately.

**Status:** REGISTERED

---

### E3: Naskh = Qirāʾāt

**Error:** Classifying qirāʾāt differences as examples of naskh.

**Risk:** MODERATE — conflates reading traditions with abrogation.

**Why wrong:** Qirāʾāt are different canonical reading traditions (synchronic
variants). Naskh is diachronic supersession (earlier ruling replaced by later).
They are separate categories.

**Safe formulation:** Qirāʾāt differences are NOT examples of naskh. They are
separate evidence categories.

**Status:** REGISTERED

---

### E4: Naskh = Rasm

**Error:** Assuming naskh affects the consonantal skeleton (rasm).

**Risk:** MODERATE — conflates abrogation with textual base.

**Why wrong:** Rasm is the consonantal skeleton (STEP 104). Naskh is about rulings
or texts being superseded. Naskh does not necessarily alter the rasm.

**Safe formulation:** Naskh and rasm are separate categories. Naskh does not
automatically affect the rasm.

**Status:** REGISTERED

---

### E5: Naskh = Every Contradiction

**Error:** Classifying every apparent contradiction as naskh.

**Risk:** HIGH — inflates naskh counts; ignores reconciliation possibilities.

**Why wrong:** Many apparent contradictions can be reconciled through specification
(takhṣīṣ), qualification (taqyīd), clarification (bayān), or contextual
differentiation. Naskh is a last resort.

**Safe formulation:** Naskh should only be invoked when reconciliation is impossible.
Many apparent contradictions are actually specification, qualification, or
contextual differentiation.

**Status:** REGISTERED

---

### E6: Later Rule = Automatically Proven Later

**Error:** Assuming a ruling is "later" merely because it is claimed to be later.

**Risk:** HIGH — naskh requires reliable chronology.

**Why wrong:** Naskh requires establishing which revelation came first. Chronology
must be proven through reliable evidence (asbāb, ḥadīth, historical context), not
mere assertion.

**Safe formulation:** A valid naskh claim requires reliable chronology established
through source-critical evidence.

**Status:** REGISTERED

---

### E7: Ṣaḥīḥ Ḥadīth = Complete Chronology

**Error:** Assuming a ṣaḥīḥ ḥadīth automatically establishes complete chronology.

**Risk:** MODERATE — conflates authenticity with chronology.

**Why wrong:** Ḥadīth grading (ṣaḥīḥ/ḥasan/ḍaʿīf) measures transmission reliability,
not chronology. A ṣaḥīḥ ḥadīth may not establish which revelation came first.

**Safe formulation:** Ḥadīth authenticity does not automatically establish chronology.
Chronology requires separate evidence.

**Status:** REGISTERED

---

### E8: Asbāb Report = Certain Chronology

**Error:** Assuming an asbāb al-nuzūl report automatically establishes certain
chronology.

**Risk:** MODERATE — ignores asbāb transmission issues.

**Why wrong:** Asbāb reports have their own transmission issues (STEP 109). Multiple
conflicting reports may exist. Source criticism is required.

**Safe formulation:** Asbāb reports can contribute to chronology but require source
criticism. They are not automatically reliable.

**Status:** REGISTERED

---

### E9: Naskh Count = Objective Fixed Number

**Error:** Assuming there is an objective, fixed number of abrogated verses.

**Risk:** MODERATE — ignores methodological variation.

**Why wrong:** Counts vary dramatically (5 to 500+) depending on definition and
methodology. Broader definitions include specification; narrower definitions only
include genuine incompatibility.

**Safe formulation:** Naskh counts depend on definition and methodology. There is
no objective fixed number.

**Status:** REGISTERED

---

### E10: Specification = Always Naskh

**Error:** Classifying specification (takhṣīṣ) as naskh.

**Risk:** HIGH — inflates naskh counts; misclassifies reconciliation as abrogation.

**Why wrong:** Specification narrows a general ruling. Naskh supersedes a ruling.
They are distinct. Shāh Walī Allāh demonstrates most "naskh" cases are actually
specification.

**Safe formulation:** Specification (takhṣīṣ) is NOT naskh. They are distinct
categories.

**Status:** REGISTERED

---

### E11: Naskh = Failure of Preservation

**Error:** Assuming naskh proves Qurʾānic preservation failed.

**Risk:** HIGH — conflates theological concept with textual transmission.

**Why wrong:** Naskh is a theological concept about how revelation addresses changing
circumstances. Preservation is about textual transmission. They are separate
categories.

**Safe formulation:** Naskh does not prove preservation failure. They are separate
categories requiring different evidence.

**Status:** REGISTERED

---

### E12: Naskh Theory = Historical Proof of Manuscript Change

**Error:** Using naskh theory as historical proof that manuscripts were changed.

**Risk:** HIGH — conflates theological doctrine with historical evidence.

**Why wrong:** Naskh theory is based on revelation and legal reasoning. Manuscript
change requires physical evidence (paleography, codicology, textual criticism).
Theological doctrine ≠ historical evidence.

**Safe formulation:** Naskh theory is a theological/legal doctrine. It does not
constitute historical proof of manuscript change.

**Status:** REGISTERED

```
QURʾĀNIC VERSE (7th century)
    ↓

    ↓
TAFSIR WORKS (8th-15th centuries)
    ↓
UṢŪL AL-FIQH (9th-15th centuries)
    ↓
CLASSICAL NASKH COMPILATIONS (10th-16th centuries)
    ↓
MODERN SCHOLARSHIP (19th-21st centuries)
```

### AA.2 Key Dependencies

**Al-Naḥḥās (d. 338/950):**
- Depends on: Earlier tafsir works, ḥadīth collections
- NOT independent witness

**Al-Makkī (d. 437/1045):**
- Depends on: Al-Naḥḥās, earlier tafsir
- NOT independent witness

**Al-Zarkashī (d. 794/1392):**
- Depends on: Earlier works but more critical
- More independent methodology

**Al-Suyūṭī (d. 911/1505):**
- Depends on: Al-Zarkashī, earlier works
- NOT independent witness

**Shāh Walī Allāh (d. 1176/1762):**
- More independent; critical of earlier methodologies
- Re-classifies most cases

### AA.3 Derivative Works

**Do NOT count derivative compilations as independent witnesses.**

- Al-Makkī depends on al-Naḥḥās
- Al-Suyūṭī depends on al-Zarkashī
- Later works often repeat earlier material

**Each source must be evaluated for independence.**

## AB. FINAL SYNTHESIS

### AB.1 Ten Established Principles

1. **Naskh has multiple definitions** — lexical, technical, exegetical, legal
   meanings must be distinguished.

2. **Classical scholars disagreed** about scope, definition, and methodology.

3. **Naskh ≠ textual corruption** — abrogation of ruling does not mean manuscript
   alteration.

4. **Chronology is essential** — valid naskh requires establishing which revelation
   came first.

5. **Asbāb can assist chronology** but requires source criticism (STEP 109).

6. **Ḥadīth can support naskh** but does not automatically prove entire historical
   reconstruction.

7. **Qirāʾāt and manuscript variants** are separate evidence categories (STEP 104).

8. **Numerical counts depend on definitions** — broader definitions inflate counts.

9. **Specification ≠ naskh** — many "naskh" cases are actually specification,
   qualification, or contextual differentiation.

10. **Naskh theory and textual preservation** are analytically distinct categories.

### AB.2 Master Principle

**"Naskh must be analyzed as a graded exegetical, legal, and theological concept
whose lexical meaning, chronology, textual basis, transmission, and juristic
application must be separated before drawing conclusions about revelation, law,
or textual preservation."**

### AB.3 Anti-Overclaim Rules

1. Do NOT assume naskh = textual corruption.
2. Do NOT assume naskh = manuscript alteration.
3. Do NOT assume qirāʾāt differences = naskh.
4. Do NOT assume every contradiction = naskh.
5. Do NOT assume later rule = automatically proven later.
6. Do NOT assume ṣaḥīḥ ḥadīth = complete chronology.
7. Do NOT assume asbāb report = certain chronology.
8. Do NOT assume naskh count = objective fixed number.
9. Do NOT assume specification = naskh.
10. Do NOT assume naskh = failure of preservation.
11. Do NOT use naskh theory as historical proof of manuscript change.
