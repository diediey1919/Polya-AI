# POST-CHALCEDON RECEPTION & ECCLESIASTICAL IDENTITY FORENSIC AUDIT

## 1. CORE QUESTION

**Primary historical question:** How did the decisions and terminology of Chalcedon (451 CE) get received, resisted, negotiated, and interpreted in the century following the council, and how did this process contribute to the formation of distinct Chalcedonian and non-Chalcedonian ecclesiastical identities?

**Critical methodological principle:** Reception is a *process*, not an event. The council's authority was not self-executing; it required imperial enforcement, episcopal subscription, theological integration, liturgical incorporation, and popular acceptance. These dimensions did not always align, and the gap between them reveals the complexity of post-conciliar identity formation.

**Epistemic boundaries:**
- Reception history ≠ theological truth
- Imperial enforcement ≠ proof of correctness
- Resistance ≠ proof of heresy
- Later denominational identity ≠ original position in 451
- Political motivation ≠ absence of theological conviction

**Prohibited reductions:**
- "Chalcedon immediately created two modern churches" — ANACHRONISTIC
- "The conflict was purely political" — REDUCTIONIST
- "The conflict was purely theological" — REDUCTIONIST
- "Non-Chalcedonians were Monophysites" — POLEMICAL (see STEP 84)
- "Chalcedonians were Nestorians" — POLEMICAL (see STEP 84)

## 2. RECEPTION AS PROCESS: METHODOLOGICAL FRAMEWORK

### 2A. Dimensions of Reception

Reception operates across multiple distinct dimensions that do not automatically correlate:

| Dimension | Mechanism | Evidence Type | Timeframe |
|-----------|-----------|---------------|-----------|
| **Conciliar subscription** | Episcopal signatures | ACO signature lists | Immediate (451) |
| **Imperial enforcement** | State coercion | Edicts, legal records | Short-term (451-457) |
| **Episcopal acceptance** | Bishops' formal assent | Letters, synodal acts | Short-term (451-460s) |
| **Local implementation** | Regional church adoption | Local synods, liturgy | Medium-term (451-480s) |
| **Theological integration** | Doctrinal synthesis | Patristic citations, treatises | Long-term (451-553+) |
| **Liturgical reception** | Worship incorporation | Liturgical texts, diptychs | Long-term (451-600+) |
| **Institutional consolidation** | Denominational structures | Separate hierarchies, councils | Very long-term (500s-700s) |
| **Retrospective historiography** | Modern scholarly classification | Academic literature | Contemporary |

**Critical observation:** A council can be:
- Subscribed but not theologically integrated (many bishops in 451)
- Imperially enforced but locally resisted (Chalcedon in Egypt)
- Imperially reversed but theologically persistent (Basiliscus's Enkyklikon)
- Liturgically received while institutionally contested (Constantinople in 480s-510s)

### 2B. Historiographical Controls

Every major conclusion must distinguish:

| Level | Description | Example |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| **Primary evidence** | Contemporary documents, acts, letters | ACO subscription lists, imperial edicts |
| **Early reception** | 5th-6th century interpretations | Timothy Aelurus's letters, Severus's homilies |
| **Later interpretation** | Medieval/modern theological readings | John of Damascus, modern Orthodox theology |
| **Modern historiography** | Contemporary scholarly analysis | Grillmeier, Frend, Meyendorff |

**Methodological rule:** Modern denominational self-description is NOT direct evidence for 5th-century positions. Coptic Orthodox identity in 2026 is not identical to Egyptian Christian identity in 451.

## 3. IMMEDIATE POST-CHALCEDON CONTEXT (451–457)

### 3A. The Council's Decisions

**Subscription:** Approximately 520 bishops attended Chalcedon; the Definition received widespread subscription. However, subscription numbers alone do not equal theological consensus. Many bishops subscribed under imperial pressure or without fully understanding the terminological nuances.

**Dioscorus:** Deposed on procedural grounds (refusal to appear when summoned, not primarily on theology). Died in exile at Gangra (454). The Alexandrian church continued to regard him as the legitimate patriarch.

**Eutyches:** Condemned as heretical. This was NOT controversial — most parties agreed Eutyches had erred in teaching absorption of humanity into divinity.

**Proterius:** Installed as Chalcedonian patriarch of Alexandria (451). Faced immediate popular resistance. Murdered by mob in the baptistery of the church of Quirinus (457).

### 3B. Imperial Enforcement Under Marcian (450–457)

Marcian and Pulcheria enforced Chalcedon through:
- Imperial edicts confirming the council's decisions
- Exile of Dioscorus
- Military enforcement in Egypt
- Pressure on reluctant bishops to subscribe

**Assessment:** Imperial enforcement was real and materially affected reception. However:
- Enforcement ≠ proof of theological truth
- Resistance ≠ proof of theological error
- Political motivation and theological conviction often coexisted

### 3C. Regional Responses (451–457)

**Constantinople:** Generally accepted under imperial pressure. Anatolius (Chalcedonian patriarch) pressed bishops to sign Leo's Tome. The capital's theological establishment was largely Chalcedonian, though popular sentiment was mixed.

**Rome:** Leo I initially welcomed Chalcedon but objected to Canon 28 (elevating Constantinople's status). Roman acceptance was conditional and not uniform. The papacy's relationship with Chalcedon was complex and evolved over decades.

**Alexandria:** MASSIVE resistance. The murder of Proterius (457) demonstrates the depth of opposition. Timothy Aelurus installed as rival patriarch. Egyptian resistance cannot be reduced to:
- Eutychianism (Timothy Aelurus was explicitly anti-Eutychian)
- Political rebellion (theological convictions were genuine)
- Simple misunderstanding (the terminological concerns were substantive)

**Antioch:** Mixed reception. Some bishops accepted; others resisted. The region remained a battleground. The Antiochene theological tradition (Theodore of Mopsuestia, Theodoret, Ibas) had already been controversial, and Chalcedon's reception reopened these debates.

**Palestine:** Monastic communities largely opposed Chalcedon, associating it with Nestorian tendencies. The desert fathers had deep roots in Cyrillian theology.

**Asia Minor:** Generally accepted, with some local resistance.

### 3D. Assessment

The immediate post-Chalcedon period (451-457) reveals:
1. **Subscription was widespread but not uniform** — many signed under pressure
2. **Imperial enforcement was real** — but enforcement ≠ theological consensus
3. **Regional variation was significant** — Alexandria resisted; Constantinople accepted; Rome was conditional
4. **Theological concerns were genuine** — not reducible to politics or misunderstanding
5. **Identity formation had begun** — but modern denominational boundaries did not yet exist

## 4. KEY FIGURES IN POST-CHALCEDON RECEPTION

### 4A. Dioscorus of Alexandria (d. 454)

**Deposition:** Deposed at Chalcedon on procedural grounds (refusal to appear when summoned). The charges were:
- Procedural: Refusal to attend sessions despite three summonses
- Jurisdictional: Actions at Ephesus II (449) deemed excessive
- Theological: Accusations of Eutychianism (disputed)

**Alexandria:** Remained the legitimate patriarch in Egyptian eyes. The Alexandrian church refused to accept Proterius as his successor.

**Theological reputation:**
- **Chalcedonian interpretation:** Dioscorus was a violent, ambitious prelate who supported Eutychianism and abused his authority at Ephesus II (449).
- **Non-Chalcedonian interpretation:** Dioscorus was a faithful successor to Cyril, unjustly deposed by a politically motivated council that betrayed Cyril's theology.
- **Modern historiography:** More nuanced; acknowledges both procedural irregularities and genuine theological controversy.

**Later memory:** Venerated as a saint in the Coptic tradition. His deposition became a symbol of Alexandrian resistance to imperial/Chalcedonian authority.

**Critical assessment:** Dioscorus ≠ Eutyches. Dioscorus opposed Eutychian confusion of natures. However, his reinstatement of Eutyches at Ephesus II (449) was a procedural error that damaged his reputation.

### 4B. Timothy Aelurus ("the Cat") (patriarch 457–477, with interruptions)

**Background:** Priest under Dioscorus; attended Ephesus II (449). Rigorous Cyrillian theologian.

**Installation:** Installed as anti-Chalcedonian patriarch after Proterius's murder (457). Represented the continuation of Dioscorus's theological line.

**Exile and restoration:**
- Exiled multiple times by imperial authorities (460s)
- Restored under Basiliscus (475)
- Died in office (477)

**Theological position:**
- Rigorous opponent of Eutychianism while opposing Chalcedon
- Considered himself faithful to Cyril and Dioscorus
- Insisted on *mia physis tou Theou Logou sesarkōmenē* as the orthodox formula
- Rejected Chalcedon's "in two natures" as a betrayal of Cyril

**Assessment:** Timothy Aelurus demonstrates that anti-Chalcedonian resistance was NOT reducible to Eutychianism. He was explicitly anti-Eutychian while opposing Chalcedon.

### 4C. Proterius (patriarch 451–457)

**Installation:** Installed by imperial authority after Dioscorus's deposition. Chalcedonian figure.

**Reception:** Not accepted by Egyptian populace. Murdered by mob in 457.

**Assessment:** Represents the Chalcedonian minority in Egypt. His violent death demonstrates the depth of Egyptian resistance to Chalcedon.

### 4D. Timothy Salofaciolus ("of the white turban") (Chalcedonian patriarch 460–475, 477–482)

**Background:** Chalcedonian patriarch installed after Timothy Aelurus's exile.

**Actions:** Retired to monastery when Timothy Aelurus returned (475). Represents the Chalcedonian minority in Egypt.

**Assessment:** Demonstrates that there WAS a Chalcedonian community in Egypt, though it was a minority and required imperial support.

## 5. BASILISCUS AND THE ENKYKLIKON (475–476)

### 5A. Political Context

Basiliscus seized power in 475, overthrowing Emperor Zeno. His reign was brief (475-476) but theologically significant.

### 5B. The Enkyklikon (April 9, 475)

**Content:** Imperial edict condemning Chalcedon and Leo's Tome. Affirmed:
- Nicaea (325)
- Constantinople I (381)
- Ephesus (431)
- Cyril's Twelve Anathemas

**Subscription:** Approximately 500 bishops signed, including Timothy Aelurus (restored to Alexandria).

**Opposition:** Patriarch Acacius of Constantinople opposed. Daniel the Stylite led popular resistance.

### 5C. Assessment

**Critical question:** Does the Enkyklikon prove Chalcedon was universally rejected?

**Answer:** NO.

**Evidence:**
- The Enkyklikon was an imperial edict, not a council
- It was reversed when Zeno regained power (476)
- Many bishops who signed later returned to Chalcedonian communion
- Popular resistance in Constantinople (Daniel the Stylite) shows Chalcedon had support

**Historiographical lesson:** Imperial decree ≠ ecclesiastical reception ≠ theological consensus. The Enkyklikon demonstrates that anti-Chalcedonian sentiment was widespread but not universal.

## 6. ZENO AND THE HENOTIKON (482)

### 6A. Political Context

Zeno restored to power (476). Faced ongoing Christological controversy. Sought compromise to restore unity.

### 6B. The Henotikon (482)

**Authorship:** Drafted by Acacius of Constantinople, issued by Zeno.

**Content:** Deliberately avoided explicit Chalcedonian terminology:
- Affirmed Nicaea (325), Constantinople I (381), Ephesus (431)
- Condemned Eutyches and Nestorius
- Affirmed Cyril's Twelve Anathemas
- Did NOT explicitly affirm or condemn Chalcedon
- Did NOT use "in two natures" language

**Purpose:** Reconciliation formula avoiding divisive terminology.

**Reception:**
- **Alexandria:** Accepted by Peter Mongus (anti-Chalcedonian patriarch)
- **Constantinople:** Accepted by Acacius
- **Rome:** Rejected by Pope Felix III (led to Acacian Schism, 484)

### 6C. Assessment

**Critical question:** Does the Henotikon prove Chalcedon was rejected?

**Answer:** NO.

**Evidence:**
- The Henotikon avoided explicit condemnation of Chalcedon
- It was a political compromise, not a theological settlement
- Rome rejected it precisely because it did NOT explicitly affirm Chalcedon
- The Acacian Schism (484-519) shows the Henotikon failed to achieve unity

**Critical question:** Does the Henotikon prove Chalcedon was accepted?

**Answer:** NO.

**Evidence:**
- The Henotikon's avoidance of Chalcedonian terminology suggests Chalcedon was controversial
- Alexandria accepted it, suggesting they preferred ambiguity to explicit Chalcedonian affirmation
- The Henotikon's failure (Acacian Schism) shows it satisfied neither side

**Historiographical lesson:** The Henotikon demonstrates that Chalcedon was controversial but not universally rejected. It was a political attempt to avoid theological precision, and it failed.

## 7. ACACIAN SCHISM (484–519)

### 7A. Origins

Pope Felix III excommunicated Acacius of Constantinople (484) for:
- Accepting the Henotikon (which did not explicitly affirm Chalcedon)
- Communing with Peter Mongus (anti-Chalcedonian patriarch of Alexandria)

Acacius responded by striking Felix from the diptychs (liturgical commemoration).

### 7B. Duration and Dynamics

The schism lasted 35 years (484-519), extending through:
- Zeno (476-491)
- Anastasius I (491-518)

**Anastasius I (491-518):** Miaphysite-leaning emperor. Maintained Henotikon; increasingly favored anti-Chalcedonian position. Deposed Chalcedonian patriarchs; installed Severus of Antioch (512). Trisagion controversy (511-512) led to riots in Constantinople.

### 7C. Resolution

Justin I (518-527) restored Chalcedonian orthodoxy. Acacian Schism resolved (March 24, 519, Easter). Severus and Philoxenus exiled.

### 7D. Assessment

**Critical observation:** The Acacian Schism demonstrates:
1. **Rome's commitment to Chalcedon** — Felix III excommunicated Acacius for not explicitly affirming Chalcedon
2. **Constantinople's pragmatism** — Acacius sought unity through the Henotikon
3. **The limits of imperial compromise** — The Henotikon failed to satisfy either side
4. **The persistence of Chalcedonian identity** — Despite 35 years of schism, Chalcedon was restored in 519

**Historiographical lesson:** The Acacian Schism shows that Chalcedonian identity was already crystallizing by 484. Rome's willingness to break communion over Chalcedon demonstrates that the council had become a marker of orthodoxy in the West.

## 8. JUSTINIAN AND CONSTANTINOPLE II (553)

### 8A. Justinian's Theological Policy

Justinian I (527-565) sought to reconcile Chalcedonians and non-Chalcedonians through:
- Edict against Three Chapters (543/544)
- Condemnation of Theodore of Mopsuestia, Theodoret's anti-Cyril writings, Ibas's letter to Mari

**Purpose:** Demonstrate that Chalcedon was NOT Nestorian by condemning Nestorian sources.

### 8B. Three Chapters Controversy

**The "Three Chapters":**
1. Theodore of Mopsuestia (d. 428) — teacher of Nestorius
2. Theodoret of Cyrrhus's anti-Cyril writings
3. Ibas of Edessa's letter to Mari (criticizing Cyril)

**Justinian's logic:** If these Nestorian sources are condemned, then Chalcedon cannot be accused of Nestorianism.

**Reception:**
- **Non-Chalcedonians:** Saw this as too little, too late; Chalcedon itself remained the problem
- **Western Chalcedonians:** Resisted condemning Theodoret and Ibas (who had been rehabilitated at Chalcedon)
- **Eastern Chalcedonians:** Generally accepted as necessary clarification

### 8C. Constantinople II (553)

**Fifth Ecumenical Council:** Confirmed Three Chapters condemnation. Reaffirmed Cyril's Third Letter and Twelve Anathemas. 152 bishops (overwhelmingly Eastern); Pope Vigilius coerced.

**Critical question:** Did Constantinople II "reverse" Chalcedon?

**Answer:** NO.

**Evidence:**
- Constantinople II explicitly affirmed Chalcedon
- It refined Chalcedonian Christology by emphasizing Cyril's anti-Nestorian concerns
- It condemned Nestorian sources to demonstrate Chalcedon's orthodoxy
- It did NOT reject "in two natures" terminology

**Critical question:** Did Constantinople II prove Chalcedon was always identical to Cyril?

**Answer:** NO.

**Evidence:**
- Constantinople II was a response to non-Chalcedonian criticism
- It emphasized aspects of Chalcedon (Cyril's Twelve Anathemas) that had been controversial
- It demonstrates that Chalcedon's reception was ongoing and contested
- It shows that later councils could reinterpret earlier councils

**Historiographical lesson:** Constantinople II demonstrates that Chalcedon's reception was a process, not an event. The council of 553 reinterpreted Chalcedon in light of Cyril's Twelve Anathemas, showing that conciliar authority could be refined and clarified over time.

### 8D. Conciliar Sequence

The relationship between councils:

**Nicaea (325)** → **Constantinople I (381)** → **Ephesus (431)** → **Chalcedon (451)** → **Constantinople II (553)**

Each council interpreted its predecessors:
- Constantinople I interpreted Nicaea
- Ephesus interpreted Constantinople I and Nicaea
- Chalcedon interpreted Ephesus (and Cyril's Second Letter)
- Constantinople II interpreted Chalcedon (and Cyril's Third Letter)

**Critical observation:** Later councils do not "reverse" earlier councils; they reinterpret them in light of new controversies. This is a normal feature of conciliar tradition.

## 10. SEVERUS OF ANTIOCH (512–518)

### 10A. Background

Severus of Antioch (c. 465-538) was a key Miaphysite theologian:
- Patriarch of Antioch (512-518)
- Rigorous Cyrillian Christology
- Ordained by twelve bishops including Philoxenus of Mabbug
- Opposed Flavian II; demanded Chalcedon anathematization

### 10B. Theological Position

**Miaphysite theology:**
- One incarnate nature of God the Word (*mia physis tou Theou Logou sesarkōmenē*)
- Explicitly anti-Eutychian (rejected absorption of humanity)
- Insisted on complete humanity (rational soul, body)
- Rejected Chalcedon's "in two natures" as Nestorian

**Relationship to Cyril:** Severus saw himself as Cyril's faithful interpreter. He insisted that Chalcedon had betrayed Cyril's theology.

**Relationship to Eutyches:** Severus explicitly condemned Eutyches. He was NOT a Monophysite in the Eutychian sense.

### 10C. Reception

Severus's patriarchal tenure (512-518) demonstrates:
- Anti-Chalcedonian sentiment was strong in Antioch
- Imperial support (Anastasius I) enabled his installation
- His exile (518) demonstrates the fragility of anti-Chalcedonian power
- His theological influence persisted in Syriac Christianity

### 10D. Assessment

Severus demonstrates that anti-Chalcedonian theology was:
- NOT Eutychian (explicitly anti-Eutychian)
- NOT politically motivated (genuine theological conviction)
- NOT a simple misunderstanding (sophisticated theological argument)
- Rooted in Cyrillian tradition (faithful to Cyril's formula)

**Historiographical lesson:** Severus of Antioch is a key figure in Miaphysite identity formation. His theology demonstrates that non-Chalcedonian Christology was a legitimate interpretation of Cyril, not a heretical deviation.

## 11. MIAPHYSITE IDENTITY FORMATION

### 11A. Process, Not Event

Miaphysite identity did not emerge fully formed in 451. It developed over the 5th-7th centuries through:
- Theological articulation (Severus, Philoxenus, Timothy Aelurus)
- Institutional separation (separate patriarchates, hierarchies)
- Liturgical development (distinct rites, diptychs)
- Political consolidation (Persian vs. Byzantine alignment)
- Later self-identification (Oriental Orthodox communion)

### 11B. Terminology

**Preferred self-designation:**
- Miaphysite (one united nature from two)
- Oriental Orthodox (Eastern Orthodox of the Orient)
- Non-Chalcedonian (neutral descriptor)

**Rejected labels:**
- Monophysite (polemical; implies Eutychianism)
- Eutychian (inaccurate; OO explicitly reject Eutyches)

### 11C. Assessment

**Critical observation:** Modern Oriental Orthodox identity is NOT identical to 5th-century anti-Chalcedonian identity. The Oriental Orthodox communion (Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian, Eritrean, Malankara) developed over centuries and includes theological, liturgical, and institutional developments that postdate the 5th century.

**Historiographical lesson:** Do not project modern denominational boundaries backward into 451. The process of identity formation was gradual and complex.

## 12. TERMINOLOGY: MIAPHYSITE, MONOPHYSITE, NON-CHALCEDONIAN, ORIENTAL ORTHODOX, EUTYCHIAN

### 12A. Miaphysite

**Etymology:** From *mia physis* (one nature)

**Historical usage:** 5th-6th century theological term for those who affirmed *mia physis tou Theou Logou sesarkōmenē* while rejecting Eutychianism.

**Modern usage:** Preferred scholarly term for non-Chalcedonian Christology. Self-designation of Oriental Orthodox churches.

**Assessment:** Accurate and respectful. Use this term.

### 12B. Monophysite

**Etymology:** From *monos* (alone, only) + *physis* (nature) = "one nature only"

**Historical usage:** Polemical term used by Chalcedonians to accuse opponents of Eutychianism.

**Modern usage:** Outdated scholarly term; rejected by Oriental Orthodox.

**Assessment:** POLEMICAL. Do not use without qualification. Implies Eutychianism, which is inaccurate.

### 12C. Non-Chalcedonian

**Etymology:** Descriptive term for those who did not accept Chalcedon.

**Historical usage:** Modern scholarly term.

**Modern usage:** Neutral descriptor.

**Assessment:** Accurate and neutral. Use this term when Miaphysite is not appropriate.

### 12D. Oriental Orthodox

**Etymology:** "Eastern Orthodox of the Orient"

**Historical usage:** Modern term for the communion of non-Chalcedonian churches (Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian, Eritrean, Malankara).

**Modern usage:** Self-designation of these churches.

**Assessment:** Accurate for modern churches. Do not use for 5th-century figures (anachronistic).

### 12E. Eutychian

**Etymology:** From Eutyches of Constantinople (c. 378-454).

**Historical usage:** Term for those who taught absorption of humanity into divinity.

**Modern usage:** Accurate for Eutyches and his followers. Inaccurate for Miaphysites.

**Assessment:** Use ONLY for Eutyches and those who explicitly taught absorption. Do NOT use for Miaphysites or Oriental Orthodox.

## 14. CLAIM TESTS (A–O)

### Claim A
**Classification:** UNSAFE

**Finding:** Chalcedon immediately created two modern churches (Chalcedonian and Oriental Orthodox).

**Evidence:** Modern denominational identities did not exist in 451. The process of identity formation took over a century. Many bishops who resisted Chalcedon in 451 later returned to Chalcedonian communion. Separate hierarchies did not crystallize until the 6th-7th centuries.

**Reason:** Anachronistic projection of modern denominational boundaries onto 5th-century reality.

---

### Claim B
**Classification:** UNSAFE

**Finding:** The post-Chalcedonian conflict was purely political (imperial enforcement vs. local resistance).

**Evidence:** Theological concerns were genuine. Timothy Aelurus, Severus of Antioch, and other Miaphysite leaders were rigorous theologians with sophisticated arguments. The conflict involved genuine theological disagreement over terminology (*mia physis* vs. *dyo physeis*).

**Reason:** Reductionist. Political and theological factors coexisted. Reducing the conflict to politics ignores genuine theological conviction.

---

### Claim C
**Classification:** UNSAFE

**Finding:** The post-Chalcedonian conflict was purely theological (terminology and doctrine).

**Evidence:** Imperial enforcement, ecclesiastical jurisdiction, and local politics played significant roles. The deposition of Dioscorus involved procedural and jurisdictional issues. The murder of Proterius involved popular resistance to imperial imposition. The Acacian Schism involved ecclesiastical authority (Rome vs. Constantinople).

**Reason:** Reductionist. Political, ecclesiastical, and theological factors were intertwined.

---

### Claim D
**Classification:** UNSAFE

**Finding:** Non-Chalcedonians were Monophysites (Eutychians who taught absorption of humanity).

**Evidence:** Miaphysite theologians (Timothy Aelurus, Severus of Antioch) explicitly rejected Eutychianism. They insisted on complete humanity (rational soul, body). They affirmed *mia physis tou Theou Logou sesarkōmenē* (one INCARNATE nature), which explicitly affirms the incarnation. (See STEP 84)

**Reason:** Polemical conflation of Miaphysitism with Eutychianism. Inaccurate and disrespectful.

---

### Claim E
**Classification:** UNSAFE

**Finding:** Chalcedonians were Nestorians (who divided Christ into two persons).

**Evidence:** Chalcedon explicitly affirmed "one person and one hypostasis" and condemned Nestorius. The Definition insists on the unity of Christ while distinguishing the natures. (See STEP 84)

**Reason:** Polemical conflation of Chalcedonian dyophysitism with Nestorianism. Inaccurate.

---

### Claim F
**Classification:** SAFE WITH QUALIFICATION

**Finding:** Dioscorus was unjustly deposed at Chalcedon.

**Evidence:** Dioscorus was deposed on procedural grounds (refusal to appear when summoned), not primarily on theology. The Alexandrian church regarded him as the legitimate patriarch. Modern historiography acknowledges procedural irregularities.

**Reason:** Qualified. The deposition involved procedural irregularities, but Dioscorus's actions at Ephesus II (449) (reinstating Eutyches) were also problematic. The assessment depends on whether one emphasizes procedural fairness or theological substance.

---

### Claim G
**Classification:** UNSAFE

**Finding:** The Henotikon (482) proves Chalcedon was universally rejected.

**Evidence:** The Henotikon avoided explicit condemnation of Chalcedon. Rome rejected it precisely because it did NOT explicitly affirm Chalcedon. The Acacian Schism (484-519) shows the Henotikon failed to achieve unity.

**Reason:** The Henotikon demonstrates Chalcedon was controversial but not universally rejected. It was a political compromise that satisfied neither side.

---

### Claim H
**Classification:** UNSAFE

**Finding:** The Henotikon (482) proves Chalcedon was universally accepted.

**Evidence:** The Henotikon's avoidance of Chalcedonian terminology suggests Chalcedon was controversial. Alexandria accepted it, suggesting they preferred ambiguity to explicit Chalcedonian affirmation.

**Reason:** The Henotikon demonstrates Chalcedon was controversial. Its failure (Acacian Schism) shows it satisfied neither side.

---

### Claim I
**Classification:** UNSAFE

**Finding:** Constantinople II (553) reversed Chalcedon.

**Evidence:** Constantinople II explicitly affirmed Chalcedon. It refined Chalcedonian Christology by emphasizing Cyril's anti-Nestorian concerns. It condemned Nestorian sources to demonstrate Chalcedon's orthodoxy. It did NOT reject "in two natures" terminology.

**Reason:** Constantinople II reinterpreted Chalcedon in light of Cyril's Twelve Anathemas, but did not reverse it. This is a normal feature of conciliar tradition (later councils interpret earlier councils).

---

### Claim J
**Classification:** UNSAFE

**Finding:** Constantinople II (553) proves Chalcedon was always identical to Cyril's theology.

**Evidence:** Constantinople II was a response to non-Chalcedonian criticism. It emphasized aspects of Chalcedon (Cyril's Twelve Anathemas) that had been controversial. It demonstrates that Chalcedon's reception was ongoing and contested.

**Reason:** Constantinople II shows that Chalcedon's reception was a process. The council of 553 reinterpreted Chalcedon in light of new controversies, showing that conciliar authority could be refined over time.

---

### Claim K
**Classification:** SAFE

**Finding:** Miaphysite theology is NOT Eutychian.

**Evidence:** Miaphysite theologians (Timothy Aelurus, Severus of Antioch) explicitly rejected Eutychianism. They insisted on complete humanity. They affirmed *mia physis tou Theou Logou sesarkōmenē* (one INCARNATE nature). (See STEP 84)

**Reason:** Miaphysitism and Eutychianism are distinct theological positions. Conflating them is inaccurate and polemical.

---

### Claim L
**Classification:** SAFE WITH QUALIFICATION

**Finding:** Severus of Antioch was a faithful interpreter of Cyril.

**Evidence:** Severus insisted on *mia physis tou Theou Logou sesarkōmenē* as Cyril's formula. He explicitly rejected Eutychianism. He saw himself as Cyril's faithful successor.

**Reason:** Qualified. Severus's interpretation of Cyril is one legitimate reading, but not the only one. Chalcedonians also claimed to be faithful to Cyril (via the Second Letter and the Formula of Reunion 433). The question of who is the "true" interpreter of Cyril is theologically contested.

---

### Claim M
**Classification:** SAFE

**Finding:** Reception of Chalcedon was a process, not an event.

**Evidence:** The reception matrix demonstrates that imperial decree, episcopal subscription, theological integration, and popular acceptance did not always align. The process took over a century (451-553+) and involved multiple reversals (Basiliscus's Enkyklikon, Henotikon, Acacian Schism) and reinterpretations (Constantinople II).

**Reason:** Historical evidence supports this. Reception is a complex, multi-dimensional process.

---

### Claim N
**Classification:** SAFE

**Finding:** Imperial enforcement materially affected reception but did not equal theological consensus.

**Evidence:** Marcian and Pulcheria enforced Chalcedon through edicts and military pressure. Basiliscus reversed Chalcedon through the Enkyklikon. Zeno attempted compromise through the Henotikon. Justinian sought reconciliation through Constantinople II. Imperial policy shifted multiple times, but theological disagreement persisted.

**Reason:** Imperial enforcement can compel subscription but cannot create theological consensus. The persistence of Miaphysite theology despite imperial pressure demonstrates this.

### Claim O
**Classification:** SAFE

**Finding:** Modern Oriental Orthodox identity is not identical to 5th-century anti-Chalcedonian identity.

**Evidence:** The Oriental Orthodox communion (Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian, Eritrean, Malankara) developed over centuries. It includes theological, liturgical, and institutional developments that postdate the 5th century. Modern denominational boundaries did not exist in 451.

**Reason:** Anachronistic projection of modern denominational identity onto 5th-century reality is methodologically unsound.

## 15. STEELMAN ARGUMENTS

### Side A: Chalcedonian Continuity Argument

**Strongest case:**

Chalcedon preserved Cyril's central Christological concerns while introducing necessary precision:

1. **One person:** Chalcedon affirms "one person and hypostasis" (Cyril's central concern).
2. **Full divinity:** Chalcedon affirms "consubstantial with the Father" (Cyril's concern).
3. **Full humanity:** Chalcedon affirms "consubstantial with us" (Cyril's concern).
4. **Distinction preserved:** Chalcedon affirms "without confusion, without change" (Cyril's Second Letter: "not as if the difference of the natures were taken away").
5. **Theotokos:** Chalcedon affirms Theotokos (Cyril's boundary term).
6. **Cyril approved:** Chalcedon read and approved Cyril's Second Letter.
7. **Against Nestorius:** Chalcedon condemns division ("without division, without separation").
8. **Against Eutyches:** Chalcedon condemns confusion ("without confusion, without change").
9. **Later councils clarified:** Constantinople II (553) reaffirmed Cyril's Twelve Anathemas, demonstrating Chalcedon's compatibility with Cyril.
10. **Non-Chalcedonian separation involved multiple factors:** Political, ecclesiastical, and theological factors were intertwined. The separation was not purely theological.

**Conclusion:** Chalcedon is a legitimate clarification that protects both Cyril's unity and the distinction of natures. Later councils (Constantinople II) refined Chalcedon in light of Cyril's anti-Nestorian concerns, demonstrating continuity rather than contradiction.

---

### Side B: Non-Chalcedonian Critique

**Strongest case:**

Chalcedon introduced changes that created genuine tensions with Cyril:

1. **Terminological shift:** Cyril used *mia physis* (one nature). Chalcedon uses *dyo physeis* (two natures). This is a significant terminological change.
2. **Conceptual precision:** Chalcedon distinguishes *hypostasis* (person) from *physis* (nature) with Cappadocian precision. Cyril used these terms more loosely. This is a conceptual development not identical to Cyril.
3. **Semantic framework:** Cyril's *physis* meant "concrete individual reality." Chalcedon's *physis* means "abstract nature." This is a semantic shift.
4. **Emphasis:** Cyril emphasized unity (*mia physis*). Chalcedon emphasizes distinction (four adverbs, especially "without confusion, without change"). This creates tension.
5. **Leo's Western vocabulary:** Leo's Tome uses Latin terminology (*persona*, *natura*) that reflects Western conceptual categories. This may not align perfectly with Cyril's Greek framework.
6. **Reception:** Oriental Orthodox churches rejected Chalcedon, claiming it contradicted Cyril. This is historical evidence of perceived contradiction.
7. **Ecclesiastical consequences:** Chalcedon led to schism with Oriental Orthodox churches. This suggests substantive disagreement, not mere terminology.
8. **Later reconciliation attempts:** The Henotikon (482) and Constantinople II (553) demonstrate that Chalcedon's reception was contested and required clarification. If Chalcedon were simply identical to Cyril, these efforts would be unnecessary.
9. **Imperial politics:** Imperial enforcement (Marcian, Justinian) and ecclesiastical politics (Acacian Schism) played significant roles in Chalcedon's reception. This suggests the council's authority was not self-evident.
10. **Miaphysite theology is legitimate:** Severus of Antioch and other Miaphysite theologians provided sophisticated interpretations of Cyril that are theologically coherent and explicitly anti-Eutychian.

**Conclusion:** Chalcedon changes terminology and emphasis in ways that create genuine tensions with Cyril's *mia physis* language. The post-Chalcedonian controversy (Henotikon, Acacian Schism, Constantinople II) demonstrates that the council's reception was contested and required ongoing clarification. Miaphysite theology is a legitimate interpretation of Cyril, not a heretical deviation.

## 16. HISTORIOGRAPHICAL CONTROLS

### 16A. Primary Evidence vs. Later Interpretation

Every major conclusion must distinguish:

| Level | Description | Example |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| **Primary evidence** | Contemporary documents, acts, letters | ACO subscription lists, imperial edicts, Severus's homilies |
| **Early reception** | 5th-6th century interpretations | Timothy Aelurus's letters, John of Damascus |
| **Later interpretation** | Medieval/modern theological readings | Modern Orthodox theology, ecumenical dialogues |
| **Modern historiography** | Contemporary scholarly analysis | Grillmeier, Frend, Meyendorff |

**Methodological rule:** Modern denominational self-description is NOT direct evidence for 5th-century positions. Coptic Orthodox identity in 2026 is not identical to Egyptian Christian identity in 451.

### 16B. Anachronism Controls

**Do not project:**
- Modern Oriental Orthodox identity backward into 451
- Modern Chalcedonian identity backward into 451
- Modern denominational boundaries onto 5th-century actors
- Modern theological categories onto 5th-century controversies

**Do acknowledge:**
- 5th-century actors operated within their own theological and ecclesiastical frameworks
- Identity formation was a gradual process
- Later developments (liturgical, institutional, theological) postdate the 5th century

## 18. FINAL SYNTHESIS

### 18A. Core Question: Did Chalcedon Immediately Create Two Modern Churches?

**Answer:** NO.

**Evidence:** Modern denominational identities (Oriental Orthodox, Eastern Orthodox, Roman Catholic) did not exist in 451. The process of identity formation took over a century and involved:
- Theological articulation (Severus, Philoxenus, Timothy Aelurus)
- Institutional separation (separate patriarchates, hierarchies)
- Liturgical development (distinct rites, diptychs)
- Political consolidation (Persian vs. Byzantine alignment)
- Later self-identification (Oriental Orthodox communion)

**Conclusion:** Historical separation was a process rather than a single instantaneous event.

---

### 18B. Was the Conflict After 451 Only About Terminology?

**Answer:** NO.

**Evidence:** The conflict involved:
- **Terminology:** *Mia physis* vs. *dyo physeis* (genuine terminological disagreement)
- **Theology:** Emphasis on unity vs. distinction (genuine theological disagreement)
- **Politics:** Imperial enforcement, ecclesiastical jurisdiction, local resistance
- **Ecclesiastical authority:** Rome vs. Constantinople, Alexandria vs. imperial center

**Conclusion:** Terminology was significant but not the only factor. Theological, political, and ecclesiastical factors were intertwined.

---

### 18C. How Much Did Theology Matter?

**Answer:** SIGNIFICANTLY.

**Evidence:** Miaphysite theologians (Timothy Aelurus, Severus of Antioch) were rigorous theologians with sophisticated arguments. They insisted on *mia physis tou Theou Logou sesarkōmenē* as Cyril's formula. They explicitly rejected Eutychianism. Their theological convictions were genuine and not reducible to politics.

**Conclusion:** Theological concerns were genuine and played a significant role in the post-Chalcedonian controversy.

---

### 18D. How Much Did Imperial Politics Matter?

**Answer:** SIGNIFICANTLY.

**Evidence:** Imperial enforcement (Marcian, Justinian), imperial reversals (Basiliscus's Enkyklikon), and imperial compromises (Zeno's Henotikon) materially affected reception. Political alignment (Persian vs. Byzantine) influenced Armenian reception. Ecclesiastical politics (Acacian Schism) shaped East-West relations.

**Conclusion:** Imperial politics played a significant role but did not determine theological conviction. Political motivation and theological conviction often coexisted.

---

### 18E. How Did Ecclesiastical Jurisdiction Matter?

**Answer:** SIGNIFICANTLY.

**Evidence:** The deposition of Dioscorus involved procedural and jurisdictional issues. The murder of Proterius involved popular resistance to imperial imposition. The Acacian Schism involved ecclesiastical authority (Rome vs. Constantinople). Separate patriarchates (Timothy Aelurus vs. Timothy Salofaciolus) institutionalized the division.

**Conclusion:** Ecclesiastical jurisdiction played a significant role in identity formation.

---

### 18F. When Did Non-Chalcedonian Identity Begin to Consolidate?

**Answer:** GRADUALLY OVER THE 6TH-7TH CENTURIES.

**Evidence:** Separate patriarchates emerged in the 5th-6th centuries (Timothy Aelurus in Alexandria, Severus in Antioch). Institutional separation (separate hierarchies, liturgies, diptychs) developed over the 6th-7th centuries. Modern Oriental Orthodox communion (Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian, Eritrean, Malankara) developed over centuries and includes later developments.

**Conclusion:** Non-Chalcedonian identity consolidated gradually over the 6th-7th centuries, not immediately in 451.

---

### 18G. What Can and Cannot Be Established from Stored Evidence?

- Reception was a process, not an event (reception matrix)
