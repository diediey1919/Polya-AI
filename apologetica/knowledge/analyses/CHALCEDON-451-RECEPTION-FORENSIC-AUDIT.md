# CHALCEDON 451 RECEPTION FORENSIC AUDIT

## 1. SCOPE & PURPOSE

This audit is dedicated to the RECEPTION of the Council of Chalcedon (451 CE). STEP 74 audited the Definition itself. STEP 82 audited the bridge from Ephesus 431 through Reunion 433 to Chalcedon 451. This audit addresses what happened AFTER 451: how the council was received, resisted, enforced, reinterpreted, and ultimately institutionalized across different ecclesiastical traditions.

**Epistemic boundary:**
- Reception history ≠ theological truth
- Political enforcement ≠ proof of correctness
- Resistance ≠ proof of heresy
- Later denominational identity ≠ original position

**Prohibited reductions:**
- "Chalcedon was orthodox and opponents were heretics" — REDUCTIONIST
- "Chalcedon was politically imposed and therefore false" — REDUCTIONIST
- "Rejection of Chalcedon means rejection of Nicene Christianity" — UNSAFE
- "Acceptance of Chalcedon proves theological truth" — UNSAFE

---

## 2. DEFINITION OF "RECEPTION"

Reception is NOT a single event. It operates across multiple distinct dimensions:

| Dimension | Description | Evidential Basis |
|-----------|-------------|------------------|
| **Attendance** | Physical presence at the council | Conciliar acts, subscription lists |
| **Participation** | Active involvement in debates | Conciliar acts |
| **Subscription** | Formal signing of the Definition | Signature lists (ACO) |
| **Episcopal acceptance** | A bishop's formal assent | Letters, synodal acts |
| **Local acceptance** | A local church's implementation | Regional synodal acts, liturgical evidence |
| **Imperial enforcement** | State coercion of compliance | Imperial edicts, legal records |
| **Liturgical reception** | Incorporation into worship | Liturgical texts, diptychs |
| **Theological reception** | Integration into theological discourse | Patristic citations, treatises |
| **Institutional reception** | Long-term ecclesiastical identity | Denominational structures, later councils |
| **Retrospective historiography** | Modern scholarly classification | Academic literature |

**Critical observation:** These dimensions do not always align. A council can be imperially enforced but theologically resisted (Chalcedon in Egypt), imperially reversed but theologically persistent (Basiliscus's Enkyklikon), or liturgically received while institutionally contested (Constantinople).

## 4. IMMEDIATE POST-CHALCEDON CONTEXT (451–457)

### 4A. The Council's Decisions

**Subscription:** Approximately 520 bishops attended; the Definition received widespread subscription. However, subscription numbers alone do not equal theological consensus.

**Dioscorus:** Deposed on procedural grounds (refusal to appear when summoned, not primarily on theology). Died in exile at Gangra (454). The Alexandrian church continued to regard him as de jure patriarch.

**Eutyches:** Condemned as heretical. This was NOT controversial — most parties agreed Eutyches had erred.

**Proterius:** Installed as Chalcedonian patriarch of Alexandria (451). Faced immediate popular resistance. Murdered by mob in the baptistery of the church of Quirinus (457).

### 4B. Imperial Enforcement Under Marcian (450–457)

Marcian and Pulcheria enforced Chalcedon through:
- Imperial edicts confirming the council's decisions
- Exile of Dioscorus
- Military enforcement in Egypt
- Pressure on reluctant bishops to subscribe

**Assessment:** Imperial enforcement was real and materially affected reception. However, enforcement ≠ proof of theological truth, and resistance ≠ proof of theological error.

### 4C. Regional Responses (451–457)

**Constantinople:** Generally accepted under imperial pressure. Anatolius (Chalcedonian patriarch) pressed bishops to sign Leo's Tome.

**Rome:** Leo I initially welcomed Chalcedon but objected to Canon 28 (elevating Constantinople). Roman acceptance was conditional and not uniform.

**Alexandria:** MASSIVE resistance. The murder of Proterius (457) demonstrates the depth of opposition. Timothy Aelurus installed as rival patriarch.

**Antioch:** Mixed reception. Some bishops accepted; others resisted. The region remained a battleground.

**Palestine:** Monastic communities largely opposed Chalcedon, associating it with Nestorian tendencies.

**Asia Minor:** Generally accepted, with some local resistance.

## 5. ALEXANDRIA AND EGYPT

### 5A. Why Egypt Resisted

**Theological factors:**
- Cyril of Alexandria's formula "μία φύσις τοῦ θεοῦ λόγου σεσαρκωμένη" (one incarnate nature of God the Word) was deeply embedded in Alexandrian theology
- Chalcedon's "ἐν δύο φύσεσιν" (in two natures) was perceived as a betrayal of Cyril
- The deposition of Dioscorus was seen as unjust (he was considered the rightful patriarch)
- Leo's Tome was perceived as Nestorianizing

**Ecclesiastical factors:**
- Dioscorus remained the de jure patriarch in Egyptian eyes
- The installation of Proterius (and later Timothy Salofaciolus) was seen as imperial imposition
- Alexandrian theological tradition had deep roots and was not easily displaced

**Political factors:**
- Egypt had a history of ecclesiastical independence from Constantinople
- The Alexandrian populace and monastic communities strongly identified with Cyril and Dioscorus
- Imperial enforcement was resented as foreign domination

### 5B. Key Figures

**Dioscorus (d. 454):**
- Deposed at Chalcedon on procedural grounds
- Died in exile at Gangra
- Remained the legitimate patriarch in Egyptian eyes
- Later venerated as a saint in the Coptic tradition
- NOT the same as Eutyches — Dioscorus opposed Eutychian confusion of natures

**Timothy Aelurus ("the Cat") (patriarch 457–477, with interruptions):**
- Priest under Dioscorus; attended Ephesus II (449)
- Installed as anti-Chalcedonian patriarch after Proterius's murder (457)
- Exiled multiple times by imperial authorities
- Restored under Basiliscus (475)
- Rigorous opponent of Eutychianism while opposing Chalcedon
- Considered himself faithful to Cyril and Dioscorus

**Proterius (patriarch 451–457):**
- Installed by imperial authority after Dioscorus's deposition
- Murdered by mob in 457
- Chalcedonian figure; not accepted by Egyptian populace

**Timothy Salofaciolus ("of the white turban") (Chalcedonian patriarch 460–475, 477–482):**
- Chalcedonian patriarch installed after Timothy Aelurus's exile
- Retired to monastery when Timothy Aelurus returned (475)
- Represents the Chalcedonian minority in Egypt

### 5C. Assessment

Egyptian resistance to Chalcedon CANNOT be reduced to:
- Eutychianism (Timothy Aelurus was explicitly anti-Eutychian)
- Political rebellion (theological convictions were genuine)
- Simple misunderstanding (the terminological concerns were substantive)

Egyptian resistance CANNOT be automatically validated as:
- Theologically correct (reception history ≠ theological truth)
- Exclusively representative of Cyril (both sides appealed to Cyril)

## 6. SYRIA AND ANTIOCH

### 6A. Antiochene Reception

Syria was deeply divided. The Antiochene theological tradition had its own internal diversity:

**Pro-Chalcedonian:**
- Some bishops accepted the Definition
- Flavian II (patriarch 498–512) accepted Chalcedon alongside Cyril's formula (proto-neo-Chalcedonianism)
- Imperial pressure supported Chalcedonian position

**Anti-Chalcedonian:**
- Philoxenus of Mabbug (bishop 485–523): Rigorous Miaphysite theologian
- Peter the Fuller (patriarch of Antioch): Anti-Chalcedonian; introduced Trisagion addition
- Later: Severus of Antioch (patriarch 512–518): Key Miaphysite theologian
- Monastic communities largely opposed Chalcedon

### 6B. Key Figures

**Philoxenus of Mabbug (d. 523):**
- Consecrated bishop of Mabbug (Hierapolis) by Peter the Fuller (485)
- Supported Henotikon; visited Constantinople (484) to offer Miaphysite profession
- Produced Philoxenian version of the NT (508)
- Bitter opponent of Flavian II
- Instrumental in Flavian's deposition and Severus's installation (512)
- Exiled by Justin I (519); died in exile (523)
- Theologically rigorous: Miaphysite Christology rooted in Cyril, NOT Eutychianism

**Flavian II (patriarch 498–512):**
- Accepted Chalcedon alongside Cyril's one-nature formula
- Adhered to Henotikon
- Refused to anathematize Chalcedon (Philoxenus's demand)
- Deposed (512) under Anastasius I

**Severus of Antioch (patriarch 512–518):**
- Born c. 465 in Sozopolis, Pisidia; pagan family
- Baptized 488 at Tripoli; became monk in Palestine
- Consecrated patriarch November 16, 512 by twelve bishops (including Philoxenus)
- Rigorous Miaphysite Christology: one composite physis from two, without confusion
- Opposed Eutychianism explicitly
- Deposed by Justin I (518); fled to Egypt
- Died c. 538 in Egypt
- Later venerated in Oriental Orthodox tradition

### 6C. Assessment

Syrian reception was NOT uniform. The region produced both strong Chalcedonian and strong anti-Chalcedonian figures. Equating "Antiochene" with "Nestorian" is UNSAFE — Philoxenus and Severus were rigorously anti-Nestorian while being anti-Chalcedonian.

## 7. ARMENIA

### 7A. Why Armenia Was Not at Chalcedon

**CRITICAL CHRONOLOGICAL FACT:** The Armenian Church was NOT present at Chalcedon (451). This was NOT a simple matter of geographical distance. In May 451 — the same year as Chalcedon — the Battle of Avarayr was fought between Armenian forces (under Vartan Mamikonian) and the Sasanian Persian army. Armenia was in the midst of an existential military crisis against Zoroastrian Persia. Sending bishops to a council in Chalcedonia was impossible.

**Absence ≠ rejection:** The absence itself was not initially a theological statement. However, the reports that reached Armenia about Chalcedon were hostile to it.

### 7B. First Council of Dvin (506)

**Convened by:** Catholicos Babken I of the Armenian Church
**Participants:** ~20 bishops + 14 laymen + Armenian princes; Georgian and Caucasian Albanian delegates present
**Decision:**
- Accepted the Henotikon of Emperor Zeno (482)
- Reaffirmed the Christology of Cyril of Alexandria and the Council of Ephesus
- Condemned Nestorian teachers whose theology they believed Chalcedon had rehabilitated
- Did NOT formally and explicitly anathematize the Council of Chalcedon itself

**Interpretive dispute:** Some scholars (Karekin Sarkissian) see Dvin I as "the first official and formal rejection of the Council of Chalcedon by the Armenian Church." Others note the council stopped short of explicit Chalcedonian rejection, accepting the Henotikon and keeping open the possibility of conciliation with Constantinople.

### 7C. Second Council of Dvin (554/555)

The FORMAL and EXPLICIT rejection of the Chalcedonian Definition of the dual nature of Christ did not occur until the Second Council of Dvin (554/555). This is the council that formalized the Armenian break from Chalcedonian communion.

### 7D. Assessment

**The claim "Armenia rejected Chalcedon in 451" is CHRONOLOGICALLY UNSAFE.** Armenia was not present in 451 and did not formally reject Chalcedon until 554/555. The process was gradual:
1. 451: Absence (military necessity)
2. 506: Acceptance of Henotikon; rejection of Nestorianism (not yet explicit Chalcedonian rejection)
3. 554/555: Formal rejection of Chalcedon's dyophysite definition

Armenian non-reception was influenced by:
- Military isolation from Byzantine ecclesiastical politics
- Genuine theological concerns (alignment with Cyril)
- Anti-Nestorian conviction
- Persian pressure (Sasanian Zoroastrianism)
- Regional identity distinct from Constantinople

## 8. CONSTANTINOPLE

### 8A. Imperial Capital: Varied Positions

Constantinople was the center of imperial power and the see of the patriarch most affected by imperial policy. Positions varied across patriarchs:

**Anatolius (449–458):** Initially installed with Dioscorus's support; shifted to Chalcedonian position under Marcian.

**Gennadius I (458–471):** Pro-Chalcedonian.

**Acacius (471–489):** Most controversial. Initially supported Chalcedon and opposed Basiliscus (475). Later drafted the Henotikon (482). Excommunicated by Pope Felix III (484). Died 489.

**Euphemius (490–496):** Strongly Chalcedonian. Refused to omit Acacius from diptychs. Deposed.

**Macedonius II (496–511):** Initially Chalcedonian; eventually pressured toward Henotikon compliance.

**Timothy I (511–518):** Miaphysite-leaning under Anastasius I.

### 8B. The Constantinopolitan Populace

The Constantinopolitan populace was NOT uniformly aligned with imperial policy:
- Under Basiliscus (475): Acacius and Daniel the Stylite led popular opposition to the Enkyklikon
- Under Anastasius (511–512): Riots over the Trisagion addition; populace supported Chalcedonian patriarch Macedonius
- Popular sentiment often favored Chalcedonian orthodoxy even when emperors leaned Miaphysite

### 8C. Assessment

Constantinople's position was NOT a single permanent viewpoint. It shifted with imperial policy, patriarchal appointments, and popular pressure. Reducing Constantinople to either "Chalcedonian" or "anti-Chalcedonian" as a fixed identity is UNSAFE for the period 451–553.

## 9. ROME

### 9A. Leo I and Chalcedon

**Leo's Tome (449):** Written before Chalcedon; read and acclaimed at the council. Leo's two-nature language (Latin: *naturae*; Greek translation: *physeis*) became central to the Chalcedonian Definition.

**Leo's response to Chalcedon:** Initially welcomed the council's Christological decisions. Objected strongly to Canon 28 (elevating Constantinople's status over older sees). This objection was about ecclesiastical jurisdiction, not Christology.

### 9B. Post-Chalcedonian Roman Involvement

**Pope Simplicius (468–483):** Attempted to maintain Chalcedonian orthodoxy. Communicated with Acacius.

**Pope Felix III (483–492):**
- Sent legates to Constantinople demanding Acacius answer charges
- Legates imprisoned and coerced into communicating with Acacius and Peter Mongus
- Excommunicated Acacius (484): precipitated the Acacian Schism
- Refused any compromise on Chalcedon

**Pope Gelasius (492–496):** Refused any compromise as a betrayal of Chalcedon.

**Pope Hormisdas (514–523):** Negotiated the end of the Acacian Schism (519). Required Constantinople to condemn Acacius and accept Chalcedon fully.

### 9C. Assessment

Rome was consistently Chalcedonian throughout this period. However, Roman involvement does NOT prove later developed doctrines of papal supremacy. The Roman see's authority was contested by Constantinople, and the Acacian Schism demonstrates that Rome's excommunications were not automatically effective in the East. Retrojecting Vatican I definitions into fifth-century events is UNSAFE.

## 10. BASILISCUS AND THE ENKYKLIKON (475–476)

### 10A. Historical Context

Basiliscus seized the throne from Emperor Zeno in January 475. His wife Zenonis may have been Miaphysite and may have influenced his religious policies.

### 10B. The Enkyklikon (April 9, 475)

**Content:**
- Promoted the first three ecumenical councils: Nicaea, Constantinople, Ephesus
- CONDEMNED the Council of Chalcedon
- CONDEMNED Leo's Tome
- Required bishops to sign or face removal

**Reception:**
- ~500 bishops signed
- Timothy Aelurus restored to Alexandria
- Peter the Fuller restored to Antioch

**Opposition:**
- Patriarch Acacius of Constantinople strongly opposed
- Daniel the Stylite led popular resistance
- Constantinopolitan populace turned against Basiliscus

### 10C. Assessment

**Imperial decree ≠ theological consensus.** The Enkyklikon demonstrates that:
- Imperial power could temporarily reverse ecclesiastical policy
- But such reversals were unstable without popular and theological support
- The failure of the Enkyklikon shows that Chalcedonian identity had already become rooted in significant portions of the church by 475

## 11. ZENO AND THE HENOTIKON (482)

### 11A. Historical Context

Zeno regained the throne in 476. The Christological divisions remained severe. The Henotikon was drafted by Patriarch Acacius at Zeno's request.

### 11B. The Henotikon (482)

**Content:**
- Insisted on the faith of Nicaea (325), Constantinople (381), and Ephesus (431)
- Condemned Eutyches and Nestorius
- Explicitly approved the Twelve Anathemas of Cyril
- Affirmed Christ as both God and man, "one, not two"
- DELIBERATELY AVOIDED any definitive statement on whether Christ had one or two natures
- Avoided explicit endorsement of Chalcedon's "two natures" terminology
- Avoided explicit endorsement of "one nature" terminology

**Strategy:** The Henotikon attempted to achieve unity by:
- Agreeing on what all parties could accept (Nicaea, Constantinople, Ephesus, condemnation of Eutyches and Nestorius)
- Remaining SILENT on the disputed point (one vs. two natures)
- Using Cyril's authority (Twelve Anathemas explicitly approved)

### 11C. Reception

**Moderate Miaphysites:** Temporarily accepted. Peter Mongus (Alexandria) accepted.
**Strict Miaphysites:** Rejected. Some became "Akephaloi" (headless ones).
**Strict Chalcedonians:** Rejected. Saw the omission of Chalcedon's two-nature language as betrayal.
**Rome:** Vehemently opposed. Pope Felix III excommunicated Acacius (484).

### 11D. Assessment

**Henotikon ≠ Chalcedonian Definition.** The Henotikon was a compromise document that deliberately avoided the central Christological question. Its failure demonstrates that:
- Top-down theological compromise has limits
- Both sides had developed strong identities that resisted ambiguity
- Silence on disputed points was not sufficient for reconciliation

## 12. ACACIAN SCHISM (484–519)

### 12A. Timeline

- **482:** Henotikon issued
- **483:** Pope Felix III sends legates to Constantinople
- **484:** Legates imprisoned and coerced into communicating with Acacius and Peter Mongus
- **484:** Felix III convenes synod, excommunicates Acacius
- **484:** Acacius strikes Felix from diptychs
- **489:** Acacius dies; successor Flavitas tries to reconcile but refuses to omit Acacius from diptychs
- **491:** Zeno dies; Anastasius I succeeds
- **492–496:** Pope Gelasius refuses compromise
- **519:** Justin I resolves schism under Pope Hormisdas (March 24, Easter)

### 12B. Significance

The Acacian Schism matters for reception history because:
- It demonstrates Rome's insistence on Chalcedonian orthodoxy
- It shows Constantinople's willingness to compromise (Henotikon) vs. Rome's refusal
- It foreshadows the later East-West Schism (1054)
- It demonstrates that reception was not merely East vs. West but involved complex intra-Eastern dynamics
- It shows that imperial attempts at unity could produce further division

### 12C. Resolution

Resolved in 519 when Emperor Justin I:
- Recognized the excommunication of Acacius
- Accepted Rome's conditions (Formula of Hormisdas)
- Reunited Chalcedonian churches

However, the Alexandrian and Antiochene patriarchs still embraced Miaphysitism. The schism between Chalcedonian and Oriental Orthodox traditions was NOT resolved.

## 13. ANASTASIUS I (491–518)

### 13A. Imperial Policy

Anastasius I was sympathetic to Miaphysitism despite initial professions of Chalcedonian orthodoxy. His policies included:
- Maintaining the Henotikon
- Deposing Chalcedonian patriarchs
- Installing Miaphysite leaders (Severus of Antioch, 512)
- Increasingly favoring anti-Chalcedonian positions

### 13B. Trisagion Controversy (511–512)

Peter the Fuller had introduced the addition "who was crucified for us" to the Trisagion hymn. This was interpreted as implying that the divine nature suffered. Anastasius supported this addition, leading to riots in Constantinople. The populace supported the Chalcedonian patriarch Macedonius.

### 13C. Assessment

Anastasius's reign demonstrates that:
- Imperial Miaphysite leanings could materially affect ecclesiastical appointments
- But popular Chalcedonian sentiment in Constantinople was strong
- The emperor's position was weaker than commonly supposed; he relied on intrigue to expel patriarchs

## 14. SEVERUS OF ANTIOCH (PATRIARCH 512–518)

### 14A. Biography

- Born c. 465 in Sozopolis, Pisidia (pagan family)
- Baptized 488 at Tripoli
- Monk in Palestine; studied in Alexandria
- Consecrated patriarch of Antioch November 16, 512 by twelve bishops including Philoxenus of Mabbug
- Deposed by Justin I (518); fled to Egypt; died c. 538

### 14B. Christology

Severus's Christology represents the most rigorous Miaphysite formulation:

**Core formula:** One composite physis of the incarnate Word — from two natures, without confusion or separation.

**Key commitments:**
- Rigorously anti-Eutychian (humanity fully intact)
- Rigorously anti-Nestorian (one subject, not two)
- Rooted in Cyril of Alexandria
- Insisted that Chalcedon's "two natures" language implies division
- Maintained that after the union, there is one nature (not two)

**Relationship to Cyril:** Severus considered himself the faithful interpreter of Cyril. His Christology was NOT a corruption of Cyril but a rigorous application.

**Relationship to Eutychianism:** Severus EXPLICITLY opposed Eutychian confusion/absorption of natures. Equating Severus with Eutychianism is UNSAFE.

### 14C. Assessment

Severus represents the intellectual apex of anti-Chalcedonian Christology. His work demonstrates that:
- Anti-Chalcedonian theology was NOT merely reactive or politically motivated
- Rigorous Miaphysite Christology has genuine theological content
- The dispute was about terminological frameworks, not merely political power
- Both Chalcedonian and anti-Chalcedonian traditions could claim continuity with Cyril

## 15. PHILOXENUS OF MABBUG

### 15A. Biography

- Born 440–455 in Tahal, Beth Garmai (east of Tigris, modern Iraq)
- Educated at Edessa (possibly School of the Persians)
- Consecrated bishop of Mabbug (Hierapolis) by Peter the Fuller (485)
- Produced Philoxenian version of NT (508)
- Instrumental in Flavian II's deposition (512)
- Exiled by Justin I (519); died in exile (523)

### 15B. Christology

Philoxenus was a rigorous Miaphysite theologian:
- Rooted in Cyril of Alexandria
- Anti-Eutychian
- Insisted on one composite nature from two, without confusion
- Polemics aimed to demonstrate "how the logic of Chalcedonian doctrine cuts off access to the incarnate Christ"
- Integrated Syriac and Greek theological traditions (Ephrem, Cappadocians, Alexandrians)

### 15C. Relationship to Severus

Philoxenus and Severus were allies in the anti-Chalcedonian movement:
- Philoxenus participated in Severus's consecration (512)
- Both exiled by Justin I
- Both rooted in Cyrilline Christology
- Both rigorously anti-Eutychian

## 16. MIAPHYSITE VS MONOPHYSITE: TERMINOLOGY AUDIT

### 16A. Definitions

| Term | Greek | Meaning | Assessment |
|------|-------|---------|------------|
| **Miaphysite** | μία φύσις (mia physis) | One composite nature from two, without confusion or separation | Used for Oriental Orthodox Christology (Cyril, Severus, Philoxenus) |
| **Monophysite** | μία φύσις (mia physis) | One nature (humanity absorbed/diminished by divinity) | Used for Eutychianism; polemically applied to all anti-Chalcedonians |
| **Dyophysite** | δύο φύσεις (dyo physeis) | Two natures (divine and human) | Used for Chalcedonian Christology |
| **Eutychianism** | — | One nature after union; humanity absorbed by divinity | Condemned at Chalcedon; NOT the same as Miaphysitism |

### 16B. Claim Tests

**"Miaphysite = Monophysite"**
**Classification:** UNSAFE

Evidence: Miaphysite Christology explicitly maintains the full integrity of both divinity and humanity in one composite nature. Eutychian Monophysitism absorbs/diminishes humanity. Severus and Philoxenus explicitly opposed Eutychianism.

**"Oriental Orthodox = Eutychian"**
**Classification:** UNSAFE

Evidence: Oriental Orthodox churches (Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian, Eritrean, Malankara) explicitly reject Eutychianism. Their Christology is Miaphysite, rooted in Cyril. Calling them "Monophysite" without qualification is imprecise and polemical.

**"Chalcedonian = Nestorian"**
**Classification:** UNSAFE

Evidence: Chalcedon explicitly condemned Nestorius and anathematized division into two persons. Chalcedonian Christology affirms one hypostasis/prosopon. The anti-Chalcedonian perception that Chalcedon is Nestorianizing is a theological interpretation, not an established historical fact.

### 16C. Historiographical Caution

**"Monophysite" as a neutral label:** UNSAFE without qualification. It conflates Eutychianism with Miaphysitism and carries polemical baggage. Modern scholarship (Winkler 1997 and later) prefers "Miaphysite" or "non-Chalcedonian."

**"Oriental Orthodox" as a collective designation:** Safe as a MODERN scholarly label for the post-Chalcedonian non-Chalcedonian churches. However, projecting modern institutional structures backward into 451 is UNSAFE.

## 17. CHALCEDONIAN TRADITIONS

### 17A. Byzantine/Eastern Orthodox

The Byzantine tradition inherited Chalcedon as normative. Later developments:
- Constantinople II (553): Refined Chalcedonian Christology; condemned Three Chapters
- Constantinople III (680–681): Defined two wills and two energies in Christ
- John of Damascus (c. 675–749): Systematized Chalcedonian theology

### 17B. Roman Catholic

The Roman tradition maintained Chalcedon with additional developments:
- Leo's Tome given equal authority with Cyril's letters
- Later papal claims to universal jurisdiction (not present in 451)
- Western Christological tradition influenced by Latin terminology

### 17C. Later Protestant

Protestant traditions generally inherited Chalcedon through the Roman/Byzantine mediation. Chalcedonian Christology became the default position of most Protestant denominations.

### 17D. Assessment

**Projecting modern denominational structures backward into 451 is UNSAFE.** The Byzantine, Roman, and later Protestant traditions all developed Chalcedonian Christology in different ways. The "Chalcedonian tradition" is not a monolithic entity.

## 18. CYRIL'S RECEPTION AFTER 451

### 18A. Both Sides Appealed to Cyril

**Chalcedonian appeal:**
- Cyril's Second Letter to Nestorius approved at Chalcedon
- Formula of Reunion (433) read and affirmed
- Cyril interpreted as compatible with two-nature language

**Anti-Chalcedonian appeal:**
- Cyril's Third Letter with Twelve Anathemas
- Formula "mia physis tou Theou Logou sesarkomene"
- Cyril interpreted as incompatible with two-nature language

### 18B. Assessment

**"Cyril belongs exclusively to Chalcedonian tradition":** UNSAFE
**"Cyril belongs exclusively to anti-Chalcedonian tradition":** UNSAFE

Both traditions had legitimate (though different) grounds for appealing to Cyril. The reception of Cyril was itself contested. Chalcedon approved specific Cyrilline texts (Second Letter, Formula of Reunion) while Constantinople II (553) approved others (Third Letter, Twelve Anathemas). The anti-Chalcedonian tradition appealed to texts Chalcedon did not read.

## 21. CONSTANTINOPLE II (553) AND CHALCEDONIAN RECEPTION

### 21A. Historical Context

Constantinople II was convoked by Justinian I to address the Three Chapters controversy:
1. Theodore of Mopsuestia (person and all writings)
2. Theodoret of Cyrrhus (anti-Cyrillian writings)
3. Ibas of Edessa (letter to Mari the Persian)

These figures/writings had been implicitly rehabilitated at Chalcedon (451) when Theodoret and Ibas were restored to communion.

### 21B. Decisions

**Condemned:**
- Theodore of Mopsuestia (posthumous anathema — unprecedented)
- Theodoret's anti-Cyrillian writings
- Ibas's letter to Mari the Persian

**Reaffirmed:**
- Cyril's Third Letter and Twelve Anathemas
- Chalcedonian Christology (with new precision)
- One hypostasis, two natures (but with emphasis on unity)

**Did NOT:**
- Reverse Chalcedon
- Reject two-nature language
- Condemn Chalcedon itself

### 21C. Assessment

**"Constantinople II simply reversed Chalcedon":** UNSAFE
Constantinople II refined Chalcedonian Christology and condemned Nestorian-tainted elements that Chalcedon had tolerated. It did NOT reverse Chalcedon's fundamental structure.

**"Constantinople II must be understood within Chalcedonian reception history":** SAFE
Constantinople II represents a stage in the reception and clarification of Chalcedon. It addressed tensions within the Chalcedonian tradition (the "Three Chapters") without overturning the council's core decisions.

**Relationship to Twelve Anathemas:**
Constantinople II explicitly reaffirmed Cyril's Third Letter and Twelve Anathemas. This is the most explicit conciliar reception of these texts. Per the five-category framework (STEP 80R):
- (A) Textual presence: Present from 431 onward
- (B) Reading: Likely read at Ephesus 431; not read as independent document at Chalcedon 451
- (C) Theological endorsement: Implicit at Ephesus 431; explicit at Constantinople II 553
- (D) Formal conciliar approval: Disputed at Ephesus 431; NOT at Chalcedon 451; EXPLICIT at Constantinople II 553
- (E) Later conciliar reception: Constantinople II 553 provides the definitive explicit reception

## 23. CLAIM CLASSIFICATIONS (A–R)

### A. "Chalcedon was immediately and universally accepted."
**Classification:** UNSAFE

Evidence: Massive resistance in Egypt (Proterius murdered, 457; Timothy Aelurus installed). Resistance in Syria, Palestine, and elsewhere. Imperial enforcement required.

### B. "Chalcedon was immediately and universally rejected."
**Classification:** UNSAFE

Evidence: ~500 bishops subscribed. Accepted in Constantinople (under pressure), Rome, Asia Minor. Imperial enforcement succeeded in many regions.

### C. "Chalcedon produced substantial regional resistance."
**Classification:** SAFE

Evidence: Egypt, Syria, Palestine, Armenia (later), Ethiopia. The resistance was theologically motivated, not merely political.

### D. "Egyptian resistance can be reduced to Eutychianism."
**Classification:** UNSAFE

Evidence: Timothy Aelurus was explicitly anti-Eutychian. Egyptian resistance was rooted in Cyrilline Christology, not Eutychianism.

### E. "Armenian non-reception occurred exactly in 451."
**Classification:** UNSAFE

Evidence: Armenia was absent from Chalcedon (Battle of Avarayr, 451). First Council of Dvin (506) accepted Henotikon but did not formally reject Chalcedon. Second Council of Dvin (554/555) formally rejected Chalcedon.

### F. "Miaphysite means Monophysite."
**Classification:** UNSAFE

Evidence: Miaphysite Christology explicitly maintains full humanity and divinity in one composite nature. Eutychian Monophysitism absorbs humanity. They are NOT identical.

### G. "Oriental Orthodox means Eutychian."
**Classification:** UNSAFE

Evidence: Oriental Orthodox churches explicitly reject Eutychianism. Their Christology is Miaphysite (Cyril, Severus, Philoxenus).

### H. "Chalcedonian means Nestorian."
**Classification:** UNSAFE

Evidence: Chalcedon explicitly condemned Nestorius and division into two persons. Anti-Chalcedonian perception of Nestorianizing tendency is a theological interpretation, not established fact.

### I. "Imperial policy materially affected reception."
**Classification:** SAFE

Evidence: Imperial enforcement under Marcian, reversal under Basiliscus, Henotikon under Zeno, Miaphysite leanings under Anastasius, restoration under Justin I. Imperial policy clearly affected ecclesiastical outcomes.

### J. "Imperial policy alone explains the controversy."
**Classification:** UNSAFE

Evidence: Theological convictions were genuine on both sides. Popular resistance to both Chalcedon and anti-Chalcedonian imperial policies shows that theology was not merely a function of imperial preference.

### K. "Rome accepted Chalcedon."
**Classification:** SAFE WITH QUALIFICATION

Evidence: Leo I accepted Chalcedon's Christology but objected to Canon 28. Subsequent popes (Felix III, Gelasius, Hormisdas) consistently defended Chalcedon. However, Roman acceptance was conditional and not uniform throughout the period.

### L. "Roman involvement proves later papal supremacy."
**Classification:** UNSAFE

Evidence: Rome's excommunications were not automatically effective in the East (Acacian Schism). The Roman see's authority was contested by Constantinople. Vatican I definitions (1870) cannot be retrojected into fifth-century events.

### M. "Cyril was used as an authority by both sides."
**Classification:** SAFE

Evidence: Chalcedonians cited Cyril's Second Letter and Formula of Reunion. Anti-Chalcedonians cited Cyril's Third Letter and Twelve Anathemas. Both had legitimate (though different) grounds for appealing to Cyril.

### N. "Chalcedon and Cyril are necessarily contradictory."
**Classification:** NOT ESTABLISHED

Evidence: Chalcedon approved Cyrilline texts. The dispute was about terminological frameworks (mia physis vs. dyo physeis), not about fundamental Christological commitments. Constantinople II (553) attempted reconciliation. The relationship is disputed, not necessarily contradictory.

### O. "Constantinople II simply reversed Chalcedon."
**Classification:** UNSAFE

Evidence: Constantinople II refined Chalcedonian Christology and condemned Nestorian-tainted elements. It reaffirmed Chalcedon's fundamental structure. It did NOT reverse Chalcedon.

### P. "Constantinople II must be understood within Chalcedonian reception history."
**Classification:** SAFE

Evidence: Constantinople II addressed tensions WITHIN the Chalcedonian tradition (Three Chapters). It represents a stage in the reception and clarification of Chalcedon.

### Q. "Reception history is distinct from determining theological truth."
**Classification:** SAFE

Evidence: Reception history describes what happened. Theological truth is a separate question. A council can be received or rejected for reasons that do not determine its truth value.

### R. "Later denominational identities should not automatically be projected backward into 451."
**Classification:** SAFE

Evidence: Modern denominational structures (Coptic Orthodox, Syriac Orthodox, Armenian Apostolic, Eastern Orthodox, Roman Catholic, Protestant) developed over centuries. The ecclesiastical landscape of 451 was fluid and contested.

## 24. STEELMAN ARGUMENTS

### Steelman A — Chalcedonian Continuity

**Position:** Chalcedon was a legitimate clarification of Christological boundaries, preserving Cyril's anti-Nestorian concern while protecting the full integrity of Christ's humanity and divinity.

**Strengths:**
- Chalcedon explicitly condemned both Nestorianism and Eutychianism
- Chalcedon approved Cyrilline texts (Second Letter, Formula of Reunion)
- The four adverbs (without confusion, change, division, separation) provide genuine theological precision
- Constantinople II (553) refined rather than reversed Chalcedon
- Chalcedonian Christology became the dominant tradition (Eastern Orthodox, Roman Catholic, Protestant)

**Limitations:**
- The "two natures" language was perceived by some as Nestorianizing
- Imperial enforcement was real and materially affected reception
- The anti-Chalcedonian tradition had genuine theological concerns
- Chalcedon did not read Cyril's Third Letter and Twelve Anathemas as an independent document

### Steelman B — Miaphysite / Non-Chalcedonian Critique

**Position:** Chalcedon's "two natures" formulation introduced a terminology that endangered Cyril's emphasis on the unity of the incarnate Christ, and political enforcement intensified resistance.

**Strengths:**
- Cyril's formula "mia physis tou Theou Logou sesarkomene" is genuinely different from "en dyo physesin"
- The terminological shift from "one incarnate nature" to "in two natures" is real
- Imperial enforcement was real and resented
- Anti-Chalcedonian theology (Severus, Philoxenus) is rigorous and intellectually substantive
- Oriental Orthodox churches maintain genuine Christological convictions, not merely political resistance

**Limitations:**
- Chalcedon explicitly condemned Nestorianism
- The anti-Chalcedonian tradition sometimes conflated Chalcedonian Christology with Nestorianism
- The Miaphysite tradition also developed over time
- Both sides appealed to Cyril with legitimate (though different) grounds

### Assessment

Both steelman positions have genuine merit. The evidence supports a complex picture:
- Chalcedon was a legitimate conciliar development
- But its reception was contested and materially affected by politics
- Anti-Chalcedonian theology was intellectually rigorous
- But its perception of Chalcedon as Nestorian was a theological interpretation, not established fact
- The dispute was about terminological frameworks, not merely political power

**Theological truth is NOT determined by reception history alone.** Both traditions have continued for over 1,500 years. Both have produced saints, theologians, and spiritual traditions. Neither can be dismissed as merely political or merely heretical.

## 25. LIMITATIONS

1. **Incomplete surviving evidence:** Much of the anti-Chalcedonian literature was suppressed or lost. Our knowledge of Miaphysite theology is mediated through Chalcedonian sources in many cases.

2. **Mediated transmission:** The acts of councils are conciliar redactions, not verbatim records. Letters are preserved selectively.

3. **Terminology translation problems:** Greek physis/hypostasis/prosopon/ousia do not have one-to-one English equivalents. Syriac and Coptic terminology adds further complexity.

4. **Polemical sources:** Much of the surviving literature is polemical. Distinguishing genuine theological positions from polemical caricatures requires careful analysis.

5. **Retrospective interpretations:** Later denominational identities shape how we read the historical record. Projecting modern categories backward is a constant risk.

6. **Regional reception differences:** Reception varied enormously by region. Generalizing about "the church" or "Christianity" in this period is dangerous.

7. **Theological presuppositions of modern historians:** Modern scholars bring their own theological commitments to the study of reception history. Complete neutrality is impossible.

8. **Chronological precision:** Some dates and events are disputed in the scholarly literature. This audit uses the most widely accepted chronology but acknowledges uncertainty where it exists.
