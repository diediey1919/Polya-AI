# STEP 111: Western Academic & Modern Critical Perspectives on Qur'anic Chronology and Makki/Madani Classification

## 1. Theodor Nöldeke's Chronological System (Geschichte des Qorans, 1860)

### Core Methodology
- **Publication**: *Geschichte des Qorans* (History of the Qur'an), first edition 1860; revised second edition 1909–1938 (with Friedrich Schwally and Gotthelf Bergsträsser)
- **Criteria for chronology** (four principal indicators):
  1. **Style**: Short, rhythmic, impassioned prose with intense eschatological imagery in early surahs vs. longer, more prosaic, legalistic passages in later ones
  2. **Vocabulary**: Shift from vivid, concrete imagery to abstract theological terminology; presence of specific terms (e.g., *al-Raḥmān* as a divine name peaks in middle Meccan period, then declines)
  3. **Themes**: Early surahs focus on eschatology, monotheism, prophetic stories; later surahs shift to legislation, community-building, polemics with People of the Book
  4. **Surah length**: General trend from shorter surahs (early Meccan) to progressively longer ones (Medinan)
- **Three-period scheme**: Early Meccan, Middle Meccan, Late Meccan, and Medinan (four periods total in his final system)
- **Chronological ordering**: Provided a complete chronological ordering of all 114 surahs, departing significantly from the canonical muṣḥaf arrangement

### Influence and Legacy
- Nöldeke's framework became the **foundational reference** for virtually all subsequent Western academic study of Qur'anic chronology
- His approach represented the first systematic application of historical-critical literary analysis to the Qur'an
- The Corpus Coranicum project at the Berlin-Brandenburg Academy explicitly builds on Nöldeke's analytical approach
- His surah ordering, while debated, remains the baseline against which alternative chronologies are measured

### Limitations
- Relied heavily on internal textual analysis without access to manuscript evidence (pre-dating modern codicology)
- Assumed progressive stylistic development that some scholars question as overly linear
- His chronological ordering of some specific surahs remains contested
- Did not adequately account for the possibility of composite surahs containing material from different periods
- The style-based criteria involve subjective judgments about literary development

## 2. Friedrich Schwally's Revisions to Nöldeke

### Key Contributions
- Schwally revised and expanded Nöldeke's work for the **second edition** of *Geschichte des Qorans* (1909–1919), volumes 1–2
- He refined the chronological criteria, adding more attention to:
  - **Formulaic expressions**: tracking recurring liturgical formulas (e.g., basmala, oaths, doxologies) across surahs
  - **Verse-ending rhyme patterns** (fawāṣil): more systematic analysis of rhyme changes within surahs as indicators of compositional layers
  - **Historical allusions**: connecting specific passages to events in Muhammad's life with greater precision
- Schwally maintained Nöldeke's basic chronological framework but adjusted the placement of several surahs
- He paid closer attention to the **oral-liturgical character** of the proclamations, recognizing performance context

### Scholarly Assessment
- Schwally's revisions are generally seen as improvements that made Nöldeke's system more empirically grounded
- His work on verse-ending rhymes anticipated later structural analysis (Neuwirth, Cuypers)
- The Schwally-Nöldeke system remained the standard reference until the mid-20th century

## 3. Gustav Weil's Earlier Chronological Work

### Pioneering Contribution
- **Key work**: *Historisch-kritische Einleitung in den Koran* (Historical-Critical Introduction to the Qur'an), 1844
- Weil was the **first Western scholar** to attempt a systematic chronological ordering of the Qur'anic surahs
- He divided surahs into three Meccan periods and one Medinan period, anticipating Nöldeke's scheme
- His criteria included:
  - Stylistic features (rhythm, vocabulary, imagery)
  - Thematic content (eschatological vs. legislative)
  - References to historical events
- Weil's work directly influenced Nöldeke, who acknowledged his debt to Weil's pioneering effort

### Limitations and Assessment
- Weil's work was less methodologically rigorous than Nöldeke's subsequent analysis
- He lacked access to many manuscripts and comparative Semitic materials that later scholars used
- His chronological ordering has been largely superseded by Nöldeke's more detailed analysis
- However, his basic insight — that internal literary analysis could yield a reliable relative chronology — proved foundational

## 4. Richard Bell's Introduction to the Qur'an (1953) and Commentary

### Composite Surah Theory
- **Key work**: *Introduction to the Qur'ān* (1953); *A Commentary on the Qur'ān* (2 vols., 1991, posthumously edited by W. Montgomery Watt)
- Bell proposed that most surahs are **composite texts**, assembled from originally independent passages revealed at different times
- His famous **"scissors-and-paste" hypothesis**: the canonical surahs were compiled by cutting and joining disparate passages, often without regard for literary coherence
- He argued that the apparent incoherence within surahs (shifts in topic, addressee, tone, rhyme) results from this editorial process rather than from the original proclamation context

### Revisionist Approach
- Bell was more radical than Nöldeke in questioning surah unity
- He assigned individual passages (not whole surahs) to different periods, producing a more fragmented chronological picture
- His commentary attempts to identify the specific period of each passage within a surah
- Bell also questioned the reliability of the traditional sīra (biography) as a chronological framework

### Influence and Limitations
- Bell's work influenced later scholars who emphasized redaction criticism (Dye, Reynolds)
- However, his extreme fragmentation has been challenged by scholars (Neuwirth, Cuypers) who demonstrate structural coherence through Semitic rhetoric analysis
- His "scissors-and-paste" model is now seen by many as overstated; the Qur'an exhibits more internal coherence than Bell recognized
- His methodology was criticized for circularity: passages that didn't fit his chronological scheme were declared "later insertions"

## 5. Régis Blachère's Le Coran (1949–1950)

### Chronological Ordering and Translation
- **Key work**: *Le Coran* (2 vols., 1949–1950), a French translation arranged in chronological order according to Nöldeke's system
- Blachère presented the Qur'an to Western readers in the order of revelation rather than the canonical muṣḥaf order
- His chronological ordering largely followed Nöldeke with some modifications

### Literary Analysis
- Blachère emphasized the **literary and rhetorical character** of the Qur'an
- He analyzed surah structure, identifying compositional units and transitions
- He paid attention to shifts in register, tone, and addressee within surahs
- His work bridged the gap between pure chronology and literary criticism

### Assessment
- Blachère's translation made the chronological approach accessible to a broader scholarly audience
- His literary analysis anticipated later structural studies
- However, he generally accepted the composite surah hypothesis without the radical fragmentation of Bell
- His work remains an important reference in French-language Qur'anic scholarship

## 6. Angelika Neuwirth's Liturgical/Structural Approach

### Core Methodology
- **Key works**: *Der Koran als Text der Spätantike* (2010); *Scripture, Poetry, and the Making of a Community* (2014); numerous articles
- Neuwirth approaches the Qur'an as a **liturgical text** with deep connections to Late Antique Jewish and Christian worship traditions
- She argues for a **dialogic model**: the Qur'anic text emerged from intense theological debate with the Prophet's audience, not from monologic authorship
- Her analysis emphasizes the **oral-performative** character of the proclamations

### Formulaic Analysis
- Neuwirth traces recurring formulas (e.g., basmala, oath clusters, doxologies, prophetic narratives) across surahs
- She identifies **liturgical forms** characteristic of different periods:
  - **Early Meccan**: psalm-like hymns, eschatological warnings, oath clusters
  - **Middle/Late Meccan**: extended prophetic narratives, covenant theology
  - **Medinan**: communal legislation, liturgical prayer forms, polemics with People of the Book
- Her micro-structural analysis of rhyme, verse structure, and formulaic patterns provides detailed evidence for compositional layers

### Corpus Coranicum Project
- Neuwirth directed the **Corpus Coranicum** project at the Berlin-Brandenburg Academy of Sciences (2007–2024)
- The project's commentary follows a structure: (1) textual/literary analysis, (2) structural unity or disruption, (3) rhyme functionality, (4) verse numbering traditions across schools

### Assessment
- Neuwirth's work represents the most sophisticated development of Nöldeke's approach
- Her liturgical model provides a functional explanation for the Qur'an's formal features
- Her emphasis on Late Antique context has been influential but also contested by scholars who emphasize the Qur'an's originality
- Some critics note that her analysis relies heavily on internal textual criteria and could benefit from more engagement with material evidence

## 7. Nicolai Sinai's Work

### Key Contributions
- **Key works**: *Fortschreibung und Auslegung* (2009); *The Qur'an: A Historical-Critical Introduction* (2017); *Key Terms of the Qur'an: A Critical Dictionary* (2023)
- Sinai is Professor of Islamic Studies at the University of Oxford
- He builds on and refines Nöldeke's chronological framework through:
  - **Inner-Qur'anic chronology**: using cross-references between surahs to establish relative dating
  - **Linguistic criteria**: tracking the evolution of key terms (*āyah*, *kitāb*, *ittaqa*, *allāh*, *al-Raḥmān*) across the corpus
  - **Fortschreibung** (progressive writing): the concept that later Qur'anic passages interpret, expand, and respond to earlier ones

### Chronological Refinement
- Sinai endorses the Nöldeke-Sinai principle that **mean verse length increases** over the course of revelation
- He identifies a "synoptic interpretive habitus": new revelation is best understood within the horizon of already-circulating revelations
- His analysis of dietary prohibition passages (Q 2:172–173, 5:3, 6:145, 16:114–115) demonstrates how chronological analysis reveals theological development
- He argues for **ʿUthmānic codification** of the consonantal skeleton (*rasm*) by mid-7th century, supported by three evidence streams: Islamic historiography, internal textual analysis, and early manuscript/epigraphic data

### Assessment
- Sinai's work represents the current state-of-the-art in historical-critical Qur'anic chronology
- His *Key Terms* dictionary (2023) provides the most comprehensive philological tool for chronological analysis
- His approach balances internal textual analysis with material evidence
- He has engaged directly with revisionist critics (Shoemaker, Dye) while defending early codification

## 8. Corpus Coranicum (Berlin-Brandenburg Academy)

### Methodology
- **Project period**: 2007–2024
- **Directors**: Angelika Neuwirth (founding director), later Dirk Hartwig
- **Core team**: Michael Marx, Nicolai Sinai, David Kiltz, Yousef Kouriyhe
- The project treats the Qur'an as "a text from Late Antiquity" within its broader religious and cultural context

### Modules
1. **Manuscripta Coranica**: Digital archive of early Qur'anic manuscripts with metadata (verse ranges, script type, provenance)
2. **Variae Lectiones Coranicae**: Database of variant readings (qirāʾāt) at the word level, comparing canonical and non-canonical traditions
3. **Texte aus der Umwelt des Korans** ("Texts from the Environment of the Qur'an"): Collection of Jewish, Christian, and other Late Antique texts relevant to Qur'anic intertextuality
4. **Historical-critical commentary**: Surah-by-surah commentary integrating textual, structural, and contextual analysis

### Chronological Framework
- The commentary follows Nöldeke's chronological ordering, with refinements based on Neuwirth's liturgical analysis
- Each surah's commentary includes: structural analysis, rhyme scheme, formulaic patterns, relationship to earlier/later surahs, textual variants
- The project has produced commentaries on early Meccan, mid-Meccan, and late mid-Meccan surahs (as of project conclusion)

### Technical Infrastructure
- SQL databases for manuscript metadata and variant readings
- XML-based commentary
- Custom "Coranica" font for multilingual display (Ancient South Arabian, Arabic, Greek, Hebrew)
- All content is open access
- Integration of the **Gotthelf-Bergsträsser Archive** (photographs of early Qur'anic manuscripts collected in the 1920s–30s)

## 9. Radiocarbon (C14) Dating of Qur'anic Manuscripts

### Birmingham (Mingana 1572a)
- **Date**: 568–645 CE (95.4% confidence / 2σ); radiocarbon measurement: 1465 ± 21 BP (OxA-29418)
- **Laboratory**: Oxford Radiocarbon Accelerator Unit (2015)
- **Content**: Parts of Surahs 18–20, written in Hijazi script
- **Significance**: The early end of the range predates Muhammad's traditional birth date (c. 570 CE); the late end extends to 13 years after his death (632 CE)
- **Relationship**: The Birmingham leaves belong to the same codex as BnF Arabe 328(c) (Codex Parisino-petropolitanus)
- **Assessment by Marijn van Putten**: Despite early C14 date, the manuscript is "clearly a descendant of the ʿUthmānic text type" based on shared orthographic idiosyncrasies; "impossible" that it is pre-ʿUthmānic

### Sana'a Palimpsest (DAM 01-27.1)
- **Lower text (scriptio inferior)**: Multiple C14 dates reported:
  - University of Arizona lab: 75.1% probability of dating before 646 CE
  - Lyon laboratory: 543–643 CE; another fragment from same MS: 433–599 CE
- **Significance**: The lower text represents a **non-standard Qur'anic version** that deviates regularly from the ʿUthmānic text, making it an extremely rare witness to pre-canonical textual diversity
- The upper text conforms to the standard ʿUthmānic recension

### Tübingen (Ms M a VI 165)
- **Date**: 649–675 CE (94.8% confidence)
- **Content**: Qur'anic verses 17:36 to 36:57, in Hijazi script (originally catalogued as "Kufic" in 1930)
- **Significance**: Dates to the post-ʿUthmānic period, consistent with the standardization timeframe

### Codex Parisino-petropolitanus (BnF Arabe 328 + St. Petersburg)
- The Paris portion (BnF Arabe 328) is linked to the Birmingham leaves
- Not independently C14-dated, but the Birmingham association provides a date range
- Represents one of the most important early manuscript witnesses

### What C14 Dates Tell Us
- **Parchment dates, not ink**: C14 dating measures when the animal died (i.e., when the skin was produced), not when the text was written. Parchment could be stored for years or decades before use
- **Calibration ranges**: Results are probabilistic ranges (typically 70–100 year spans at 2σ), not precise dates. This limits their value for narrow chronological claims
- **Wide ranges**: The Birmingham result (568–645 CE) spans 77 years — from before Muhammad's birth to after his death — making it impossible to use alone to date the text precisely
- **Statistical interpretation**: A 95.4% confidence interval means the true date falls within the range with that probability, but the actual date is more likely near the center of the distribution
- **Opistography and reuse**: Results can be skewed if parchment was reused (palimpsest) or stored for extended periods

### Limitations Summary
- C14 dating cannot distinguish between a manuscript written in 610 CE vs. 640 CE when the range is 568–645
- It dates the material substrate, not the act of writing
- It cannot resolve debates about whether the text reflects early or late compilation
- C14 results must be combined with palaeographic and codicological analysis

## 10. Palaeographic Dating: Hijazi Script Development

### Script Classification
- **François Déroche's classification** (1983, 1992):
  - **Hijazi scripts** (7th century): Four groups, characterized by slanted vertical strokes, uneven spacing, minimal diacritics
  - **Early Abbasid scripts** (8th–9th century): Six groups, more regularized
  - **New Style** (9th–10th century): Two groups, more decorative

### Hijazi Script Characteristics
- Developed from Nabataean script via gradual orthographic innovations (5th–6th centuries CE)
- Key markers: accentuated vertical strokes slanting to the right, uneven word spacing, minimal vowel marking
- Loss of wawation (first attested 528 CE) and hamzah (attested 643 CE) mark developmental stages
- Regional variation: distinct features in manuscripts from different production centers

### Dating Precision
- Arabic palaeography is still a developing discipline; the script's evolution was gradual
- Regional variations complicate dating: a manuscript from one region may retain archaic features longer
- Déroche's system provides relative dating (century-level) rather than precise decade-level dating
- Script analysis is most useful when combined with C14 dating and codicological evidence

## 11. Modern Debates: Reliability of Traditional Makki/Madani Classification

### Traditional Islamic Position
- **Definition**: Makki = revealed before Hijrah (regardless of location); Madani = revealed after Hijrah
- **Consensus data** (classical scholars):
  - 82 surahs agreed as Makki
  - 20 surahs agreed as Madani
  - 12 surahs disputed
- **Method**: Based on Companion testimony (especially Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Masʿūd), not on the Prophet's explicit labeling
- **Important nuance**: Even agreed-Makki surahs may contain Madani verses and vice versa

### Academic Assessment

#### Points of Agreement
- The **broad Makki/Madani distinction** (pre-Hijrah vs. post-Hijrah) is generally accepted as reflecting a genuine historical shift in the Qur'an's content, style, and concerns
- The thematic and stylistic differences between the two categories align with what Western scholars independently identify:
  - Short, eschatological, monotheistic proclamations (Meccan)
  - Longer, legislative, community-building passages (Medinan)
- Nöldeke's chronological system broadly confirms the traditional binary division

#### Points of Disagreement
- **Fine-grained chronology**: Academic scholars often disagree with traditional lists on the ordering of specific surahs within each category
- **Composite surahs**: Western scholars more readily identify mixed-period surahs; traditional scholars acknowledge this but minimize its significance
- **Specific surah placements**: Academic chronologies place some surahs differently than traditional lists (e.g., al-Raʿd, al-Raḥmān, al-Muṭaffifīn)
- **Reliability of isnād-based attributions**: Some scholars question whether the Companion reports are reliable for every surah

#### Major Contemporary Positions
- **Nicolai Sinai**: The Makki/Madani distinction is broadly reliable; academic chronologies refine but do not overturn it
- **Gabriel Said Reynolds**: Questions the epistemological validity of chronological reconstruction altogether; advocates reading the Qur'an synchronically
- **Stephen Shoemaker**: Challenges early codification; argues the text may have continued to develop into the late 7th century
- **Angelika Neuwirth**: Accepts the basic division but emphasizes liturgical function over geographic origin

## 12. The 'First Revelation' in Academic Perspective

### Traditional Position
- **Majority view**: Q. 96:1–5 (Sūrat al-ʿAlaq) was the first revelation, based on the ḥadīth of ʿĀʾishah (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4953)
- **Minority views**: 
  - Q. 74:1 (Sūrat al-Muddaththir) — based on ḥadīth of Jābir (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī)
  - Q. 1 (Sūrat al-Fātiḥah) — minority position

### Academic Perspectives
- **Nöldeke and successors**: Generally accept Q. 96:1–5 as among the earliest revelations based on stylistic criteria (short, impassioned, eschatological register consistent with early Meccan style)
- **However**: Academic scholars do not treat the sīra/hadith accounts as historically reliable in detail; the specific narrative of the Cave of Ḥirāʾ is treated critically
- **Alternative theories**:
  - Some scholars note that Q. 96 as a complete surah shows signs of composite composition (vv. 1–5 differ markedly in style and theme from vv. 6–19)
  - The "first revelation" tradition may reflect later theological concerns about prophetic initiation rather than historical fact
  - The *fatrah* (interval of cessation) between Q. 96 and subsequent revelations is debated
- **Consensus**: Academic scholars generally treat Q. 96:1–5 as plausibly early but do not claim certainty about absolute priority

## 13. Surah Composition Theories: Unitary vs. Composite

### The Unitary Position
- **Defenders**: Michel Cuypers (Semitic Rhetorical Analysis), Mustansir Mir, Raymond Farrin, Neuwirth (for many surahs)
- **Core argument**: Surahs exhibit sophisticated ring composition, concentric structures, and mirror patterns consistent with intentional design
- **Evidence**: Semitic rhetoric analysis reveals parallel, concentric, and mirror compositions analogous to those in biblical and ancient Near Eastern texts
- **Implication**: Apparent incoherence results from readers unfamiliar with Semitic literary conventions, not from actual compositional disorder

### The Composite/Redaction Position
- **Defenders**: Richard Bell, Régis Blachère (moderate), Guillaume Dye, Gabriel Said Reynolds, some contributors to *The Quran of Historians* (2022)
- **Core argument**: Shifts in topic, addressee, tone, rhyme, and meter within surahs indicate multiple compositional layers
- **Evidence**:
  - Rhyme changes within surahs (e.g., Q. 55, Q. 89)
  - Synoptic problem: variant parallel narratives (e.g., Adam-Iblis stories in Q. 2:30–38, Q. 15:26–43, Q. 38:71–85)
  - Dietary prohibition passages in "Meccan" surahs (Q. 6, Q. 16) that presuppose Medinan legislative concerns
- **Redaction criticism**: Adapted from biblical studies; identifies editorial layers and theological developments across the text's formation

### Intermediate Positions
- **Neuwirth**: Accepts some compositional growth within surahs (*Fortschreibung*) while maintaining basic liturgical unity
- **Sinai**: Recognizes secondary insertions in some surahs (e.g., Q. 73, Q. 74) but argues for overall authorial coherence
- **Stylometric analysis**: Some computational studies (e.g., Halim & Dridi 2011) find stylistic unity consistent with single authorship; others detect layered composition

### Current State of the Debate
- The field has moved away from Bell's extreme "scissors-and-paste" model
- Most scholars now recognize a **spectrum**: some surahs are clearly unitary, others show evidence of secondary revision or expansion
- The concept of **Fortschreibung** (Sinai, Neuwirth) — progressive interpretation within the revelatory process — provides a middle path between pure unitary composition and radical fragmentation
- Redaction criticism is increasingly applied (Dye, Reynolds) but its conclusions remain contested
- Stylometric and computational methods are emerging but not yet decisive

## Summary of Key Findings

### What Academic Chronologies Agree On (with Traditional Lists)
1. The broad Makki/Madani distinction is valid
2. Early Meccan surahs are short, eschatological, and stylistically distinctive
3. Medinan surahs are longer, legislative, and address community concerns
4. The Qur'an's content reflects a 22-year revelatory process with genuine development

### Where Academic Chronologies Disagree (with Traditional Lists)
1. Fine-grained ordering of specific surahs within categories
2. The extent of composite composition within individual surahs
3. The reliability of isnād-based chronological attributions for disputed surahs
4. Whether some "Meccan" surahs contain Medinan material (and vice versa)

### Unresolved Questions
1. Did the ʿUthmānic codex achieve closure by c. 650 CE (Sinai) or later under ʿAbd al-Malik (Shoemaker)?
2. How much of the Qur'an's current form reflects editorial activity vs. prophetic proclamation?
3. Can radiocarbon dating ever resolve the early-vs-late compilation debate given its inherent limitations?
4. Is the Qur'an best read synchronically (Reynolds) or diachronically (Sinai, Neuwirth)?
