# Homoousios Terminology Development: Nicaea 325 → Athanasius → Constantinople 381

## Structured Scholarly Findings

**Research Date:** 2026-09-05  
**Sources:** Hanson (Search for the Christian Doctrine of God, 1988), Ayres (Nicaea and Its Legacy, 2004), Gwynn (The Eusebians, 2007; Athanasius of Alexandria, 2012), Anatolios (Journal of Early Christian Studies, 2013), primary texts (De Decretis, De Synodis, Tomus ad Antiochenos, Orations Against the Arians)

---

## 1. Athanasius's First Explicit Defense of Homoousios

### Finding: NOT in early works — the first extensive defense is De Decretis (ca. 353)

**Chronology of Athanasius's homoousios usage:**

| Date | Work | Homoousios Usage |
|------|------|-----------------|
| 328-335 | *Contra Gentes* / *De Incarnatione* | Homoousios theology taught but term NOT used |
| 339-346 | *Orations Against the Arians* (I-III) | Homoousios appears ONCE (Orat. i.9) — evidence of "lack of commitment to Nicaea's terminology at this stage" (Ayres, *Nicaea and Its Legacy*, 115) |
| ca. 353 | *De Decretis Nicaenae Synodi* | **First extensive explicit defense** of homoousios |
| 359-361 | *De Synodis* | Homoousios defended strategically, with conciliatory language toward Homoiousians |
| 362 | *Tomus ad Antiochenos* | Homoousios as theological basis, with terminological flexibility on hypostasis |

**Key evidence:**

- Ayres (2004): "Athanasius begins to defend Nicaea's homoousios in the De Decretis of ca. 353" — this is the first *extensive* defense.
- Hanson (1988, p. 438): "He began to use it first in the De Decretis … in 356 or 357" (minor dating variance; Ayres argues ca. 353 is more plausible based on the shifting climate of the 350s).
- The traditional date of 350/1 for De Decretis has been "rightly rejected as founded on insufficient evidence" (Ayres, citing Schwartz).
- In the *Orations Against the Arians* (early 340s), homoousios appears only once. Athanasius concentrated his theology on "the relation of the Son to the Father" through scriptural argumentation rather than the technical Nicene term.
- For nearly 20 years after Nicaea (325–345), homoousios was not mentioned by anyone: "What is conventionally regarded as the key-word in the Creed homoousion, falls completely out of the controversy very shortly after the Council of Nicaea and is not heard of for over twenty years" (Hanson Lecture).

**Athanasius's strategy in De Decretis (two-pronged):**
1. Defends homoousios as a necessary corollary of Nicaea's phrase *ek tēs ousias tou Patros* ("from the essence of the Father") — this phrase is more fundamental to Athanasius.
2. Defends both terms as necessary to preserve scripture's insistence that the Son is the Father's "true" Wisdom and Word — truly "proper to" (*idios*) and "from God."

**Confidence: HIGH** (directly attested in primary texts and confirmed by multiple independent scholars)

## 2. Homoiousios vs. Homoousios Controversy and Athanasius's Bridging Role

### Finding: Athanasius strategically bridged the divide in De Synodis (359-361) and Tomus ad Antiochenos (362)

**The theological spectrum (ca. 357-362):**

| Position | Key Term | Key Figures | Relation to Nicene |
|----------|----------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Homoousian** | *homoousios* (same substance) | Athanasius, later Cappadocians | Pro-Nicene |
| **Homoiousian** | *homoiousios* (similar substance) | Basil of Ancyra, George of Laodicea | Closest anti-Nicene to Nicene; rejected homoousios as Sabellian |
| **Homoian** | *homoios* (like, "according to scriptures") | Acacius, Eudoxius, Valens of Mursa | Vague; avoided all *ousia* language |
| **Anomoean/Heterousian** | *anomoios* (unlike) | Aetius, Eunomius | Radical subordinationism |

**Emergence of Homoiousianism (358):**
- Formulated at a council called by Basil of Ancyra in 358 as a response to "the emerging shape of Heterousian theology" (Ayres) in the form of the creed of Sirmium 357.
- The Homoiousians were NOT simply "Arians" — they rejected Aetius/Eunomius and were closer to Nicene theology than to radical subordinationism.
- Ironically, "the term homoiousios plays no role in Basil's surviving texts" (Hanson/Wikipedia on Homoiousian), suggesting the label may be partially a later construction.

**Athanasius's bridging strategy in De Synodis (359-361):**
- Claims Homoousians and Homoiousians "fundamentally teach the same doctrine."
- "Reaches out to the Homoiousians by attempting to refute their objections to Nicaea's two uses of ousia language" (Ayres, p. 171).
- Defends "from the Father's ousia" by saying it merely means the Son was not produced like created things.
- Deliberately avoids the term homoousios in the Orations Against the Arians, adopting the semi-Arian formula "like in essence" (*homoios kat' ousian*) where it serves his purpose.
- Avoids commenting on Basil's explicit anathema against homoousios — a strategic silence.

**Critical nuance:** Athanasius's conciliation was not entirely disinterested. Some scholars (Elliott, 2007) argue Athanasius was "not trying to be conciliatory at all, but rather to ensure the victory of the side he favoured in the political clash going on in Antioch" and that "in 362 there was a difference between a Nicene believer and a Nicene crank, and Athanasius was a member of the latter, very small, group."

**Confidence: HIGH** for Athanasius's bridging attempt; MODERATE for whether it was genuinely irenic or strategically political (scholars disagree)

## 3. Council of Alexandria 362 (Tomus ad Antiochenos)

### Finding: Athanasius's most significant conciliatory document, proposing terminological flexibility

**Historical context:**
- Emperor Julian (361-363) recalled all exiled bishops in 362, partly to sow discord among Christians.
- Athanasius returned to Alexandria in February 362 and convened a regional synod.
- The Tomus was addressed to the Antiochene schism between Eustathians/Paulinians (one-hypostasis model) and Meletians (three-hypostases model).

**Key proposals of the Tomus:**
1. **Nicene Creed as sole theological basis** — no new creeds needed.
2. **Terminological flexibility on hypostasis:** Freedom to profess either one hypostasis (Western/Latin model) or three hypostases (Eastern/Greek model), recognizing that the dispute was partly linguistic/conceptual rather than substantive.
3. **Full humanity of Christ in the incarnation** — against Apollinarian tendencies.
4. **Identity of Jesus as the divine Word incarnate.**
5. **Divinity of the Holy Spirit proclaimed.**
6. **Call for conciliatory attitude** — leave aside the "love of dispute."

**Significance:**
- "This letter played a key role in the Trinitarian theological debates between the one-hypostasis model and the three-hypostasis model of the Trinity, anticipating the turning point in this question from the 370s onward" (Wikipedia, Tomus ad Antiochenos).
- Recognized that "certain theological points of contention in the Arian controversy were based not only on differences of belief, but also on different language rules or conceptual differences between Latin and Greek."
- Paved the way for the Cappadocian formula: *mia ousia, treis hypostaseis* (one essence, three hypostases).
- The Tomus denies that the Western Council of Sardica (343) promulgated a different creed than Nicaea — some scholars interpret this as Athanasius lying about what happened at Sardica.
- The Tomus never mentions Meletius by name, who was the "rightful" bishop of Antioch in many people's minds — a politically motivated omission favoring Paulinus.

**Confidence: HIGH** for the content and proposals; MODERATE for the purity of Athanasius's motives (political vs. genuinely irenic — debated)

## 4. Did Nicaea 325 Immediately Settle the Controversy?

### Finding: NO — scholarly consensus is unanimous that reception took 55+ years (325-381)

**Timeline of non-resolution:**

| Year | Event | Significance |
|------|-------|-------------|
| 325 | Council of Nicaea | Homoousios affirmed, Arius condemned |
| 327 | Arius recalled from exile | Constantine begins backing away from strict enforcement |
| 330s | Eusebius of Nicomedia gains influence | Anti-Nicene positions resurge at court |
| 337 | Constantine dies | No strong imperial enforcement of Nicaea |
| 339-346 | Athanasius's second exile | Nicene party weakened |
| 341 | Council of Antioch | Creed issued excluding homoousios |
| 343 | Council of Sardica | East/West split; separate documents |
| 351 | Sirmium Council | Condemns Athanasius, Marcellus, Photinus; anti-ousia language |
| 353-355 | Councils of Arles, Milan | Constantius pressures Western bishops |
| 357 | "Blasphemy of Sirmium" | Explicitly bans all ousia language (Homoian position) |
| 358-359 | Sirmium, Ariminum, Seleucia | Homoian creed imposed by imperial pressure |
| 360 | Constantinople synod | Homoian creed as imperial dogma |
| 381 | Constantinople | Reaffirms and extends Nicaea; adds Spirit clause |

**Key scholarly quotes:**

- Ayres (2004, p. 96): "It was not seen as that useful or important."
- Ayres (2004, p. 100): "During the years 325–42 neither Arius nor the particular technical terminology used at Nicaea were at the heart of theological controversy."
- Hanson: "For 30 or more years after 325, the Council of Nicaea is hardly mentioned and the word homoousion rarely used."
- Jerome (contemporary): "Ingemuit totus orbis, et Arianum se esse miratus est" ("The whole world groaned, and was astonished to find itself Arian") — referring to the period after Sirmium 357/Ariminum 359.

**Why it didn't settle the controversy:**
1. Homoousios was new, non-biblical technical terminology — many bishops were uncomfortable.
2. Fears of Sabellianism (modalism) — homoousios seemed to flatten Father/Son distinction.
3. No sustained imperial enforcement after Constantine.
4. The creed focused on the Son-Father relation; did not address the Holy Spirit.
5. The controversy was about deeper issues (hypostasis, personhood) of which homoousios was only a symptom.

**Confidence: VERY HIGH** — this is the scholarly consensus across all major works (Hanson, Ayres, Behr, Gwynn, Williams)

## 5. Role of Imperial Politics in the Arian Controversy

### Finding: Imperial politics was decisive — every major theological shift corresponds to an imperial change

### Constantine I (306-337)
- **Convened Nicaea 325** — motivated by imperial unity, not purely theological concern.
- **Initially enforced** the Nicene settlement and exiled Arius.
- **Recalled Arius from exile (327)** — began shifting toward reconciliation under influence of Eusebius of Nicomedia.
- **Baptized by Eusebius of Nicomedia** on his deathbed (337) — an Arianizing bishop.
- **Assessment:** Instrumentalized the controversy for political unity; not consistently pro-Nicene.

**Confidence: HIGH**

### Constantius II (337-361, sole emperor 353-361)
- **Most significant imperial actor in the controversy.** Became sole emperor in 353.
- **Arianizing/Homoian sympathizer** — promoted compromise formulas distancing from Nicaea.
- **Convened multiple councils:** Sirmium (347, 351, 357, 358, 359), Arles (353), Milan (355), Ariminum/Seleucia (359), Constantinople (360).
- **Exiled Athanasius multiple times** (third exile, 356-362, was under Constantius).
- **Imposed the "Dated Creed"** (Homoian: Son "like" Father, avoiding all ousia language) at Ariminum 359 and Constantinople 360.
- **Sirmium 351** anathemas attacked ousia terminology — targeting not just Photinus/Marcellus but also the Nicene trajectory.
- **Assessment:** The most active imperial interventionist; his Homoian program was the dominant theological position for much of the 350s.

**Confidence: HIGH**

### Julian "the Apostate" (361-363)
- **Pagan emperor** who rejected Christianity entirely.
- **Recalled all exiled bishops (362)** — including Athanasius — ostensibly for religious tolerance, but with the strategic intent of increasing Christian factionalism ("probably with the intention … to increase the violence of the confrontations").
- This recall directly enabled the **Council of Alexandria 362** and the Tomus ad Antiochenos.
- **Assessment:** Indirect but crucial impact — his recall created the window for Athanasius's conciliatory initiative.

**Confidence: HIGH**

### Valens (364-378, Eastern emperor)
- **Homoian Christian** — the last Arianizing emperor of significance.
- **Persecuted Nicene Christians** in the East, especially in Egypt (371-374).
- **Exiled bishops** who refused the Homoian creed; monks sent to mines at Phaeno.
- **Athanasius's fourth exile** (365-366) was under Valens, though brief.
- His **death at Adrianople (378)** was interpreted by Nicene Christians as divine judgment and opened the way for **Theodosius I** and the Council of Constantinople 381.
- **Assessment:** Sustained anti-Nicene pressure in the East for 14 years; his removal was the necessary political precondition for 381.

**Confidence: HIGH**

### Theodosius I (379-395)
- **Pro-Nicene emperor** — issued the edict *Cunctos populos* (February 380) requiring all subjects to profess the Nicene faith.
- **Convened Constantinople 381** — the council that reaffirmed and extended Nicaea.
- **Assessment:** Imperial enforcement finally aligned with Nicene theology.

**Confidence: HIGH**

## 6. "Arian" as Polemical Label vs. Historiographical Category

### Finding: "Arianism" as a unified category is primarily Athanasius's polemical construction; modern scholarship has deconstructed it

### Athanasius's Construction

**Gwynn (The Eusebians, 2007) — the definitive study:**
- "The polarising categories of 'Nicene' and 'Arian' are largely a tendentious construct of Athanasius himself, created to help his own cause, and fail to do justice to the diversity that existed in the opposing parties."
- Athanasius constructed the "Eusebians" as an "Arian party" — but no such unified party existed. The individuals Athanasius grouped together held diverse theological positions.
- "Athanasius's lifelong use of Arius and the original controversy in Alexandria to ensure the success of later, and often endangered, Nicene orthodoxy was a masterful rhetorical and political move; his version of 'Arianism,' however, can no longer be trusted as the basis for historical research" (Cambridge Companion).

**How Athanasius used the label:**
1. Collapsed all opponents into a single "Arian heresy" regardless of their actual positions.
2. Linked diverse opponents to Arius personally (even though Arius "virtually disappears from the controversy at an early stage" — Hanson, xvii).
3. Used "Arian" as a guilt-by-association tactic: if you oppose Nicaea, you are an Arian; if you are an Arian, you follow the condemned heretic Arius.
4. Constructed a binary (orthodox vs. Arian) that obscured the actual multi-party nature of the debate.

**The four-tier distinction (from the Arian Controversy Audit):**

| Tier | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| 1. Arius himself | Historical person, documented in fragments | "There was when he was not" |
| 2. "Arianism" as polemical label | Constructed by opponents, especially Athanasius | "The Arian heresy" |
| 3. Later anti-Nicene groups | Distinct theological positions with their own names | Homoian, Homoiousian, Anomoean |
| 4. Modern historiographical category | Umbrella term used by historians | "The Arian controversy" |

### Modern Historiographical Assessment

- **Hanson (1988):** Recognizes the diversity of anti-Nicene positions. Notes that Eastern bishops "refused, in fact, to be called 'Arians' and in their creeds regularly anathematized typically 'Arian' doctrines."
- **Ayres (2004):** Emphasizes that Homoiousians were not simply "Arians" — they were closer to Nicene than to Arius.
- **Gwynn (2007):** The most thorough deconstruction — demonstrates Athanasius's rhetorical construction of the "Arian controversy."
- **Wiles (1996):** Documented the construction of "Arianism" as a heresiological school.
- **Later usage:** By the 8th century (Iconoclast controversy), "Arian" had become "the archetypal heresy" — "the first resort of those in search of a heretical term to throw at their enemies" (Parry). It was a term of abuse disconnected from the original 4th-century theological positions.

**Critical rules for safe usage:**
- Never say "Arians believed X" without specifying which group.
- Distinguish Arius himself from later groups.
- Recognize that "Arian" as a unified theological system is a polemical construct.
- When using "Arian" historiographically, note the label's loaded origins.

**Confidence: VERY HIGH** — this is the dominant position in current scholarship (Hanson, Ayres, Gwynn, Behr all agree)
