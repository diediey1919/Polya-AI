## 1. Western Academic Scholarship on Naskh

### Key Scholars and Their Contributions

**John Burton (1940-2010)**
- Published "The Sources of Islamic Law: Islamic Theories of Abrogation" (1990)
- Earlier work "The Collection of the Qur'an" (1977)
- Key finding: "no single verse" in the Quran "unequivocally points to the naskh of any other verse", nor does any "irreproachable" hadith identify "any one verse as having either undergone or effected naskh"
- Burton argued that early schools' views on naskh have been exaggerated, and that it was not as "sensitive" a topic as sometimes presented
- His work challenged the retrospective attribution of naskh theories to early Islamic figures

**Andrew Rippin (1950-2018)**
- Published "Al-Zuhrī, Naskh al-Qur'ān and the Problem of Early Tafsīr Texts" (BSOAS 47, 1984)
- "The Exegetical Literature of Abrogation: Form and Content" (Journal of Semitic Studies, 1984)
- Rippin expressed doubts about the attribution of early naskh works to al-Zuhrī
- Key insight: "naskh is based more on logic rather than on chronology"
- Argued that the naskh phenomenon "came into its own" during the generation of successors (tābi'ūn), not the Companions

**Joseph Schacht (1902-1969)**
- "The Origins of Muhammadan Jurisprudence" (Oxford, 1950)
- Established framework for understanding legal development in early Islam
- His work influenced later scholars including Burton and Hallaq
- Schacht's framework was dominant when early naskh studies were conducted

**David S. Powers**
- "The Exegetical Genre Nāsikh al-Qur'ān wa Mansūkhuhu" in Andrew Rippin (ed.), "Approaches to the History of the Interpretation of the Qur'ān" (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1988), pp. 117-138
- Key finding: "even if the Qur'an does sanction the doctrine of naskh...it nevertheless remains the case that the overwhelming majority of pairs of abrogated and abrogating verses are not identified as either 'abrogated' or 'abrogating'"
- Demonstrated that the literary genre al-nāsikh wa'l-mansūkh "developed hand-in-hand with the asbāb al-nuzūl on the one hand, and usūl al-fiqh on the other"
- Noted that references to the generation of Companions in naskh literature are "relatively infrequent"

**Wael B. Hallaq**
- "The Origins and Evolution of Islamic Law" (Cambridge, 2005)
- "Was al-Shāfi'ī the Master Architect of Islamic Jurisprudence?"
- "Law and the Qur'ān" in Encyclopedia of the Qur'ān
- Traced development of naskh within broader Islamic legal theory
- Critiqued Schacht for glossing over the 9th century

**Christopher Melchert**
- "Qur'ānic Abrogation across the Ninth Century: Shāfi'ī, Abū 'Ubaid, Muḥāsibī, and Ibn Qutaibah" in Bernard G. Weiss (ed.), "Studies in Islamic Legal Theory" (Leiden: Brill, 2002), pp. 75-94
- Traced how naskh doctrine evolved during the critical 9th century period
- Documented differences between major scholars on abrogation theory

**Louay Fatoohi**
- "Abrogation in the Qur'an and Islamic Law: A Critical Study of the Concept of 'Naskh' and its Impact" (2013)
- Identified three distinct modes of abrogation:
  1. Legal abrogation: "the abrogation of the ruling of a verse but not its wording"
  2. Legal-textual abrogation: "the abrogation of both the ruling and wording of a verse"
  3. Textual abrogation: "the abrogation of the wording of a verse but not its ruling"
- Showed that "the term naskh never appears in the Qur'an in the meaning it acquired in Islamic law"
- Traced development from basic form to complex doctrine
- Reviewed by Niels Jansen in sehepunkte 16 (2016)

## 2. John Burton's Thesis in "The Collection of the Qur'an" (1977)

### Core Argument
- Burton argued that **naskh al-tilāwah** (abrogation of recitation) was invented to explain missing verses without admitting textual loss
- The theory allowed Muslim scholars to account for verses that were known to have been revealed but did not appear in the final muṣḥaf
- This preserved the theological claim of perfect preservation while explaining textual discrepancies

### The Stoning Verse as Case Study
- Burton used the alleged "verse of stoning" (āyat al-rajm) as primary evidence
- According to hadith, a verse prescribing stoning was revealed but is absent from the Qur'an
- Traditional explanation: the verse was abrogated in recitation (tilāwah) but the ruling (ḥukm) remained in force
- Burton argued this was a post-hoc rationalization to avoid admitting the Qur'an suffered textual loss

### Scholarly Reception and Criticism

**Supportive Elements:**
- The thesis addressed real textual problems that traditional scholarship struggled with
- Provided historical-critical explanation for a theological doctrine

**Criticisms:**
- **Norman Calder** (Studies in Early Muslim Jurisprudence, 1993): Questioned dating and development of legal theories
- **Yasin Dutton**: Argued that "the importance of naskh in early Islamic law has been exaggerated, particularly by Burton, who presents it as a problematic and sensitive issue"
- **Wael Hallaq**: Noted Burton's inconsistency regarding whether isnāds can be traced to the first Islamic century
- **General criticism**: Burton somewhat exaggerated how early schools viewed naskh; it was not as controversial as he suggested
- Daniel Brown critiqued Burton's "constant inconsistency" on methodological issues

### Current Status
- Burton's thesis remains influential but is not universally accepted
- Many scholars agree naskh developed to address real textual problems
- Debate continues on whether this was deliberate invention or organic development

## 3. Source-Critical Analysis: Development of Naskh Theories

### Historical Development

**Early Period (1st-2nd/7th-8th centuries)**
- References to Companions discussing naskh are "relatively infrequent" in early literature
- Some Companions reportedly discussed and disagreed over abrogation of particular verses
- The phenomenon "came into its own" during the generation of successors (tābi'ūn)

**Formative Period (3rd/9th century)**
- Al-Shāfi'ī (d. 204/820) played crucial role in systematizing naskh within uṣūl al-fiqh
- Christopher Melchert's research shows significant variation among 9th-century scholars:
  - Shāfi'ī's approach
  - Abū 'Ubaid's methodology
  - Muḥāsibī's perspective
  - Ibn Qutayba's treatment
- This period saw the development of the dedicated genre of nāsikh wa'l-mansūkh literature

**Classical Period (4th-7th/10th-13th centuries)**
- Over 235 instances of abrogation were identified by the tenth century
- This number doubled in later centuries
- Increasing complexity and systematization

### Relationship to Legal Schools' Needs

**Harmonization Function:**
- Naskh served to resolve apparent contradictions in Qur'anic legal verses
- "It is essential to determine the relative chronology of the two verses because, if one mistakes the abrogating verse for the abrogated, Muslims would be adhering to a legal ruling that has been suppressed"
- The doctrine developed alongside asbāb al-nuzūl (occasions of revelation) and uṣūl al-fiqh

**Legal School Interests:**
- Different madhhabs developed different approaches to naskh based on their methodological commitments
- Naskh allowed schools to maintain preferred legal positions while acknowledging contrary Qur'anic texts
- Some scholars argue naskh claims were created or emphasized to harmonize legal contradictions that arose from different regional practices

### Critical Assessment
- Powers noted that treatises on abrogation "were probably intended to serve as handbooks for commentators and legal scholars"
- The development appears to be functional: addressing real interpretive problems
- However, the retrospective attribution of naskh to the Prophet or Companions is questionable
- The proliferation of abrogation claims over centuries suggests the doctrine expanded beyond its original scope

## 4. Naskh and Textual Criticism: Does Naskh Prove or Disprove Qur'anic Preservation?

### The Preservation Claim
- Qur'an 15:9: "Verily, We have revealed the Reminder (the Quran) and We will assuredly guard it (from corruption)"
- Traditional Muslim position: the Qur'an has been perfectly preserved without alteration

### Naskh as Evidence FOR Preservation

**Traditional Arguments:**
- Naskh demonstrates divine wisdom in gradual implementation of laws
- The preservation of abrogated verses in the muṣḥaf shows commitment to preserving all revealed text
- Abrogation is a feature, not a bug: it shows progressive revelation

**The Ibn 'Umar Statement:**
- 'Abdullah bin 'Umar reportedly said: "Let none of you say, 'I have got the whole of the Qur'ān.' How does he know what all of it is? Much of the Qur'ān is gone. Let him say instead, 'I have got what has survived.'"
- Muslim scholars explain this refers to abrogated verses whose recitation was given up
- "It is not possible for anyone to claim that he has learnt (all) what was revealed as Qur'an – the abrogating part of it and the abrogated"
- This is presented as evidence that abrogation was a known, accepted process

### Naskh as Evidence AGAINST Preservation

**Critical Arguments:**
- **Textual Loss Admission:** If verses were revealed but are now missing (even if "abrogated"), this constitutes textual loss
- **The Stoning Verse Problem:** A verse prescribing stoning was allegedly revealed but is absent from the Qur'an - this is either:
  1. Textual loss (against preservation claim)
  2. Never actually part of the Qur'an (raising questions about hadith reliability)
- **The Suckling Verse:** 'Ā'isha reported: "it had been revealed in the Holy Qur'ān that ten clear sucklings make the marriage unlawful, then it was abrogated (and substituted) by five sucklings and Allah's Messenger died and it was before that time (found) in the Holy Qur'ān (and recited by the Muslims)" (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim)
- **The Goat Story:** 'Ā'isha said: "The verse of stoning and of suckling an adult ten times were revealed, and they were (written) on a paper and kept under my bed. When the messenger of Allah expired and we were preoccupied with his death, a goat entered and ate away the paper" (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim - though scholars note authenticity issues)

**The Preservation Paradox:**
- If the Qur'an is perfectly preserved, how could verses be "forgotten" or lost?
- If verses were abrogated and removed, the muṣḥaf is not identical with what was recited by the Prophet
- Fatoohi argues this represents "a major crisis in Islamic scholarship"

### Academic Assessment
- **John Burton:** "no single verse" unequivocally points to naskh of any other verse
- **Muhammad Abduh:** "the Quran nowhere announced that verse so-and-so is naskh, or that verse such-and-such is mansūkh"
- **Critical conclusion:** Naskh theories developed to explain textual problems that arose in the transmission process
- The doctrine may reflect real historical development of the text rather than divine abrogation

## 5. Modern Reformist Muslim Scholars on Naskh

### Muhammad Abduh (1849-1905)
- Egyptian Grand Mufti and Islamic modernist
- **Key Position:** "the Quran nowhere announced that verse so-and-so is naskh, or that verse such-and-such is mansūkh"
- Questioned the scriptural basis for naskh as practiced in classical jurisprudence
- Emphasized rational understanding of Qur'anic revelation
- Called for return to Qur'anic principles over later juristic accretions

### Rashid Rida (1865-1935)
- Student and successor of Abduh
- Founded and edited al-Manār journal (1898-1935)
- Initially followed Abduh's modernist approach
- Later shifted toward more conservative Salafi methodology
- Called for ijtihād (independent reasoning) while maintaining traditional frameworks
- Emphasized alignment of sharī'a with modern necessities through maṣlaḥa (public welfare)
- His evolution represents tension between modernism and traditionalism

### Fazlur Rahman (1919-1988)
- Pakistani-American scholar
- **Key Works:** "Islam and Modernity" (1982), "Major Themes of the Qur'ān" (1980)
- **Double Movement Theory:** Move from particular verses to underlying ethical principles, then apply principles to contemporary contexts
- Criticized "atomistic" approach to Qur'an where laws were extracted from individual verses without understanding underlying unity
- Advocated thematic, contextualist reading
- His approach implicitly reduces need for naskh by emphasizing ethical principles over specific legal rulings
- Tamara Sonn classified his methodology as "historicist"

### Muhammad Fazlur Rahman Ansari
- Student of Allama Abdul Aleem Siddiqi Merathi
- **Radical Position:** "rejected anything which compromised the integrity of the Quran, and hence he rejected the application, within the Quran, of any Naskh (i.e., cancellation or abrogation of any Ayah or divinely-revealed verse)"
- Argued that Q.2:106 refers to "cancellation of previously revealed laws in previous scriptures, and did not imply that any verse of the Quran was ever abrogated, cancelled or forgotten"
- Maintained that "Naskh (i.e., cancellation/abrogation of an Ayah or verse) did not apply internally to verses of the Qur'ān, but, rather, externally to certain previous divine revelations"
- This represents the most restrictive interpretation of naskh among modern scholars

### Common Reformist Themes
1. **Scriptural skepticism:** Questioning whether Qur'an actually supports naskh as classically understood
2. **Rational approach:** Emphasizing coherence and unity of Qur'anic message
3. **Contextual reading:** Understanding verses in historical context rather than through later juristic frameworks
4. **Ethical focus:** Prioritizing underlying principles over specific legal rulings
5. **Integrity preservation:** Protecting Qur'anic text from claims of internal contradiction or change

## 6. The 'Stoning Verse' (Āyat al-Rajm) Debate

### The Evidence

**Hadith Sources:**
- Multiple authentic hadiths reference a "verse of stoning" that was revealed but is absent from the Qur'an
- 'Umar reportedly said: "I am afraid that after a long time has passed, people may say, 'We do not find the Verses of the Rajm (stoning to death) in the Holy Book,' and consequently they may go astray by leaving an obligation that Allah has revealed'" (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī)
- The alleged verse: "The fornicators among the married men (ash-shaikh) and married women (ash-shaikhah), stone them as an exemplary punishment from Allah, and Allah is Mighty and Wise"

**Traditional Explanation:**
- The verse was revealed as part of Sūrah al-Aḥzāb
- It was abrogated in recitation (tilāwah) but the ruling (ḥukm) remained in force
- This is classified as "naskh al-tilāwah wa-baqā' al-ḥukm" (abrogation of recitation while maintaining the ruling)

### Academic Analysis

**Historical Context:**
- Pre-Islamic Arabia: stoning was practiced among certain tribes, possibly influenced by Jewish communities
- Al-'Askari (d. 1005 CE) attributes initial Arab application to figures like Rabī'a bin Nasr
- Early Islamic cases: Muhammad ordered stoning of Jewish adulterers, reportedly judging them by Torah prescription

**Critical Scholarship:**

**John Burton:**
- Used stoning verse as primary evidence for his thesis
- Argued the theory of naskh al-tilāwah was invented to explain this textual absence
- Without this theory, Muslims would have to admit the Qur'an suffered textual loss

**David Powers:**
- In "On the Abrogation of Qur'ān 33:40" (cited in academic reviews)
- Analyzed relationship between stoning doctrine and textual history

**Modern Analysis:**
- **Kara (recent scholarship):** Using isnād-cum-matn analysis, finds that al-Zuhrī is the common link for the hadith in which Muhammad rules that a man's wife be stoned in accordance with the "book of Allah"
- Another hadith, whose kernel seems historically credible in light of the Constitution of Medina, dates to Muhammad's lifetime and involves Jews asking Muhammad to arbitrate

**Contemporary Implications:**

**Shi'i Perspective (from Persian academic journal):**
- "In Sunni ḥadīth sources, a narration regarding rajm (stoning) appears, suggesting that this punishment was legislated through a verse of the Qur'ān rather than merely established by the Prophetic Sunnah"
- "The absence of this verse in the 'Uthmānic codices, however, has reinforced suspicions of textual omission (taḥrīf bi'l-naqṣ) in the Qur'ān"
- "Drawing upon historical analysis, this article seeks to demonstrate how political motivations contributed to the fabrication and dissemination of the verse of stoning among Sunni transmitters"
- Conclusion: "both the Āye Rajm and the related narrations lack epistemic credibility"

**Legal Status:**
- Majority of Sunni jurists accept stoning as valid punishment based on hadith
- Quranists and some modernist Muslims reject stoning due to its absence from Qur'an
- The debate highlights tension between Qur'anic text and hadith-based jurisprudence

### Implications for Textual History

**If the verse was revealed and lost:**
- The Qur'an as we have it is incomplete
- The 'Uthmānic codex does not contain all revealed verses
- This contradicts the preservation claim

**If the verse was never part of the Qur'an:**
- The hadiths claiming it was Qur'anic are unreliable
- This raises questions about hadith transmission accuracy
- The doctrine of naskh al-tilāwah may be based on false premises

**Academic consensus:** The stoning verse debate remains unresolved and represents one of the most significant challenges to traditional Qur'anic preservation narratives

## 7. Naskh in Uṣūl al-Fiqh Development: Madhhab Differences

### Al-Shāfi'ī (d. 204/820)

**Foundational Role:**
- Often considered the most seminal figure in early Islamic jurisprudence
- "Kitāb al-Risāla fī Uṣūl al-Fiqh" - foundational text on principles of Islamic jurisprudence
- "Kitāb al-Umm" - his fiqh work
- Systematized naskh within broader hermeneutical framework

**Approach to Naskh:**
- Integrated naskh into comprehensive theory of legal sources
- Established clear criteria for identifying abrogating and abrogated texts
- Emphasized chronology and context
- His approach became standard for later Shāfi'ī scholars

**Influence:**
- Christopher Melchert's research shows Shāfi'ī's influence on 9th-century naskh discourse
- Norman Calder controversially argued Shāfi'ī's Risāla must be dated later based on hermeneutic sophistication (though this has been challenged)

### Hanafī School

**Methodological Characteristics:**
- Founded by Abū Ḥanīfa (d. 150/767)
- Greater reliance on ra'y (reasoned opinion) and qiyās (analogical reasoning)
- More flexible approach to textual interpretation

**Approach to Naskh:**
- Accepted naskh but with specific conditions
- Emphasized rational reconciliation of texts before invoking abrogation
- Used istihsān (juristic preference) which could function similarly to naskh
- Representative scholar: Al-Shaybānī (d. 189/804)

### Mālikī School

**Methodological Characteristics:**
- Founded by Mālik b. Anas (d. 179/795)
- Emphasized 'amal ahl al-Madīna (practice of Medina) as legal source
- "Muwaṭṭa'" - foundational text combining hadith and legal rulings

**Approach to Naskh:**
- Accepted naskh within framework of Medinan practice
- Used istiṣlāḥ (public interest) which could override apparent textual meanings
- Less emphasis on formal naskh theory, more on practical application
- Representative scholar: Ibn Rushd al-Jadd (d. 520/1126)

### Ḥanbalī School

**Methodological Characteristics:**
- Founded by Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal (d. 241/855)
- Strong emphasis on hadith and literal text
- Minimal use of ra'y and qiyās

**Approach to Naskh:**
- Accepted naskh but preferred literal readings when possible
- Reluctant to invoke abrogation without strong evidence
- Representative scholar: Ibn Taymiyya (d. 728/1328)

### Ẓāhirī School (Ibn Ḥazm)

**Foundational Principles:**
- Founded by Dāwūd al-Ẓāhirī (d. 270/884)
- Most prominent representative: Ibn Ḥazm (d. 456/1064)
- "People are bound to obey only the law of God, in its zāhir or literal sense, without restrictions, additions, or modifications"

**Rejection of Analogical Reasoning:**
- "The ẓāhirī denied the legitimacy of legal rulings based upon qiyās (analogy)"
- "Rejected the use of the principles of personal evaluation; the pursuit of what is considered good (istihsān), the pursuit of values for the common good (istiṣlāḥ), and especially the recourse to personal opinion (ra'y)"
- Focused exclusively on explicit textual meanings

**Approach to Naskh:**
- Ibn Ḥazm's "Al-Muḥallā" - comprehensive Ẓāhirī jurisprudence
- Accepted naskh but with strict evidentiary requirements
- Required clear textual proof of abrogation
- Rejected naskh based on inference or reasoning alone
- More conservative than other schools in invoking abrogation

**Historical Context:**
- "Originally a Shāfi'ī jurist, Ibn Ḥazm joined the Ẓāhirī school and brought to it a systematic structure of logic"
- "Opposed the allegorical interpretation of religious texts"
- School flourished in al-Andalus but later declined
- "Ibn Khaldun considered it to be extinct" in 14th century
- Modern revival through Ahmad Shākir's republication of Al-Muḥallā

### Comparative Summary

| School | Approach to Naskh | Key Characteristic |
|--------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Shāfi'ī | Systematic, integrated | Clear hermeneutical framework |
| Hanafī | Flexible, rational | Preference for reconciliation |
| Mālikī | Practical, contextual | Emphasis on Medinan practice |
| Ḥanbalī | Conservative, textual | Reluctant invocation |
| Ẓāhirī | Strict evidentiary | Literal interpretation only |

## 8. Critical Evaluation: "Abrogation Proves the Qur'an Was Changed"

### The Critical Argument

**Premise 1:** If verses were revealed but are now absent from the Qur'an, the text has changed
**Premise 2:** Naskh al-tilāwah acknowledges that revealed verses are not in the muṣḥaf
**Conclusion:** The Qur'an as we have it is not identical to what was revealed

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Stoning verse: allegedly revealed but absent
- Suckling verse: "it had been revealed in the Holy Qur'ān...and Allah's Messenger died and it was before that time (found) in the Holy Qur'ān"
- Ibn 'Umar's statement: "Much of the Qur'ān is gone"
- 'Ā'isha's goat story (though authenticity disputed)

### Traditional Muslim Responses

**Response 1: Naskh is Divine Wisdom**
- Abrogation shows God's mercy in gradual implementation
- It reflects divine wisdom, not textual corruption
- The preserved abrogated verses serve as historical record

**Response 2: Preservation Promise**
- Qur'an 15:9 promises preservation
- Abrogated verses were never meant to be permanently preserved
- The muṣḥaf contains what God intended to preserve

**Response 3: Contextual Understanding**
- Ibn 'Umar's statement refers to abrogated verses, not textual loss
- "It is not possible for anyone to claim that he has learnt (all) what was revealed as Qur'ān – the abrogating part of it and the abrogated"
- This is expected given the abrogation process

### Critical Analysis of These Responses

**Problem 1: Circular Reasoning**
- Claim: Qur'an is perfectly preserved
- Evidence: God promised preservation (Qur'an 15:9)
- Problem: Using the text to prove its own preservation is circular

**Problem 2: Redefining "Preservation"**
- If "preservation" means "only what God intended to preserve," this is tautological
- The claim becomes unfalsifiable: any missing verse was "intended" to be missing

**Problem 3: Historical Evidence**
- 'Uthmān's burning of variant codices suggests textual variation existed
- Hadith literature preserves verses not in the Qur'an
- Early Islamic sources acknowledge textual problems

**Problem 4: Theological Implications**
- If God could abrogate and remove verses, why not preserve them?
- The doctrine implies God "changed His mind" - problematic for divine immutability
- As one critic noted: "the idea of God changing His mind...is widely established and deeply entrenched" in Islamic tradition, but this raises philosophical questions

### Academic Assessment

**Middle Position (Most Scholars):**
- Naskh developed to address real textual problems in transmission
- The doctrine reflects historical development of the text
- Whether this constitutes "corruption" depends on theological presuppositions

**Critical Position (Burton, Powers, etc.):**
- Naskh theories were developed retrospectively to explain textual discrepancies
- The Qur'an underwent development like any other ancient text
- Traditional claims of perfect preservation are historically untenable

**Traditional Position (Muslim Scholars):**
- Naskh is a divine feature, not a textual problem
- The Qur'an is perfectly preserved as God intended
- Abrogated verses serve theological and historical purposes

### The Falsifiability Problem

**Critical Challenge:**
- The preservation claim, as traditionally understood, sets an impossibly high standard
- "A perfectly preserved revelation does not require emergency compilation because it is in danger of disappearing"
- 'Umar's fear of losing Qur'an knowledge (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4986) contradicts preservation claim
- 'Uthmān's burning of variant codices (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4987) suggests textual instability

**Traditional Response:**
- Compilation was preservation, not evidence against it
- 'Uthmān standardized to prevent variation, not to suppress it
- The current text represents the authentic revelation

### Conclusion

The claim "abrogation proves the Qur'an was changed" depends on definitional and theological commitments:

**If "changed" means:**
- Any difference between original revelation and current text → **Yes, proven**
- Corruption or distortion of intended message → **Debated**
- Divine abrogation as intended feature → **No, not changed**

**Academic consensus:** The Qur'an, like all ancient texts, has a complex transmission history. The doctrine of naskh reflects attempts to address textual problems. Whether this constitutes "change" or "divine wisdom" depends on one's theological framework.

**Critical insight:** The very existence of naskh theory demonstrates that early Muslims recognized textual problems and developed sophisticated hermeneutical tools to address them. This is evidence of intellectual rigor, not necessarily textual corruption.

## Summary of Critical Academic Perspective

**Mainstream Academic Consensus:**
1. Naskh developed as a hermeneutical tool to address textual and legal contradictions
2. The doctrine evolved significantly from the 8th to 10th centuries
3. Early attribution of naskh to the Prophet or Companions is questionable
4. The stoning verse represents a genuine textual problem
5. Different legal schools developed different approaches based on methodological commitments

**Minority/Controversial Positions:**
1. **Burton's thesis:** Naskh al-tilāwah was invented to hide textual loss - influential but not universally accepted
2. **Radical skepticism:** The Qur'an underwent significant editorial development - held by some revisionist scholars
3. **Complete rejection:** Naskh has no Qur'anic basis - held by some modernist Muslims

**Speculative Claims (Not Well-Supported):**
1. Deliberate suppression of verses by early caliphs
2. Multiple competing Qur'anic codices with different contents
3. Qur'an as we have it is fundamentally different from Muhammad's recitation

**Controversial but Debated:**
1. Extent of 'Uthmānic editorial activity
2. Reliability of hadith about "missing" verses
3. Whether naskh reflects divine wisdom or human development
