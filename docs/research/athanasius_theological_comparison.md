## Part A: Feature-by-Feature Comparison Table

### 1. Ousia (οὐσία — essence/being)

| Feature | Arius | Athanasius | Cappadocians |
|---------|-------|-----------|--------------|
| Meaning | God the Father alone is the true ousia; the Son derives from nothing (ex ouk ontōn) | The Father and Son share the same divine ousia; the Son is "from the ousia of the Father" (ek tēs ousias tou patros) | ʿOusia = the common divine nature/essence shared by all three persons; the "what" of God |
| Usage | Rejected homoousios; the Son is of a different ousia (heteroousios) | Championed homoousios — the Son is "of the same substance" as the Father | Systematized ousia as the universal/common term: "one ousia" expresses divine unity |

### 2. Hypostasis (ὑπόστασις — subsistence/person)

| Feature | Arius | Athanasius | Cappadocians |
|---------|-------|-----------|--------------|
| Meaning | Used loosely; Nicaea's anathemas condemned those who said the Son was "from another hypostasis or ousia" | Used ousia and hypostasis as SYNONYMS (following Nicene usage); preferred "one hypostasis" (mia hypostasis) for God | **Separated the terms**: hypostasis = particular person/subsistence (the "who"); distinguished from ousia (the "what") |
| Formula | N/A | **One hypostasis** (with the Father, Son, Spirit united in one divine reality) | **Three hypostases** (treis hypostaseis) — Father, Son, Spirit as distinct subsistences |

**Key scholarly finding:** Athanasius's use of hypostasis is fundamentally different from the Cappadocians'. For Athanasius, hypostasis ≈ ousia ≈ the one divine reality. For the Cappadocians, hypostasis = a particular person distinct from other persons sharing the same ousia. (Source: Ayres 2004; Tomus ad Antiochenos scholarship; Prestige cited in academia.edu article on Cappadocian Settlement)

### 3. Father-Son Relation

| Feature | Arius | Athanasius | Cappadocians |
|---------|-------|-----------|--------------|
| Nature of relation | The Son is a creature (ktisma), brought into being by the Father's will before ages; the Father is "unbegotten" (agennētos), the Son is not | The Son is eternally begotten (gennētos) from the Father's essence; the Son is idios (proper to) the Father, not external (exothen) | Father-Son relation defined by the "mode of existence" (tropos hyparxeos): the Father's property is unbegottenness, the Son's is being begotten |
| Subordination | Ontological subordination: the Son is inferior in nature | No subordination in nature; the Son is fully equal in divinity. However, Athanasius retains some asymmetrical language (the Son as "ray from sun," "river from source") — Stead (1977) notes Athanasius is "perhaps moving only very cautiously away from moderate Origenism" | Co-equality affirmed; the Father has logical/causal priority as "source" (archē) but NOT superiority of nature |

### 4. Spirit Theology (Pneumatology)

| Feature | Arius | Athanasius | Cappadocians |
|---------|-------|-----------|--------------|
| Status of the Spirit | The Spirit is a creature, produced by the Son (per the later "Eusebian" and Neo-Arian position) | **Letters to Serapion** (c. 358-360): The Spirit is NOT a creature; belongs to the divine side of the Creator/creature divide. The Spirit "shares in the being of Father and Son" (I.25). The Spirit unites us to God (I.24). | Developed full pneumatology: Spirit is fully divine, co-equal, co-eternal. Basil's *On the Holy Spirit* (375); Gregory of Nazianzus's *Fifth Theological Oration* (380) explicitly calls the Spirit "God" |
| Procession | N/A | Affirms the Spirit "proceeds from the Father" and is "given and sent from the Son." Spirit is "image of the Word as Son is image of Father." But **does not develop precise procession terminology** | **Key Cappadocian development**: distinguished the Spirit's "procession" (ekporeusis) from the Son's "generation" (gennēsis). Gregory of Nazianzus (Or. 31.8): Father = unbegotten; Son = begotten; Spirit = proceeding. Each has a unique tropos hyparxeos. |
| Worship | Spirit not to be worshipped | Spirit is to be worshipped together with Father and Son (implied divinity, made explicit but not extensively developed) | Spirit is co-worshipped (symproskynēton); Basil defended the doxology "to the Father AND to the Son AND to the Holy Spirit" |

### 5. Generation (Gennēsis)

| Feature | Arius | Athanasius | Cappadocians |
|---------|-------|-----------|--------------|
| Nature | The Son was "begotten" but this = created by the Father's will before ages. Arius used "begotten" and "created" as SYNONYMS (per Synod of Ancyra analysis) | Sharp distinction: BEGOTTEN ≠ CREATED. Begotten = from the nature/being of the begetter. Created = from nothing (ex nihilo). The Son is eternally begotten from the ousia of the Father. | Generation = the Father's eternal act of communicating the divine ousia to the Son. Defined as the Son's unique tropos hyparxeos (mode of existence). |
| Temporal aspect | The Son had a beginning: "there was when he was not" (ēn hote ouk ēn) | **Eternal generation**: the Father is eternally Father, the Son eternally Son. No "before" or "when." | Eternal generation affirmed; the generation is beyond time and comprehension. |

### 6. Procession (Ekporeusis)

| Feature | Arius | Athanasius | Cappadocians |
|---------|-------|-----------|--------------|
| Concept | N/A (no developed Spirit theology) | Uses language of the Spirit "proceeding from" the Father but does not technically distinguish this from the Son's generation | **Developed the key distinction**: procession (ekporeusis) is the Spirit's unique mode of origin, different from generation (gennēsis) which is the Son's. This prevents the Spirit from being a "grandson" (Gregory of Nazianzus, Or. 31.8). |

### 7. Monotheism

| Feature | Arius | Athanasius | Cappadocians |
|---------|-------|-----------|--------------|
| Approach | Strict monotheism: only the Father is truly God (ho theos). The Son is theos in a derivative/subordinate sense. "Theos" had wide semantic range in antiquity. | One God, but the Son and Spirit are fully included in the one divine nature. Defended against charges of tritheism by insisting on the indivisibility of the divine ousia. | One ousia shared by three hypostases. Divine unity is secured by the single ousia; monotheism preserved while allowing real personal distinctions. |
| Concern | Preserved God's absolute uniqueness as the sole unbegotten monad | Preserved unity through the homoousios — Father and Son share one identical divine being | Preserved unity through the "one ousia" formula; also appealed to the single divine will (thelema) and activity (energeia) |

### 8. Divine Unity

| Feature | Arius | Athanasius | Cappadocians |
|---------|-------|-----------|--------------|
| Mechanism | Unity = the Father alone is God; the Son is a separate being | Unity = one hypostasis, one divine being; Father, Son, Spirit are inseparable (coinherence/perichoresis implied). The Trinity is "indivisible and self-consistent" with "a singular will and activity." | Unity = one ousia; three hypostases are "unmixed" yet inseparable; one will, one activity; perichoresis (mutual indwelling) |

### 9. Homoousios (ὁμοούσιος)

| Feature | Arius | Athanasius | Cappadocians |
|---------|-------|-----------|--------------|
| Position | REJECTED homoousios. Son is heteroousios (different substance) from the Father | DEFENDED homoousios as the Nicene standard. The Son is "of the same substance" as the Father. But Athanasius always says the SON is homoousios WITH THE FATHER (asymmetric: never "Father homoousios with Son") — Stead (1977) | Used homoousios within their "one ousia, three hypostases" framework. The Spirit is also homoousios with the Father and Son (extending the term beyond what Nicaea 325 addressed). |
| Meaning for them | N/A | The Son shares the Father's divine nature essentially (not by participation/grace). The Son is idios to the Father — what belongs to the Father belongs to the Son by nature. | Ousia as universal shared by the three hypostases; homoousios guarantees the common divine nature. |

### 10. Eternal Generation

| Feature | Arius | Athanasius | Cappadocians |
|---------|-------|-----------|--------------|
| Position | DENIED eternal generation. The Son was brought into existence before time but had a beginning. "There was when he was not." | AFFIRMED eternal generation. The Father is eternally Father, the Son eternally Son. The generation is not temporal but eternal. "For Athanasius the Son belongs on the divine side of the radical gulf...being himself by nature the eternally begotten (gennētos), transcendent and uncreated Son." | AFFIRMED eternal generation as the Son's distinctive tropos hyparxeos. Beyond time, beyond comprehension. |
| Key text | Arius: "Before he was begotten, he was not" | Athanasius: the Father's being Father implies the eternal existence of the Son (you can't be a father without a child) | Gregory of Nazianzus: the generation is an eternal act, not a temporal event |

### 11. Creation Through the Son

| Feature | Arius | Athanasius | Cappadocians |
|---------|-------|-----------|--------------|
| Role of Son in creation | The Son was the Father's instrument/agent for creating the rest of creation. The Son was first created, then used to create everything else. The Father was too transcendent to interact directly with matter. | Creation is the Father's work THROUGH the Son. The Son is not a mediator needed because of divine transcendence (that's absurd, says Athanasius — it leads to infinite regress of mediators, *Against the Arians* 2.26). The Son is the Father's own Word and Wisdom in creation. | Creation is the common work of the Trinity: from the Father, through the Son, in the Holy Spirit (taxis of divine activity extending ad extra). |
| Key argument | The Father needs a mediator because He cannot directly touch creation | **Reductio ad absurdum**: if God is so transcendent that He needs a mediator to create, then the mediator (being a creature) would need another mediator, ad infinitum (*Second Oration Against the Arians* 26). "A vast throng of accumulating mediators." | Unified divine activity (energeia): all three persons act together, though in an ordered way reflecting the taxis. |

## Part B: Answers to the Five Scholarly Consensus Questions

### Question 1: How does Athanasius's terminology differ from the Cappadocians?

**Scholarly consensus: SIGNIFICANT DIFFERENCE, not mere variation.**

1. **Ousia and Hypostasis:** Athanasius used these terms as SYNONYMS (following the original Nicene Creed of 325, where the anathemas treat "from another hypostasis or ousia" as equivalent). The Cappadocians SEPARATED the terms: ousia = common nature; hypostasis = particular person.

2. **Pre-Cappadocian vocabulary:** Athanasius's mature Trinitarian vocabulary is pre-Cappadocian. He speaks of "one hypostasis" (mia hypostasis) and uses the Nicene homoousios to express the Son's equality with the Father. He does NOT use the later Cappadocian distinction between the "what" (ousia) and the "who" (hypostasis).

3. **Tomus ad Antiochenos (362):** Athanasius explicitly recognized that the dispute between "one hypostasis" (Western/Alexandrian) and "three hypostases" (Eastern/Origenist) was a **linguistic difference, not a doctrinal one** (Tom 6:3-4). He legitimized both terminologies but did not himself adopt the three-hypostasis formula.

4. **Procession terminology:** Athanasius does not distinguish the Spirit's "procession" (ekporeusis) from the Son's "generation" (gennēsis) in the technical way the Cappadocians do. He uses analogies (light/radiance/enlightening; fountain/river/drink) but does not develop the technical tropos hyparxeos framework.

5. **Asymmetry:** Athanasius consistently says "the Son is homoousios with the Father" but never "the Father is homoousios with the Son" — this asymmetry reflects his Alexandrian/Origenist heritage and his cautious departure from moderate Origenism (Stead 1977). The Cappadocians moved toward more reciprocal language.

**Sources:** Ayres (2004); Tomus ad Antiochenos (Wikipedia article, citing Opitz/Brennecke critical editions); Stead (1977, *Divine Substance*); Anatolios (1998); Behr (2004); the didymus.org lecture notes on Athanasius and the Cappadocians.

### Question 2: Did Athanasius use the "one ousia, three hypostases" formula?

**Scholarly consensus: NO. He did NOT use this formula.**

1. **Athanasius's own formula:** "One hypostasis" (mia hypostasis), consistent with the Western/Latin tradition and the Nicene Creed's anathemas. He equated ousia with hypostasis.

2. **The formula "mia ousia, treis hypostaseis"** was the achievement of the Cappadocians (Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nazianzus, Gregory of Nyssa), developed from the 370s onward and formalized at Constantinople 381. Prestige: "The Cappadocian Settlement finally fixed the statement of Trinitarian orthodoxy in the formula of one ousia and three hypostaseis" (cited in academia.edu article).

3. **Athanasius's contribution was preparatory, not constitutive:** In the Tomus ad Antiochenos (362), Athanasius "paved the way" (Wikipedia) for the Cappadocian formula by:
   - Recognizing both "one hypostasis" and "three hypostases" as legitimate
   - Eliminating the identification of ousia and hypostasis as the only acceptable reading
   - Creating theological space for the Cappadocians to distinguish the terms
   - But he did not himself formulate "one ousia, three hypostases"

4. **He died in 373**, before the Cappadocians fully developed the formula (Basil's major works date 370s; Gregory of Nazianzus's Fifth Theological Oration is 380; Constantinople is 381).

5. **Note:** Some later texts attributed to Athanasius may use Cappadocian-style language, but these are considered anachronistic interpolations or post-Cappadocian redactions. The critical editions (Opitz, Brennecke) confirm the authentic Athanasius uses the one-hypostasis model.

**Sources:** Tomus ad Antiochenos (critical edition Opitz/Brennecke); Ayres (2004); Wikipedia on Tomus ad Antiochenos (citing Gemeinhardt 2006, Tetz 1975); academia.edu article on the Cappadocian Settlement.

### Question 3: How did Athanasius distinguish 'begotten' (gennētos) from 'created' (ktistos)?

**Scholarly consensus: This is the CENTRAL distinction in Athanasius's anti-Arian theology.**

1. **The distinction defined:**
   - **Created (ktistos/poiētos):** Brought into existence from nothing (ex ouk ontōn / ex nihilo) by an act of will. Characterizes all creatures.
   - **Begotten (gennētos):** Coming from the nature/being (ousia) of the begetter. An essential, not voluntaristic, derivation. Characterizes the Son alone.

2. **Against Arius's equation:** Arius treated "begotten" and "created" as SYNONYMS. He described the Son as "a creature (ktisma), but not as one of the creatures" (Letter to Alexander). The Son was brought into being from nothing (ex ouk ontōn) before the ages.

3. **Athanasius's response (key texts):**
   - The Son is "proper to" (idios) the Father, not "external to" (exothen) Him. Creatures are external; the Son is from the Father's own being.
   - The Son does not receive divinity by PARTICIPATION (methexis) but by NATURE (physis). A creature that receives by participation cannot give to others what it doesn't own (*De Synodis* 51).
   - The Son is "Word itself, light itself, truth itself, justice itself, virtue itself, and the very power, stamp, effulgence, and image (eikōn) of the Father" (*Against the Heathen* 46).

4. **Hermeneutical application — Proverbs 8:22 ("The Lord created me"):**
   - Arius read this as proof that the Son is a creature.
   - Athanasius used **partitive exegesis** (Behr 2004): this text refers to the INCARNATION, not the eternal generation. "This took place when he became man, for this is a property of a human being." The Son is "created for the works" (incarnationally) but "begotten in respect to his nature" (eternally).

5. **Ontological divide:** For Athanasius, there is a RADICAL ontological gulf between Creator and creature (following Platonic metaphysics). The Son belongs on the CREATOR side. Arius placed the Son on the CREATURE side (albeit as the highest creature). This is the fundamental disagreement.

**Sources:** Christou, "Uncreated and Created, Unbegotten and Begotten in the Theology of Athanasius" (1973, *Augustinianum*); Behr (2004); Anatolios (1998); uncommonphilosophyuk.wordpress.com analysis; garrettham.com article citing Ayres, Williams, Pelikan.

### Question 4: Did Athanasius teach the complete Constantinopolitan Trinity or a developing form?

**Scholarly consensus: A DEVELOPING FORM — highly advanced, but not yet the full Constantinopolitan formulation.**

**What Athanasius DID achieve:**
- Full defense of the Son's consubstantiality (homoousios) with the Father
- Well-developed Spirit theology in Letters to Serapion: the Spirit is divine, not a creature, shares in the being of Father and Son, is to be worshipped with them
- In the Tomus ad Antiochenos (362), insisted on condemnation of those who say the Spirit is a creature, extending Nicene faith to pneumatology
- Affirmed the Trinity as "indivisible and self-consistent" with "a singular will and activity"
- Created the theological conditions for the Cappadocian settlement by legitimizing multiple Trinitarian vocabularies

**What Athanasius DID NOT achieve (compared to Constantinople 381):**
1. **No "one ousia, three hypostases" formula** — he used "one hypostasis" and treated ousia/hypostasis as synonymous
2. **No precise procession/generation distinction** — he affirmed the Spirit proceeds from the Father but did not develop the technical distinction (ekporeusis vs. gennēsis) that the Cappadocians formulated
3. **No explicit calling of the Spirit "God"** — Athanasius implied the Spirit's full divinity but did not make the explicit declaration that Gregory of Nazianzus made in 380 (Or. 31.12)
4. **No fully developed tropos hyparxeos framework** — the Cappadocian categories of "unbegotten/begotten/proceeding" as the distinguishing marks of the three persons were not Athanasius's vocabulary
5. **Pneumatology less developed than Christology** — his Letters to Serapion are significant but "essentially stop" at the affirmation level without the detailed argumentation the Cappadocians later provided (per the didymus.org lecture notes)
6. **Nicene Creed as sufficient** — Athanasius consistently argued that the Nicene Creed (325) was sufficient and needed no supplementation. The Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed (381) expanded the pneumatological article significantly beyond what Athanasius himself formulated.

**Scholarly verdict:** Athanasius is the essential BRIDGE between Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381). He preserved and defended Nicene theology when it was politically and ecclesiastically unpopular. His Tomus ad Antiochenos made the Cappadocian settlement possible. But his own Trinitarian formulation is a DEVELOPING form — a proto-Trinitarianism that contains all the essential elements but lacks the precise terminological refinements of the Cappadocian settlement.

**Sources:** Ayres (2004); Behr (2004); Tomus ad Antiochenos scholarship; didymus.org lecture notes; danieltomberlin.net on Letters to Serapion; garrettham.com scholarly article.

### Question 5: Athanasius vs. Arius on John 1:1 interpretation

**Scholarly consensus: Fundamentally opposed readings rooted in different hermeneutical approaches.**

#### John 1:1: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God"

**ARIUS (and the Eusebians):**

1. **Emphasized John 1:1b** ("the Word was WITH God" — pros ton theon): This shows DISTINCTION and SUBORDINATION. The Logos cannot be the ultimate reality because "He who is" cannot be "with" Him who is; they cannot both represent ultimate reality (Hanson, p. 835, citing Eusebius of Caesarea's pre-controversy usage).

2. **Interpreted "the beginning" (en archē) as the creation of all things**, linking it to Genesis 1 and John 1:2-3. The Word was the first thing made "in the beginning" of creation.

3. **Theos had a wide semantic range:** In antiquity, theos was not equivalent to "God" (capital G, Ultimate Reality). It described beings with varying levels of divinity. The Son could be called theos without being the Supreme God. The Dedication Creed of 341 (Antioch) explicitly cites John 1:1 and calls the Son "God" (theos) while maintaining subordinationism — showing that affirming "the Word was God" and being a subordinationist were not mutually exclusive in this period (Ayres, p. 124).

4. **Used supporting texts:** John 1:18 ("only-begotten" — implying a beginning); John 14:28 ("the Father is greater than I" — essential subordination); Colossians 1:15 ("firstborn of all creation" — the Son is the first created being).

5. **Limitation of evidence:** We have only ~5 pages of Arius's own writings (about 3 letters). We do not have explicit Arius commentary on John 1:1. The Eusebian position (Eusebius of Caesarea) is better attested. "Arius was not important" as a movement leader (per Hanson); it was Athanasius who constructed "Arianism" as a polemical category.

**ATHANASIUS:**

1. **Emphasized John 1:1c** ("the Word WAS God" — theos ēn ho logos): The predicate theos affirms the Word's FULL DIVINITY. The Word shares the same divine nature as the Father.

2. **Grammatical argument (imperfect tense):** The Greek imperfect ēn ("was") indicates CONTINUOUS EXISTENCE in past time, not a point of origin. It does not say egeneto ("became") but ēn ("was"). The Word was God eternally, not at some point in time.

3. **"In the beginning" = before creation:** The Word existed BEFORE the beginning of creation. The Word is not part of the "all things" that were made through Him (John 1:3).

4. **Partitive exegesis:** Subordinationist texts (John 14:28, etc.) are interpreted as referring to Christ's HUMANITY (what He became in the Incarnation), not His eternal divine nature. Athanasius distinguishes between passages that refer to Christ's divinity and those that refer to His humanity (Behr 2004).

5. **Homoousios as John 1:1's theological implication:** The Nicene homoousios is the creedal expression of what John 1:1 teaches — the Word shares the same ousia as the Father.

6. **Creation through the Word:** John 1:3 ("all things were made through him") shows the Word is the AGENT of creation, not a product of creation. If the Word made all things, the Word cannot be one of the things made.

**Key scholarly nuance (from Ayres, Hanson, Williams):**
- The debate was NOT simply "divine vs. not divine." Both sides called the Son theos. The issue was WHAT KIND of divinity the Son possesses — essential or derivative.
- The term theos in antiquity had a "wide variety of meanings" (Hanson). The translation "the Word was God" (capital G) ALREADY ASSUMES the Nicene conclusion.
- The "Eusebians" (the real anti-Nicene party, not just Arius) were not irrational heretics but represented a mainstream Origenist subordinationist tradition.

**Sources:** garrettham.com (citing Ayres, Williams, Pelikan, Brown, Keener); revelationbyjesuschrist.com (citing Ayres 2004, Hanson 1987); uncommonphilosophyuk.wordpress.com (citing Behr, Anatolios, Grillmeier, Stead).

## Part C: Summary Assessment for Forensic Audit

### Critical Distinction to Maintain

These three positions must NOT be conflated:

1. **Arius** (c. 250-336, active 310s-336): Pre-Nicene subordinationist. Son is a creature, however exalted. Different ousia from the Father. Monotheism preserved by making only the Father truly God.

2. **Athanasius** (c. 296-373, active 325-373): Nicene defender. Son is homoousios with the Father. One hypostasis. Spirit is divine. But terminology is PRE-CAPPADOCIAN — ousia and hypostasis are synonyms, procession not distinguished from generation.

3. **Cappadocians** (Basil c. 330-379, Gregory Naz. c. 330-390, Gregory Nyssa c. 335-394): Post-Athanasian. One ousia, three hypostases. Technical distinction of generation vs. procession. Tropos hyparxeos framework. This is the formula that became Constantinopolitan orthodoxy in 381.

### The Development Trajectory

```
Arius (subordinationism, Son as creature)
  ↓ [rejected at Nicaea 325]
Athanasius (homoousios, one hypostasis, Spirit is divine but terminology developing)
  ↓ [Tomus ad Antiochenos 362: space for multiple terminologies]
Cappadocians (one ousia, three hypostases, procession distinguished from generation)
  ↓ [formalized at Constantinople 381]
```

### Key Forensic Warning

When auditing Athanasius's theology:
- Do NOT read the Cappadocian formula ("one ousia, three hypostases") back into Athanasius
- Do NOT assume Athanasius used hypostasis to mean "person" in the modern or Cappadocian sense
- Do NOT claim Athanasius taught the "complete" Constantinopolitan Trinity — he taught a developing, proto-Trinitarian form
- DO recognize that Athanasius's theology is the ESSENTIAL BRIDGE between Nicaea and Constantinople
- DO recognize that Athanasius's Spirit theology is SIGNIFICANT but less technically precise than the Cappadocians'
