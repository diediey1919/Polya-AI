# Forensic Theological Audit: Athanasius of Alexandria (c. 296–373 CE)

## Structured Findings with Confidence Levels

---

### 1. Exact Dates and Number of His Five Exiles

**Confidence: HIGH** (based on the *Historia Acephala*, a contemporary 4th-century chronological document)

The traditional "five exiles" are well-attested:

| # | Start Date | End Date | Duration | Trigger |
|---|-----------|----------|----------|---------|
| 1 | July 11, 335 | Nov 23, 337 | ~2 years 4 months | Council of Tyre; sent to Trier by Constantine |
| 2 | April 16, 339 | Oct 21, 346 | ~7 years 6 months | Fled to Rome after Gregory of Cappadocia installed as rival bishop |
| 3 | Feb 8, 356 | Feb 21, 362 | ~6 years | Fled into Egyptian desert under Constantius II; most productive period |
| 4 | Oct 24, 362 | Feb 14, 364 | ~4 months | Exiled by Julian the Apostate |
| 5 | Oct 5, 365 | Feb 1, 366 | ~4 months | Exiled briefly under Valens |

**Total exile time**: ~17 years, 6 months, 20 days out of ~40 years as bishop (consecrated June 8, 328).

**Key source**: *Historia Acephala* (Verona manuscript, discovered 1738 by Maffei). The document itself has a lacuna for the 1st exile (which scholars reconstruct from the gap). Barnes follows this chronology.

**Scholarly consensus**: The dates and number are firmly established. Minor disputes exist about whether the departure for Tyre (335) counts as a formal "exile" since he was not yet formally deposed, but the *Historia Acephala* counts it.

### 2. His Role at Nicaea 325

**Confidence: HIGH** (scholarly consensus has shifted significantly)

- Athanasius was a **young deacon** (not yet a priest, let alone bishop) under Bishop Alexander of Alexandria.
- **Contemporary sources assign him NO specific role at the council**: Eusebius of Caesarea, Eustathius of Antioch, and Emperor Constantine's letters do not mention Athanasius in connection with the council.
- Even Athanasius's own account (written ~30 years later) barely mentions the council or the Nicene Creed in his early works.
- The *homoousios* appears only **once** in the three *Orationes contra Arianos* (c. 339–346), at i.9.
- The later tradition of Athanasius as the "champion of Nicaea" who played a leading role is a **retrojection** from his later career.

**Critical assessment**: The traditional image of Athanasius as Alexander's brilliant young secretary who dominated the council is **ecclesiastical legend, not historical fact**. He may have drafted Alexander's encyclical letter denouncing Arius (c. 319–321), but his role at the council itself was minimal and unrecorded by contemporaries.

**Key scholars**: David Gwynn (*Athanasius, Nicaea, and the "Arian Controversy"*, 2025, in *Journal of Early Christian Studies*); Lewis Ayres (*Nicaea and Its Legacy*, 2004); R.P.C. Hanson (*The Search for the Christian Doctrine of God*, 1988).

---

### 3. Dating Disputes for Major Works

**Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH** (significant scholarly disagreement exists, but Barnes's chronology is widely followed)

#### Contra Gentes / De Incarnatione (double treatise)
- **Barnes**: 326–328 (early date, pre-exile)
- **Alternative**: 328–335 (fourthcentury.com follows Barnes; some argue for c. 335)
- **Older/traditional date**: c. 318–323 — now **rejected** by most scholars
- **Key issue**: The treatise contains **no mention of Arianism by name** and **no use of homoousios**, which supports an early date before the controversy fully crystallized, OR a date where Athanasius deliberately avoided the polemical term.
- **Clavis Patrum Graecorum**: CPG 2090

#### Orationes contra Arianos (I–III)
- **Consensus range**: c. 339–346 (during or after second exile)
- **NPNF/Bright**: "best judges" assign them to the "third exile" period (356–360)
- **Barnes**: 356–360 (later date, during third exile)
- **Key issue**: Were they issued at intervals? The language of Or. ii.1 suggests they may have been composed separately.
- **Critical note**: The word *homoousios* appears only once across all three orations (i.9), and Athanasius pointedly avoids it, even using the "semi-Arian" formula "like in essence" (i.21, 26; iii.26).
- **Clavis Patrum Graecorum**: CPG 2093

#### De Decretis Nicaenae Synodi
- **Ayres**: c. 353 (most current scholarly date)
- **Traditional**: 350/351 (E. Schwartz, Opitz — now rejected as "founded on insufficient evidence")
- **Hanson**: 356–357
- **Key significance**: This is the work where Athanasius **first systematically defends homoousios**, marking a decisive shift in his rhetorical strategy.
- **Clavis Patrum Graecorum**: CPG 2120

#### Letters to Serapion (on the Holy Spirit)
- **Consensus**: c. 359–360 (during third exile)
- Written from hiding in the Egyptian desert

### 4. Development vs. Consistency of Homoousios Defense

**Confidence: HIGH** (this is one of the most important scholarly findings of the last 30 years)

**Finding: Athanasius's defense of homoousios DEVELOPED significantly over time. He did NOT consistently champion it from 325.**

Evidence:
- **325–339**: No surviving use of *homoousios* in Athanasius's writings for ~14 years after Nicaea.
- **c. 339–346 (Orationes contra Arianos)**: *homoousios* used only **once** (i.9). Ayres: "evidence of Athanasius' lack of commitment to Nicaea's terminology at this stage of his career" (*Nicaea and Its Legacy*, p. 115).
- **c. 353 (De Decretis)**: First systematic defense of the term. Hanson: "He began to use it first in the De Decretis...in 356 or 357" (p. 438).
- **Ayres**: "Athanasius' decision to make Nicaea and homoousios central to his theology has its origins in the shifting climate of the 350s and the structure of emerging Homoian theology" (p. 144).
- **Hanson**: "For nearly twenty years after Nicaea nobody mentions homoousios, not even Athanasius" (*The Search*, p. 170).

**Why the delay?**
1. *Homoousios* was associated with **Sabellianism** (modalism), making Eastern bishops suspicious.
2. The term had **Gnostic/materialist** connotations (shared substance like physical material).
3. It was politically dangerous under Constantius II, who favored anti-Nicene councils.
4. Athanasius preferred scriptural language ("true Son," "proper to the Father," "from God") in his earlier polemics.

**Critical conclusion**: The image of Athanasius as the unwavering champion of *homoousios* from 325 onward is a **later construction**. His theological development was real and strategic, responding to the emergence of Homoian theology in the 350s.

### 5. Relationship to the Cappadocian Fathers

**Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH**

- **Chronological relationship**: Athanasius (c. 296–373) was a generation older. Basil of Caesarea (329–379), Gregory of Nazianzus (325–389), and Gregory of Nyssa (335–394) were younger contemporaries.
- **Basil corresponded with Athanasius**: Basil wrote to Athanasius asking him to take action against Apollinaris and to support Nicene orthodoxy in the East. Basil asked Athanasius to sign the *Tomus ad Antiochenos* (362).
- **Tomus ad Antiochenos (362)**: Athanasius showed remarkable **flexibility** on terminology — he accepted both "three hypostases" and "one hypostasis" formulations, focusing on the underlying reality rather than terminological precision. This contrasts with the Cappadocians' later insistence on "one ousia, three hypostases."
- **Theological differences**:
  - Athanasius used *hypostasis* and *ousia* somewhat interchangeably (pre-Cappadocian usage).
  - The Cappadocians introduced the precise distinction: *ousia* = common essence; *hypostasis* = particular person.
  - Athanasius's pneumatology (in the *Letters to Serapion*) lacks the Cappadocian precision on the Spirit's mode of origin.
- **Gwynn and Ayres**: Athanasius was the **forerunner** who established the pro-Nicene trajectory; the Cappadocians provided the philosophical precision that made it intellectually defensible.

**Key caveat**: Do NOT retroject Cappadocian precision into Athanasius. His terminology is looser, his arguments more soteriological and less metaphysically technical.

### 6. Pneumatology in Letters to Serapion

**Confidence: HIGH** (well-documented from the primary texts)

**Context**: Written c. 359–360 against the "Tropici" (whom Athanasius mockingly calls "Metaphoricals") — Egyptian monks/ascetics who denied the Holy Spirit's divinity, claiming the Spirit was a created being, a kind of angel.

**Key arguments**:
1. **Indivisibility of the Trinity**: The Trinity is "indivisible and self-consistent." You cannot separate the Spirit from the Father and Son. "There is one sanctification, which is derived from the Father, through the Son, in the Holy Spirit" (I.20).
2. **Soteriological argument**: The Spirit deifies us. If the Spirit is a creature, we are not truly deified. "The Spirit unites us to God and makes us sharers in the divine nature" (citing 2 Pet 1:4).
3. **Consubstantiality**: The Spirit is consubstantial with the Son, as the Son is consubstantial with the Father. The Spirit is "the image of the Word and proper to the Father" in substance.
4. **Analogies**: Uses incorporeal analogies: light/radiance/enlightening; fountain/river/drink; wise/wisdom/spirit of wisdom.
5. **Baptismal argument**: The baptismal formula (Mt 28:19) names Father, Son, and Spirit together; to deny the Spirit's divinity is to lose the baptismal life.

**Important limitations**:
- Athanasius makes **no systematic distinction between eternal procession and temporal mission**.
- He appears to think in terms of a **double origin** for the Spirit (from the Father and from the Son), without later filioque precision.
- His pneumatology is **less developed** than his Christology — the issue was newer, and he was responding to a crisis rather than building a systematic theology.
- The Cappadocians (especially Basil's *De Spiritu Sancto*, 375) developed this further.

### 7. Fourth Oration Authorship Dispute

**Confidence: HIGH** (scholarly consensus that it is NOT by Athanasius)

**Evidence against Athanasian authorship**:
1. **Not quoted by any early Father** as part of Athanasius's anti-Arian works (unlike Orations I–III, which are quoted by Theodoret, Justinian, Cyril, Facundus, etc.).
2. **Severus of Antioch** (465–538) implies only three discourses.
3. **Terminological differences**: Uses *hypostasis* in Trinitarian contexts (unknown in Orations I–III); uses *homoousios* twice (vs. once in all three authentic orations combined); never uses "like the Father" (frequent in I–III).
4. **Primary target is NOT Arianism**: The main burden is against **Marcellus of Ancyra** and his Sabellian/modalist tendencies, with a secondary attack on followers of Paul of Samosata (Photinus).
5. **Arianism attacked is early/formative**, not the Neo-Arianism of Aetius/Eunomius — suggesting pre-360 composition.

**Authorship proposals**:
- **Apollinaris of Laodicea**: Proposed by Stegmann (1917). Some support but not proven.
- **Markus Vinzent** (1996 monograph): Published the most recent study but **did not identify the author**.
- **Current consensus**: Authorship remains **unknown**. The work is universally classified as pseudo-Athanasian.

**Implication for the audit**: Do NOT cite the Fourth Oration as evidence for Athanasius's own theology. It reflects a different author's concerns (primarily anti-Marcellan polemic).

### 8. Key Arguments in De Decretis About Non-Scriptural Terminology

**Confidence: HIGH** (primary text available; Ayres 2004 provides definitive analysis)

**Context**: Written c. 353 to a friend who was challenged by Arians objecting to non-scriptural terms in the Nicene Creed.

**Structure and arguments**:

1. **§§1–5**: Stigmatizes Arian evasions and inconsistency; describes their conduct at the council.

2. **§§6–17**: Investigates the meaning of divine Sonship through scriptural titles (Son, Word, Wisdom, Power).

3. **§§18–20** (core argument): Non-scriptural terms were **forced upon the council by Arian evasions**. The Arians kept finding loopholes in scriptural language, so the bishops had to use precise non-scriptural terms to close those loopholes.

4. **§§21–24**: The terms express **no sense not found in Scripture**. They are clarifications of scriptural meaning, not additions to it.

5. **§§25–27**: The terms have **precedent in earlier authoritative writers**: extracts from Theognostus, the two Dionysii (of Alexandria and Rome), and Origen.

6. **§§28–32**: Addresses the Arian use of *agenetos* (uncreated/unbegotten) applied to the Father alone, implying the Son is *genetos* (created). Athanasius shows the equivocation between *agen(n)etos* (unbegotten) and *agenetos* (uncreated).

**Ayres's key insight (2004)**: Athanasius's fundamental strategy is to defend *homoousios* as a **necessary corollary** of Nicaea's other controversial phrase, *ek tes ousias tou patros* ("from the essence of the Father"). For Athanasius, this phrase stands in the creed only to preserve the scriptural insistence that the Son is the "true" Son and Word of the Father, truly "proper to" and "from God." So non-scriptural terminology serves to protect scriptural meaning.

### 9. Soteriological Argument: "What Is Not Assumed Is Not Healed"

**Confidence: HIGH — this is NOT from Athanasius**

**Exact source**: **Gregory of Nazianzus**, *Epistle 101* (Letter to Cledonius), written c. 382 CE against Apollinarianism.

**Exact quotation**: "τὸ γὰρ ἀπρόσληπτον ἀθεράπευτον· ἕνωται δὲ τῷ θεῷ, τοῦτο καὶ σῴζεται" — "For that which he has not assumed he has not healed; but that which is united to his Godhead is also saved."

**Prior attestation**: The principle is **first recorded by Origen** in *Dialogue with Heraclides* (c. 240s), but Gregory made it famous.

**Athanasius's version of the soteriological argument**:
- Athanasius makes the same fundamental argument but in **different language**. His key formulation is in *De Incarnatione* §54: "He was made man that we might be made God" (αὐτὸς γὰρ ἐνηνθρώπησεν, ἵνα ἡμεῖς θεοποιηθῶμεν).
- Throughout his works, Athanasius argues: if Christ is not fully divine, he cannot deify us; if Christ does not assume full humanity, humanity is not fully saved.
- In *Orationes contra Arianos* ii.69–70: the soteriological argument is central — reality of redemption and grace depends on Christ being truly God and truly man.

**Critical distinction for the audit**: The exact phrase "what is not assumed is not healed" is **Gregory's, not Athanasius's**. Athanasius argues the same logic but uses the language of **deification (theosis/theopoiesis)** rather than "assumption" and "healing." Retrojecting Gregory's formulation onto Athanasius is anachronistic.

### 10. Interpretation of Proverbs 8:22

**Confidence: HIGH** (extensive primary text available in *Orationes contra Arianos* ii.44–86)

**The problem**: Proverbs 8:22 LXX reads "κύριος ἔκτισέν με ἀρχὴν ὁδῶν αὐτοῦ" — "The Lord created me a beginning of His ways for His works." The Arians used this as their **primary proof-text**: if Wisdom (= Christ) says "the Lord created me," then the Son is a creature.

**Athanasius's interpretation** (*Or. c. Ar.* ii.44–86):

1. **The Word is NOT a creature by nature**: "Let the Word then be excepted from the works, and as Creator be restored to the Father, and be confessed to be Son by nature."

2. **"Created" refers to the Incarnation**: Athanasius argues that "He created me" refers to the **preparation of Christ's human body**, not to the eternal generation of the Word. "We must not conceive that the whole Word is in nature a creature, but that He put on the created body and that God created Him for our sakes, preparing for Him the created body."

3. **Key hermeneutical principle**: When Scripture says things about Christ that imply limitation or creaturely status, these refer to his **humanity** (the economic/ incarnate condition), not to his **eternal divine nature**.

4. **"Beginning of His ways"**: Athanasius interprets this as Christ becoming the **beginning/firstfruits of the new creation** through his incarnate work, not as evidence of his own creation.

5. **Contrast with Prov 8:25**: "Before the mountains were settled... He begets me" — Athanasius uses this to show that the Word was **eternally begotten** (not created) before all things.

**Scholarly assessment**: This was a **defensive hermeneutical move** that departed from most pre-Nicene interpreters (who took the verse at face value as referring to the pre-existent Son's generation). Athanasius's two-natures reading became standard in later orthodoxy but was innovative in its context. It reflects the pressure of the Arian controversy forcing new interpretive strategies.

## Summary for Forensic Audit

### Key Findings Against Retrojection

1. **Athanasius did NOT consistently champion homoousios from 325.** His defense developed strategically in the 350s. This is perhaps the single most important finding for the audit.

2. **His role at Nicaea was minimal.** The later image of him as the council's champion is hagiographical retrojection.

3. **"What is not assumed is not healed" is Gregory of Nazianzus's phrase, not Athanasius's.** Athanasius makes the same soteriological argument using the language of deification (*theopoiesis*).

4. **The Fourth Oration is NOT by Athanasius** and should not be cited as evidence for his theology.

5. **Do not retroject Cappadocian precision** (one *ousia*, three *hypostaseis*) into Athanasius. His *Tomus ad Antiochenos* (362) accepts terminological flexibility that the Cappadocians later tightened.

6. **His pneumatology is less developed** than his Christology — no systematic distinction between procession and mission; double-origin language without later filioque precision.

7. **His Proverbs 8:22 interpretation is innovative** — a defensive two-natures reading that departed from most pre-Nicene exegesis.
