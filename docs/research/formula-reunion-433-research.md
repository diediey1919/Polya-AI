## 1. EVENTS BETWEEN CYRIL'S DEPOSITION (JUNE 431) AND FORMULA OF REUNION (APRIL 433)

### Chronological Reconstruction

**June 22, 431** — Cyril's council (without Eastern bishops or papal legates) convened at Ephesus, examined Nestorius's teachings, and deposed him. ~197 bishops subscribed immediately. The session also examined Cyril's Second Letter to Nestorius (Ep. 4) and declared it in agreement with Nicaea. The Twelve Anathemas (appended to the Third Letter, Ep. 17) were NOT formally canonized — a critical omission that later enabled reunion negotiations. (ACO I.1.2; McGuckin 1994, pp. 62-65; Price & Graumann 2020)

**June 26, 431** — John of Antioch arrived with ~30-40 Eastern bishops. Refusing to join Cyril's assembly, they held a rival council (counter-synod) with imperial commissioner Candidian. This counter-council issued a *psephos* (decree) deposing Cyril and Memnon of Ephesus and excommunicating all participants of the June 22 session who refused to abjure Cyril's Twelve Anathemas (the "heretic capitula"). The Easterners invoked the Nicene Creed as sole standard. (ACO I.1.1, pp. 92-93; I.1.3; Schwartz)

**June 29, 431** — Emperor Theodosius II issued a rescript annulling Cyril's June 22 decisions, citing procedural irregularities (opening before all bishops arrived). (ACO I.1.1.33)

**July 10-11, 431** — Papal legates (Bishops Arcadius and Projectus, Priest Philip) arrived. They joined Cyril's council and expressly confirmed the condemnation of Nestorius using the authority of the Apostolic See (Pope Celestine I). (ACO I.2)

**Late July 431** — Cyril's reconvened council deposed John of Antioch and 34 Eastern bishops. Theodosius, receiving delegations from both sides, rejected both councils' doctrinal decrees and declared the Nicene formula sufficient. He accepted the depositions of Cyril, Memnon, AND John — effectively nullifying everything. (ACO I.1.4; McGuckin 1994, pp. 80-85)

**August-September 431** — Cyril returned to Alexandria via bribery and political maneuvering. Nestorius, at his own request, retired to his former monastery of Euprepius near Antioch.

**October 25, 431** — Maximianus ordained patriarch of Constantinople, replacing Nestorius. This was a key Cyril victory — the see was filled by a Cyril supporter. (ACO I.1.7)

**July 31, 432** — Pope Celestine died; Sixtus III succeeded him. Sixtus would play a role in brokering reunion.

**Late 432 – Early 433** — Intensive negotiations began. Key mediators:
- **Acacius of Beroea** — the aged, venerated Syrian bishop (the only living bishop who had attended Constantinople I in 381). Both sides trusted him.
- **Imperial tribune Aristolaus** — sent by Theodosius II to shuttle between Antioch and Alexandria
- **Paul of Emesa** — John of Antioch's bishop, sent as envoy to Alexandria

(Wessel 2004, pp. 245-270; McGuckin 1994, pp. 92-105)

### The Negotiation Process

The imperial commissioner Aristolaus, backed by the menacing presence of the *magistrianos* Maximus, made the alternatives clear to Cyril: doctrinal agreement between Alexandria and Antioch, OR the setting aside of the Council of Ephesus with possible restoration of Nestorius and certain banishment of Cyril. (blog post citing McGuckin; Wessel 2004)

Acacius of Beroea played the pivotal intermediary role. Both Theodosius II and Pope Sixtus III (ordained July 31, 432) encouraged him. John and Cyril exchanged letters whose conciliatory tone revealed genuine apostolic spirit.

**Paul of Emesa** arrived in Alexandria with an orthodox profession of faith. Cyril accepted it and allowed Paul to preach at Alexandria. After further negotiations on minor points, the accord was finalized. (Catholic Encyclopedia, "John of Antioch")

**April 12 (or 23), 433** — The Formula of Reunion was completed. Cyril's Letter 39 (*Laetentur Caeli* / "Let the Heavens Rejoice") to John of Antioch formally accepted the Antiochene confession. (ACO I.1.4, pp. 17-20; Cyril Ep. 39 = PG 77:161-180)

## 2. THE ROLE OF IMPERIAL MEDIATION

### Theodosius II's Evolving Position

1. **Initial stance (431)**: Theodosius convened the council (edict Nov 19, 430, for Pentecost June 7, 431) to adjudicate the Nestorius dispute. He appointed the imperial commissioner **Candidian** (comes domesticorum) to oversee proceedings and ensure all parties were present before deliberations began.

2. **Post-Ephesus crisis (summer 431)**: Faced with two rival councils each deposing the other, Theodosius adopted a position of studied neutrality. He annulled both councils' decisions and declared the Nicene Creed sufficient. He sent bishops home without endorsing either assembly. However, he permitted Nestorius to retire to his monastery (a tacit acceptance of Nestorius's removal, though not formal deposition).

3. **Pro-Cyril shift (late 431-432)**: Several factors pushed Theodosius toward Cyril:
   - The papal legates' confirmation carried great weight
   - Massive Alexandrian gifts flooded the imperial court (Cyril's legendary bribery operation — gold, textiles, ostrich eggs — documented in surviving letters to court officials; Schwartz ACO I.4 documents Cyril's payments)
   - Popular support for Cyril in Constantinople
   - The ordination of Maximianus as Nestorius's successor (Oct 25, 431) made Nestorius's restoration practically impossible
   - 17 bishops who supported Nestorius were removed from their sees in the following months

4. **Active mediation (432-433)**: Theodosius dispatched **tribune Aristolaus** to shuttle between Antioch and Alexandria. The imperial message was blunt: reach agreement or face the consequences. The alternative to reunion was the invalidation of Ephesus entirely — which meant Cyril's own condemnation would stand. (Wessel 2004; McGuckin 1994, pp. 95-100)

5. **Post-Reunion enforcement**: After April 433, Theodosius enforced the reunion. John of Antioch was obliged to abandon Nestorius (March 433 is sometimes cited as the date of John's formal break). Nestorius's isolation was now total.

### Key Imperial Officials
- **Candidian** — comes domesticorum, imperial commissioner at Ephesus 431 (sided with Eastern bishops)
- **Aristolaus** — tribune/notarius, principal imperial mediator 432-433
- **Maximus** — magistrianos, accompanied Aristolaus as enforcer
- **Pulcheria** — Theodosius's sister, influential at court, generally pro-Cyril

## 3. THEOLOGICAL CONTENT OF THE FORMULA OF REUNION

### Authorship: An Antiochene Draft Cyril Accepted

**The Formula was drafted by the Antiochene party**, almost certainly with significant input from **Theodoret of Cyrrhus**, the most sophisticated Antiochene theologian. The text originated as a confession of faith approved by John of Antioch's counter-council at Ephesus in August 431, subsequently revised during negotiations. (3rdmillennium.blogspot.com, citing McGuckin; Cambridge Core on Ibas's Letter to Mari)

Cyril received it via Paul of Emesa, examined it, and accepted it with enthusiasm in his Letter 39 (*Laetentur*). Cyril added his own interpretive gloss but did not alter the Antiochene text itself.

### Text of the Formula (from Cyril's Letter 39, *Laetentur*)

Key clauses (translation based on Wickham and McEnerney):

> "We confess, therefore, our Lord Jesus Christ, the only-begotten Son of God, **perfect God and perfect man**, composed of a rational soul and body, begotten before the ages from the Father according to divinity, and the same in the last days, for us and for our salvation, born of the Virgin Mary according to the humanity, the same **consubstantial with the Father in terms of divinity and consubstantial with us according to humanity**."

> "**For there was a union of two natures** (ἑνώσεως γὰρ γεγονυίας ἐκ δύο φύσεων), and this is why we confess **one Christ, one Son, one Lord**."

> "According to this understanding of the **unconfused union** (ἀσυγχύτου ἑνώσεως), we confess the holy Virgin to be **Mother of God** (Θεοτόκον), because God the Word was incarnate and became man, and from the very conception he united the temple taken from her with himself."

> "Concerning the sayings about the Lord in the Gospels and Epistles, we recognize that theologians have treated some as referring to the divinity and others as referring to the humanity, applying them to **one and the same person** (πρόσωπον ἕν)."

> "We anathematize those who divide the one Lord Jesus Christ into **two Sons**."

(ACO I.1.4, pp. 17-20; Cyril Ep. 39; DS 272)

### Cyril's Interpretive Additions

In accepting the Formula, Cyril added crucial clarifications:
- No mixture, confusion, or blending of the Word with the flesh
- The divine nature of the Word is unchangeable and unalterable
- The Word suffered "in the flesh" (not in his own divine nature)
- He follows "the opinions of the holy Fathers in all things, especially our blessed and all-renowned Athanasius"
- Not one word of the Nicene Creed may be changed

### The Theological Innovation

The Formula introduced language Cyril had previously resisted:
- **"Two natures" (δύο φύσεις)** — explicitly used in the union clause
- **"Perfect God and perfect man"** — dual consubstantiality
- **Distinction of sayings** — attributing some Gospel sayings to divinity, others to humanity (the Antiochene "prosopic" approach)

This was a significant concession by Cyril, who had previously championed "one incarnate nature of God the Word" (μία φύσις τοῦ θεοῦ λόγου σεσαρκωμένη). However, Cyril interpreted the "two natures" language through his own hypostatic union framework — the natures are distinguished "in thought alone" (τῇ θεωρίᾳ μόνῃ) after the union. (McGuckin 1994; Wessel 2004, pp. 250-265)

## 4. CONCESSIONS BY EACH SIDE

### Antiochene Concessions (John of Antioch and the Easterners)

1. **Accepted the deposition of Nestorius** — John explicitly declared Nestorius a heretic, rightly deposed, and no longer bishop of Constantinople
2. **Accepted Theotokos** — the title for Mary was affirmed without qualification
3. **Accepted "union of two natures"** — language that, while preserving Antiochene duality, affirmed genuine union (not mere conjunction/συνάφεια)
4. **Anathematized "two Sons"** — rejecting the Nestorian implication of division
5. **Recognized Cyril's council as the valid Council of Ephesus** — retrospectively accepting its core decision on Nestorius
6. **Abandoned their counter-council's decrees** — the psephos deposing Cyril and Memnon was set aside

### Cyril's Concessions

1. **Accepted "two natures" language** — the Formula explicitly uses ἐκ δύο φύσεων ("from two natures"), which Cyril had previously opposed
2. **Accepted the distinction of sayings** — attributing some Gospel statements to Christ's humanity, others to his divinity (previously a Nestorian technique Cyril had anathematized in his 4th Anathema)
3. **Dropped insistence on "one incarnate nature"** — Cyril "refrained thereafter from referring to the contested formula of the unique nature" (Encyclopedia.com)
4. **Made no further mention of the Twelve Anathemas** — he never formally retracted them but effectively sidelined them
5. **Provided explanations of his Twelve Chapters** — Cyril agreed to furnish explanations making clear his disavowal of Apollinarianism (3rdmillennium.blogspot.com)
6. **Accepted the Formula without requiring subscription to his Third Letter** — the most aggressive anti-Nestorian document was left out of the reunion basis
7. **Allowed Antiochene theological vocabulary** — the Formula uses πρόσωπον (prosopon), which was the preferred Antiochene term, alongside Cyril's preferred language

### Assessment

Scholars debate whether Cyril made a "genuine concession" (Wessel 2004) or merely accepted different terminology for the same underlying theology (McGuckin 1994). Cyril himself wrote to his agent Eulogius in Constantinople defending the Formula against critics who said "two natures" was Nestorian:

> "The doctrinal statement which the Easterns have produced is under attack in certain quarters and it is being asked why the bishop of Alexandria tolerated, even applauded it, seeing that they use the words 'two natures.' The Nestorians are saying that he shares their view..." (Cyril, *Ad Eulogium*; Wickham, p. 63)

Cyril's defense: the Antiochenes use "two natures" correctly (distinguished but united), not in the Nestorian sense of divided persons. The "precise facts" were that the Formula was the best compromise Cyril could secure given the political realities. (McGuckin 1994, pp. 105-110)

## 5. THE TWELVE ANATHEMAS: ACCEPTED OR SET ASIDE?

### Status at Ephesus 431

The Twelve Anathemas were appended to Cyril's **Third Letter to Nestorius** (Ep. 17, written late 430). At the Council of Ephesus on June 22, 431:

- Cyril's **Second Letter** (Ep. 4) was formally read and declared orthodox — in agreement with Nicaea
- The **Third Letter with the Twelve Anathemas was NOT formally read or approved** at the June 22 session, according to the argument of Thomas Graumann (Price & Graumann 2020, pp. 218-220): its inclusion in the acts "reads more like an editorial insertion … than a record of the procedure actually followed"
- However, the Anathemas were associated with the council's spirit, and some later collections included them

### At the Formula of Reunion (433)

**The Twelve Anathemas were deliberately set aside — neither accepted nor condemned.**

Key evidence:
1. **John of Antioch was NOT asked to make a statement about the Twelve Anathemas** — "John was not asked to make a similar statement about Cyril's twelve anathemas" (EWTN, citing Hughes; Catholic Encyclopedia)
2. **The Formula contains language that directly contradicts Anathema 4** — The 4th Anathema states: "If anyone divides between two persons or hypostases the expressions in the Gospel and apostolic writings… and applies some to the man… and others to God the Word… let him be anathema." The Formula's clause about "theologians treating some sayings as referring to divinity and others to humanity" flatly contradicts this. (JTS article noting the Formula's final sentence "flatly contradicting his fourth anathema")
3. **Cyril accepted the Formula without protest on this point** — he must have accepted this language (McGuckin notes this)
4. **Cyril never again invoked the Anathemas** — "made no further mention of the anathemas" (Encyclopedia.com)
5. **The Anathemas were never formally approved at Ephesus 431** — this is the scholarly consensus contra some later claims. The Council approved the Second Letter and the deposition of Nestorius but did not canonize the Anathemas. (Encyclopedia.com, "Anathemas of Cyril")

### Later Reception

- **Ephesus II (449)**: Dioscorus's "Robber Council" accepted the Anathemas as canonical
- **Chalcedon (451)**: The Anathemas were **passed over** — neither read nor endorsed (see Section 7 below)
- **Constantinople II (553)**: The Fifth Ecumenical Council reaffirmed Cyril's Third Letter and the Twelve Anathemas, anathematizing those who opposed them

### Scholarly Assessment

The Anathemas were the casualty of reunion. Their aggressive miaphysite language ("one incarnate nature") and their anathematization of Antiochene theological methods made them incompatible with the compromise Formula. Cyril pragmatically allowed them to fade from the diplomatic settlement while never formally retracting them. (McGuckin 1994; Grillmeier, *Christ in Christian Tradition* I, pp. 474-480)

## 6. NESTORIUS'S FATE POST-431

### Chronology of Exile

| Date | Event |
|------|-------|
| **June 22, 431** | Condemned and deposed at Cyril's council |
| **August/September 431** | Retired at his own request to monastery of Euprepius near Antioch |
| **Oct 25, 431** | Maximianus ordained as his replacement at Constantinople |
| **April 433** | Formula of Reunion confirmed his deposition; John of Antioch abandoned him (March 433) |
| **431-435** | Lived at monastery of Euprepius near Antioch — "languishing in exile for four years" (Britannica). Continued writing and defending his orthodoxy |
| **435** | **August 3, 435** — Theodosius II issued imperial edict exiling Nestorius. He was sent to a monastery in the **Great Oasis of Hibis (al-Khargah)** in the Libyan Desert, "securely within the diocese of Cyril" (Britannica; Wikipedia). All his writings were ordered burned |
| **c. 436** | Transferred from the Great Oasis (some sources say the transfer happened c. 436 rather than 435) |
| **Later** | Moved to **Panopolis** (Akhmim) in Upper Egypt (Thebaid) |
| **During exile** | The monastery suffered attacks by desert bandits/Nubians. "Nestorius was injured in one such raid" — his hand and one rib were broken. He surrendered to the governor to avoid being accused of fleeing (Catholic Encyclopedia; Britannica) |
| **c. 450-451** | Died in exile at Panopolis, Egypt. "He lived on until about 451, strenuously defending his views" (Wikipedia). Some sources date his death to c. 450 |

### Final Years and Writings

- Nestorius composed his major apologetic work, the **Liber Heraclidis** (*Book of Heracleides*, also known as the *Bazaar of Heracleides*) during exile, probably c. 450-451. This survives in a Syriac translation (the Greek original is lost). It was discovered in 1895 in a Syriac manuscript. (This is the text covered by SRC-THEO-008 in the KB)
- In it, he claimed vindication by the "tome of Leo" at Chalcedon — arguing that Chalcedon's Definition effectively adopted his Christology
- He never accepted the justice of his condemnation
- His last major defender within the Roman Empire, **Theodoret of Cyrrhus**, finally agreed to anathematize him at Chalcedon in 451

### The Question of Nestorius's Orthodoxy

Modern scholarship (especially since the recovery of the *Liber Heraclidis*) has debated whether Nestorius actually held the "Nestorian" heresy attributed to him:
- He always denied dividing Christ into two Sons
- He accepted Theotokos (conditionally) late in the controversy
- His Sermon XVIII (preserved) contains: "through this union that the holy Virgin is called Theotokos"
- The *Liber Heraclidis* shows a more nuanced position than his opponents attributed to him

## 7. RECEPTION OF EPHESUS 431 AT CHALCEDON 451

### How Ephesus Was Cited

Chalcedon claimed fidelity to Ephesus 431. The Chalcedonian Definition (October 451) states:

> "Following, then, the holy Fathers, we all with one voice teach… this great and holy ecumenical council condemns those who pretend that there were two Sons… it deposes those who dare to say that the Godhead of the Only-begotten is passible; it stands against those who imagine a mixing or confusion of the two natures of Christ; it drives away those who erroneously think that the heavenly form of the servant is of a nature different from ours… **it anathematizes those who fabricate two natures of the Lord before the union**…"

The Definition invokes "the holy Fathers" and specifically references:
1. The Nicene Creed (325)
2. The Constantinopolitan Creed (381)
3. Cyril's **Second Letter** to Nestorius (for refuting Nestorius's errors)
4. Cyril's **Letter to John of Antioch** (the Formula of Reunion / Letter 39 / *Laetentur*)
5. Pope Leo's **Tome** to Flavian

### Documents FROM Ephesus 431 Read at Chalcedon

At the **second session** (October 10, 451), the following were read:
1. The **Nicene Creed** (confirmed at Ephesus)
2. The **Constantinopolitan Creed** (381)
3. Cyril's **Second Letter to Nestorius** (Ep. 4) — read in full and acclaimed
4. Cyril's **Letter to John of Antioch** (Ep. 39, the *Laetentur* containing the Formula of Reunion) — read and acclaimed

### The Third Letter and Twelve Anathemas: DELIBERATELY OMITTED

**Cyril's Third Letter to Nestorius (Ep. 17, containing the Twelve Anathemas) was NOT read at Chalcedon.**

This is one of the most significant and debated aspects of Chalcedon's reception of Ephesus:

1. **Evidence of omission**: The Acts of Chalcedon record the reading of the Second Letter and the Formula of Reunion but NOT the Third Letter. When some Egyptian bishops attempted to introduce it, they were prevented. (Price, *The Acts of the Council of Chalcedon*, 2005; ACO II.1)

2. **Graumann's argument** (Price & Graumann 2020, pp. 218-220): The Third Letter was likely NOT read at Cyril's own council in 431 either. Its inclusion in the Ephesine acts "reads more like an editorial insertion … than a record of the procedure actually followed." This means Chalcedon's omission was actually consistent with Cyril's own conciliar practice — though this is a minority scholarly view.

3. **Non-Chalcedonian (Miaphysite) interpretation**: The omission was deliberate and political. "Cyril's most decisive works — the Third Letter to Nestorius and the Twelve Anathemas — were set aside. These were precisely the writings that Ephesus I had upheld as definitive." The purpose was to construct a dyophysite framework using the compromise Formula of Reunion (drafted by Theodoret) rather than Cyril's strongest miaphysite statements. (myagpeya.com, citing Pierce)

4. **Chalcedonian interpretation**: The Second Letter and Formula of Reunion together represented Cyril's mature, consensus theology. The Third Letter's Anathemas had been deliberately excluded from the Formula of Reunion by Cyril himself in 433 — Chalcedon was following Cyril's own precedent.

5. **What was read instead**: The Formula of Reunion (433), which was drafted by Theodoret of Cyrrhus and used "two natures" language. Chalcedon then extended this into its own Definition with "in two natures" (ἐν δύο φύσεσιν) — language that went beyond even the Formula of Reunion. (myagpeya.com analysis)

### The Theodoret and Ibas Factor

Chalcedon **readmitted Theodoret of Cyrrhus and Ibas of Edessa** to the episcopacy after they accepted Ephesus 431 and the condemnation of Nestorius. However:
- Both had previously written extensively against Cyril's Twelve Anathemas
- Theodoret had drafted the Formula of Reunion
- Ibas's *Letter to Mari the Persian* (mid-430s) gave a narrative of Ephesus 431 and the 433 reconciliation that was critical of Cyril
- Neither was required to retract their anti-Cyrilline writings
- This became the basis of the **Three Chapters controversy** a century later

### The Chalcedonian Definition's Christological Formula

> "We confess one and the same Son, our Lord Jesus Christ… acknowledged **in two natures** (ἐν δύο φύσεσιν), **without confusion, without change, without division, without separation** (ἀσυγχύτως, ἀτρέπτως, ἀδιαιρέτως, ἀχωρίστως) — the difference of the natures being in no way destroyed by the union, but rather the distinctive character of each nature being preserved and coming together in one person (πρόσωπον) and one hypostasis."

This fourfold negative formula (the "four adverbs") derives from Cyril's own language in the Formula of Reunion ("unconfused union") but extends it into an explicitly dyophysite framework that Cyril himself never used. (Grillmeier I, pp. 541-550; Price 2005)

## CRITICAL DISTINCTIONS FOR APOLOGETICS

1. **Ephesus 431 ≠ Formula of Reunion 433**: The council deposed Nestorius but did not resolve the Christological dispute. The Formula of 433 was the actual theological settlement, and it was a compromise — not a Cyrillian victory.

2. **The Twelve Anathemas were never the basis of reunion**: They were set aside in 433 and omitted at Chalcedon in 451. Their canonical status was only restored at Constantinople II (553).

3. **"Two natures" language was accepted by Cyril in 433**: This is the strongest counter to the claim that Cyril was a pure miaphysite who never accepted dyophysite language. He accepted it — albeit interpreted through his own hypostatic union framework.

4. **The Formula of Reunion was drafted by Antiochenes (likely Theodoret)**: Cyril accepted an Antiochene text, not the other way around. This complicates the narrative of purely Cyrillian orthodoxy.

5. **Chalcedon 451 went beyond the Formula of Reunion**: The "in two natures" (ἐν δύο φύσεσιν) formula at Chalcedon was stronger dyophysite language than even the 433 Formula used ("from two natures" / ἐκ δύο φύσεων). This is the key Miaphysite objection.
